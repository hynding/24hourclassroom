## The ideas that generate every question

1. **Statistics exists because of variability (1.1).** An investigation runs question → collect → analyse → interpret. A statistical investigative question is about a group, names the population and variable(s), says whether it wants a summary, a comparison or an association, and expects variability. A survey question is what you ask one person to collect the data.
2. **Variable type decides everything after it (1.2).** Categorical variables place individuals in groups (even when written with digits, like ZIP codes); quantitative variables are measured or counted amounts, discrete or continuous.
3. **Categorical data are summarised by counts and proportions (1.3, 1.4).** Frequency and relative frequency tables; bar charts and pie charts. Describe with the most and least common categories and their percents. Watch for truncated axes and pictures scaled in two dimensions.
4. **Quantitative data are shown by dotplots, stemplots, histograms and boxplots (1.5, 1.8).** Each shows the distribution; boxplots and histograms hide individual values, and boxplots also hide gaps, clusters and sample size.
5. **A description has four parts, in context (1.6).** Shape (symmetric, skewed right or left, uniform; unimodal or bimodal), center, variability, and unusual features (outliers, gaps, clusters).
6. **Summary statistics match the shape (1.7).** The median and IQR are resistant; the mean, standard deviation and range are not. Skewed or with outliers: median and IQR. Roughly symmetric: mean and SD.
7. **Comparisons need comparative language (1.9).** All four features, for every group, with "greater than", "less than", "about the same as", "more variable than"; same statistics for every group.
8. **The question decides the data collection (1.10).** Census, sample survey, observational study or experiment. Observational studies show association; only experiments with random assignment show cause.
9. **Random sampling makes a sample represent a population (1.11).** SRS, stratified, cluster, systematic. Larger samples reduce sampling variability.
10. **Bias pushes results in one direction (1.12).** Convenience and voluntary response samples, undercoverage, nonresponse, response bias. A larger sample does not fix bias.
11. **Experiments establish cause (1.13).** Comparison, random assignment, control, replication; control groups, placebos, blinding; completely randomized, randomized block and matched pairs designs.

## Key facts and procedures

| Want | Do this |
|---|---|
| Relative frequency | count ÷ total; the relative frequencies of all categories add to 1 |
| Median of n values | order them; the ((n + 1)/2)th value (average the middle two when n is even) |
| Quartiles | medians of the lower and upper halves (middle value excluded when n is odd) |
| IQR | Q3 − Q1 |
| Outlier fences | below Q1 − 1.5 × IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 × IQR |
| Mean | x̄ = Σx / n |
| Standard deviation | s = √( Σ(x − x̄)² / (n − 1) ): the typical distance of values from the mean |
| Shape clue from statistics | mean > median suggests right skew; mean < median suggests left skew |
| Center and spread to report | skewed or outliers: median, IQR; symmetric: mean, SD |
| Boxplot | five-number summary; whiskers to the most extreme non-outliers; outliers as points |
| Different group sizes | compare relative frequencies, not counts |
| SRS | label 1 to N; random number generator, ignore repeats, until n labels; those individuals |
| Stratified | strata similar inside (on a variable related to the response); SRS from every stratum |
| Cluster | clusters varied inside (often by location); SRS of clusters; everyone in them |
| Systematic | k = N/n; random start among the first k; every kth after it |
| Random assignment | label units; random mechanism without repeats picks each treatment group |
| Treatments with two factors | (levels of factor 1) × (levels of factor 2) |
| Randomized block | blocks of similar units; random assignment separately in each block |
| Matched pairs | both treatments to each subject in random order, or pairs split by coin flip |

## Sentences that earn AP points

- **Describing a distribution:** "The distribution of weekly gaming hours is skewed to the right with a median of 5 hours and an IQR of 6 hours. 25 hours is a high outlier."
- **Comparing:** "The median commute at South (28 min) is about 10 minutes greater than at North (18 min). The IQRs are the same (10 min). North's commutes are skewed right with a high outlier at 47 min; South's are roughly symmetric."
- **Interpreting the SD:** "The battery lifetimes of Model R typically vary by about 1.4 hours from the mean of 16.7 hours."
- **Choosing statistics:** "Because the distribution is strongly skewed, the median and IQR are better summaries: they are resistant to the long tail."
- **Bias:** "Students eating in the cafeteria are more likely to buy school lunch, so they probably spend more than typical students; the estimate is likely too high."
- **Confounding:** "Class period is confounded with teaching method, because the morning and afternoon classes may differ in alertness, so a difference in scores cannot be attributed to the method."
- **Scope:** "Because treatments were randomly assigned, we can conclude that the nap caused faster reaction times. Because the subjects were volunteers, the conclusion applies only to students like them."

## Classic question types and how to attack them

**"Describe the distribution."** Shape, center, variability, unusual features; numbers with units; the variable's name.

**"Compare the distributions."** The same four features, each in one comparative sentence, sized ("about 10 minutes greater"), with the same statistics for every group.

**"Is there an outlier?"** Show the IQR, both fences and the comparison. Writing "it looks far away" earns nothing.

**"Which measure is better?"** Name the shape or the outlier, then the resistant or non-resistant property.

**"Describe how to select the sample."** Label, random mechanism, ignore repeats, identify the sample. For stratified, say what the strata are and why they are similar inside.

**"Explain the bias."** Name it, who is over- or under-represented, how they differ, and the direction.

**"Design an experiment."** Units, treatments, a detailed random assignment, what is held constant, what is measured, how it is compared; add blocking if a variable clearly affects the response.

**"What can be concluded?"** Random assignment → cause; random selection → generalize; say both.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a number-coded label (jersey number, ZIP code) as quantitative.
- Calling a bar chart a histogram, or leaving no gaps between categorical bars.
- Naming skew by the side where the data pile up instead of the tail.
- Reporting the mean and SD for a strongly skewed distribution.
- Listing two groups' statistics without comparing; leaving out context.
- "Larger sample, so no bias."
- Calling nonresponse "voluntary response".
- Claiming cause from an observational study; generalizing from volunteers.
- "Randomly assign them" with no mechanism; forgetting to ignore repeats.
- Treating blocking as a substitute for random assignment.

## Calculator or not?

Calculators are allowed throughout. Use one-variable statistics for the mean, SD and five-number summary (calculator quartiles may differ slightly from the by-hand method; either is accepted), and a random integer generator for samples and assignments. Most Unit 1 points are earned by the writing.

## Self-test

1. Classify: number of siblings; eye colour; time to run 400 m.
2. Of 160 students, 0.35 chose soccer. How many is that?
3. Data: 3, 5, 6, 6, 8, 9, 12, 30. Find the median, IQR and fences. Is 30 an outlier?
4. Which is larger for a right-skewed distribution, the mean or the median?
5. Strata or clusters: 30 homerooms, choose 3 and survey everyone.
6. A web poll gets 10,000 votes. Name the bias.
7. Two fertilizers and three watering levels: how many treatments?
8. Volunteers randomly assigned to two diets. What can be concluded, and about whom?

**Answers.** 1. Discrete quantitative; categorical; continuous quantitative. 2. 0.35 × 160 = 56. 3. Median 7, Q1 5.5, Q3 10.5, IQR 5, fences −2 and 18; 30 is an outlier. 4. The mean. 5. Clusters (cluster sample). 6. Voluntary response. 7. 2 × 3 = 6. 8. Cause and effect, for people like the volunteers only.
