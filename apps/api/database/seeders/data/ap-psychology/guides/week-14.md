## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Developmental psychology | The study of physical, cognitive and social change across the whole lifespan. |
| Cross-sectional design | Compares people of different ages at one point in time. |
| Longitudinal design | Follows the same people and measures them repeatedly over time. |
| Cohort effect | A difference between age groups caused by the era they grew up in, not by age itself. |
| Attrition | Loss of participants from a study over time; it can bias a longitudinal sample. |
| Teratogen | An agent (alcohol, nicotine, some medicines, some viruses) that can harm prenatal development. |
| Maturation | Biological growth that unfolds in a set order, largely independent of experience. |
| Critical period | A window when a particular experience must occur for normal development. |
| Puberty | The period of sexual maturation that makes reproduction possible. |

## Three debates that run through the unit (3.1)

Developmental psychologists keep returning to three questions. None has a winner; the useful answer is always "both, and how they interact."

1. **Nature and nurture.** How much of development is driven by genes and biological maturation, and how much by experience? Unit 1 introduced the modern answer: genes and environments interact. A child with a genetic tendency toward a high activity level will be treated differently by parents and teachers, and that treatment shapes the child further. Epigenetics (1.1) adds that experience can change which genes are expressed.
2. **Continuity and stages.** Is development a smooth ramp (gradual, continuous growth) or a staircase (distinct stages, each qualitatively different)? Stage theorists such as Piaget (week 16) and Erikson (week 17) describe stairs; learning theorists (weeks 18–20) describe a ramp.
3. **Stability and change.** Do early traits last? Temperament shows real stability from infancy, and personality traits become more stable with age. But people also change a great deal: adolescents are not simply larger children, and adults keep developing.

## How development is studied (3.1)

Developmental researchers use every method from Unit 1, plus designs built around age.

**Cross-sectional design.** Test 20-, 40-, 60- and 80-year-olds this month and compare them. It is fast and cheap. Its weakness is **cohort effects**: the 80-year-olds grew up with different schooling, nutrition and technology, so a difference between age groups might reflect the era, not aging.

**Longitudinal design.** Test the same people at 20, 40, 60 and 80. It shows how individuals actually change and whether early traits predict later ones. Its weaknesses are time, cost and **attrition**: participants move, lose interest or die, and the people who stay are often healthier or more motivated than those who leave, which biases the results.

**Cross-sequential design.** Start several age cohorts at once and follow each for a few years. It separates age effects from cohort effects more cheaply than a full longitudinal study.

**Worked example 1: a hypothetical memory study.** A research team tests word-list recall in 300 adults aged 25, 50 and 75, all in the same month. Mean recall is 18, 16 and 12 words. Can the team say aging lowers recall? Not cleanly: this is a cross-sectional design, so the 75-year-olds also differ in years of schooling and familiarity with test-taking. A longitudinal follow-up of the same people would show change within individuals; a cross-sequential design would let the team check whether the pattern holds across cohorts. Also note that age is never randomly assigned, so no developmental study of age is a true experiment on age itself; researchers can still run experiments within an age group (for example, randomly assigning 4-year-olds to two teaching methods).

**Studying infants.** Babies cannot answer questions, so researchers measure what they look at or suck on. In **habituation** studies, an infant sees the same picture until looking time drops (the baby is bored); a new picture that brings looking time back up shows the infant can tell the two apart. **Preferential looking** shows two images side by side; a reliable preference (newborns look longer at face-like patterns) shows discrimination. The dependent variable is operationally defined as seconds of looking, scored by observers who should be blind to which stimulus is showing.

**Ethics with children.** Children cannot give informed consent on their own: researchers get **parental permission** and the child's **assent** (agreement in age-appropriate words), let the child stop at any time, and keep data confidential. An institutional review board (IRB) reviews the plan first.

## Prenatal development (3.2)

Development begins at conception, when a sperm fertilizes an egg to form a **zygote**.

| Stage | Timing | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Germinal | Conception to about 2 weeks | Rapid cell division; the zygote implants in the uterine wall. |
| Embryonic | About weeks 3–8 | Major organs and structures form; the period most vulnerable to teratogens. |
| Fetal | Week 9 to birth | Organs grow and mature; the fetus gains weight and can respond to sound. |

**Teratogens** are agents that can cross the placenta and disrupt development. Alcohol is the best-known example: heavy prenatal exposure can cause **fetal alcohol spectrum disorders**, which may include distinctive facial features, smaller head size and lifelong learning and attention difficulties. Nicotine, some prescription and recreational drugs, and infections such as rubella can also do harm. Harm depends on dose, timing (the embryonic period is most sensitive for structural damage) and genetic susceptibility, a nature-nurture interaction.

## Infancy and childhood (3.2)

**Newborn reflexes** are unlearned, automatic responses that help survival:

- **Rooting:** touch a cheek and the baby turns toward it and opens the mouth.
- **Sucking:** anything placed in the mouth is sucked.
- **Grasping:** fingers close around an object pressed into the palm.
- **Moro (startle):** after a sudden loss of support or loud noise, the arms fling out and then pull in.
- **Babinski:** stroke the sole and the toes fan out.

Most fade within months as the cortex matures; their absence or persistence can alert doctors to neurological problems.

**Newborn senses.** Vision is blurry at birth and sharpest at about the distance to a caregiver's face during feeding; newborns prefer face-like patterns and recognize their mother's voice and smell within days. Depth perception develops in the first year: in the **visual cliff** studies (Gibson and Walk, 1960), most crawling infants would not cross a glass surface over an apparent drop (2.1).

**Motor development and maturation.** Infants lift the head, roll over, sit, crawl, stand and then walk, usually around their first birthday. The **sequence** is nearly universal because it follows **maturation** of the nervous system and muscles; the **timing** varies with individual differences and with culture (in some cultures babies are encouraged to sit and walk early, and they do). Practice cannot push a baby to walk before the necessary neural and muscular maturation. Brain growth in early childhood is explosive: synapses are overproduced, then unused ones are pruned (use it or lose it), and myelination speeds neural transmission (1.3).

**Critical and sensitive periods.** A **critical period** is a fixed window when an experience must happen. Konrad Lorenz showed that newly hatched goslings follow the first moving object they see, a process called **imprinting**, and goslings that imprinted on him followed him. Human development mostly has **sensitive periods**, windows when learning is easiest but not impossible afterward, as with learning the sounds of a first language (week 16).

## Adolescence (3.2)

**Puberty** is the stretch of hormonal and physical change that brings sexual maturity. **Primary sex characteristics** are the reproductive organs themselves (ovaries, testes, and so on); **secondary sex characteristics** are other body features that change, such as breast development, voice deepening and body and facial hair. Landmarks include **menarche** (first menstrual period) and **spermarche** (first ejaculation). On average, puberty begins a year or two earlier in girls than boys, and the timing varies widely among healthy people. Early or late timing relative to peers can affect how adolescents feel and are treated, which is another nature-nurture interaction.

**The adolescent brain.** The limbic system, including reward-sensitive circuits, matures earlier than the **prefrontal cortex**, which supports planning, impulse control and judgment (1.4). Myelination and pruning in the frontal lobes continue into the mid-20s. One result is a period in which reward sensitivity is high and self-control is still developing, which helps explain why risk-taking rises in adolescence, especially around peers.

## Adulthood and later life (3.2)

Physical abilities such as muscle strength, reaction time and sensory sharpness generally peak in early adulthood and decline gradually afterward. In midlife, **menopause** (the end of menstruation, usually around age 50) marks the end of fertility for women; men's reproductive hormones decline more gradually. Later life brings slower reaction time, reduced vision and hearing, and some loss of brain volume, but the size of the decline varies a lot. Physical exercise, social engagement and mental activity are associated with better health and cognition in older adults, though much of that evidence is correlational. Some older adults develop a **neurocognitive disorder**, such as one due to Alzheimer's disease, in which memory and other abilities decline far beyond normal aging; this is a disorder, not an inevitable part of growing old.

**Worked example 2: reading a correlation.** In a hypothetical survey of 500 adults over 65, weekly hours of exercise and scores on a memory test correlate at r = +0.32. The positive sign means more exercise goes with higher scores; 0.32 is a weak-to-moderate relationship. Because the variables were measured, not manipulated, the data cannot show that exercise causes better memory: healthier people may simply exercise more (a third variable). A study that randomly assigns older adults to an exercise program or a waitlist could test cause and effect.

## Common mistakes

- **Calling a cross-sectional study longitudinal.** Ask: are the same people measured more than once? If not, it is cross-sectional.
- **Forgetting cohort effects.** Differences between age groups in a cross-sectional study may come from the era people grew up in.
- **Mixing primary and secondary sex characteristics.** Reproductive organs are primary; other features such as voice change and body hair are secondary.
- **Treating sequence and timing as the same.** Maturation fixes the order of motor milestones; experience and individual differences change the timing.
- **Saying the teenage brain is "broken."** It is still developing, with an earlier-maturing limbic system and a later-maturing prefrontal cortex.

## Self-check

1. A researcher tests the same 120 children at ages 3, 6 and 9. Name the design and one weakness.
2. During which prenatal stage do the major organs form?
3. A baby turns toward a finger stroking her cheek. Name the reflex.
4. Why does the order of motor milestones vary little across cultures while the timing varies more?
5. Which brain region matures last, and what does it support?
6. In a habituation study, looking time rises when a new picture appears. What does that show?

*Answers:* 1. Longitudinal; attrition (or time and cost). 2. The embryonic stage (about weeks 3–8). 3. Rooting. 4. The order follows biological maturation of the nervous system and muscles; timing depends on experience and individual differences. 5. The prefrontal cortex; planning, impulse control and judgment. 6. The infant can tell the new picture from the old one.
