## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Learning | A relatively lasting change in behavior or knowledge due to experience. |
| Habituation | A decrease in response to a repeated stimulus that has no consequence. |
| Classical conditioning | Learning to associate two stimuli so that one comes to trigger a response the other already produced. |
| Unconditioned stimulus (US) | A stimulus that triggers a response naturally, without learning. |
| Unconditioned response (UR) | The unlearned response to the US. |
| Neutral stimulus (NS) | A stimulus that at first triggers no relevant response. |
| Conditioned stimulus (CS) | The former neutral stimulus, which now triggers a response after pairing with the US. |
| Conditioned response (CR) | The learned response to the CS. |

## What learning is (3.7)

**Learning** is a relatively lasting change in behavior or knowledge that comes from experience, not from maturation, fatigue or injury. The simplest kind is **non-associative**: **habituation** is a decrease in response to a stimulus repeated without consequence (you stop noticing the hum of a refrigerator), the same process that powered the infant looking-time studies in week 14. **Associative learning** links events: two stimuli (classical conditioning, this week), or a behavior and its consequence (operant conditioning, week 19).

## Pavlov's discovery (3.7)

Ivan Pavlov, a Russian physiologist studying digestion around 1900, noticed that his dogs began to salivate before food reached their mouths, at the sight of the food dish or the sound of the assistant's footsteps. He studied this systematically: he repeatedly presented a neutral sound (such as a metronome or buzzer) just before giving food. After a number of pairings, the sound alone made the dogs salivate.

| Before conditioning | During conditioning | After conditioning |
|---|---|---|
| Food (US) → salivation (UR) | Sound (NS) + food (US) → salivation (UR) | Sound (CS) → salivation (CR) |
| Sound (NS) → no salivation | Repeated pairings | The sound alone now triggers salivation |

**How to label any scenario.** Ask three questions in order:

1. What produces a response **without learning**? That is the **US**, and the response to it is the **UR**.
2. What was **paired** with the US and started out meaningless? That is the **NS**, which becomes the **CS**.
3. What response does the CS now produce? That is the **CR**, usually similar to the UR.

**Worked example 1: the dentist's drill.** As a child, Ana felt pain (and fear) when the dentist's drill touched a tooth. Now the sound of a drill in the waiting room makes her heart race. US: the painful drilling. UR: fear and a racing heart to the pain. NS then CS: the sound of the drill. CR: fear and a racing heart at the sound alone. The racing heart is a sympathetic nervous system response (1.2), and the amygdala (1.4) is central to fear conditioning.

## The processes (3.7)

- **Acquisition** is the initial learning of the CS-US association. It works best when the CS comes **shortly before** the US and reliably predicts it (forward conditioning). Presenting the US before the CS (backward conditioning) produces little learning.
- **Extinction** is the weakening of the CR when the CS is repeatedly presented **without** the US. Pavlov's dogs gradually stopped salivating to a sound never followed by food.
- **Spontaneous recovery** is the reappearance of a weakened CR after a rest period. It shows that extinction suppresses the response rather than erasing the learning.
- **Generalization** is responding to stimuli **similar** to the CS (a dog conditioned to one tone also salivates to a slightly different tone).
- **Discrimination** is learning to respond to the CS but **not** to similar stimuli that were never paired with the US.
- **Higher-order (second-order) conditioning** happens when an established CS acts like a US for a new neutral stimulus: if a tone already triggers salivation, pairing a light with the tone can make the light trigger salivation too, without food.

**Worked example 2: reading an extinction graph.** A hypothetical lab conditions rabbits to blink at a tone paired with a puff of air. In a table of trials, the percentage of trials with a blink to the tone alone rises from 5% to 85% over 10 acquisition sessions; during 6 sessions of tone without air puff it falls to 15%; after a day's rest, it jumps to 45% on the first session before falling again. Rising percentage = acquisition; falling = extinction; the jump after rest = spontaneous recovery. Reading the variables: the independent variable within the procedure is the phase (tone paired or unpaired), and the dependent variable is operationally defined as the percentage of tone-alone trials with a blink.

## Little Albert and counterconditioning (3.7)

In 1920, John B. Watson and Rosalie Rayner conditioned fear in an infant they called "Albert B." At first Albert showed no fear of a white rat. The researchers then paired the rat with a sudden loud noise (striking a steel bar) behind his head, which made him startle and cry. After several pairings, Albert cried and tried to crawl away at the sight of the rat alone, and his fear **generalized** to other furry things, such as a rabbit and a fur coat. Labels: US = loud noise; UR = fear; CS = rat; CR = fear of the rat.

**Ethics.** By today's standards the study was clearly unethical: it deliberately caused distress to an infant, it is unclear whether the mother gave fully informed consent, Albert was not deconditioned before the study ended, and there was no follow-up. Modern IRBs would not approve it.

**Counterconditioning** replaces an unwanted CR with a new, incompatible response. In 1924, Mary Cover Jones reduced a young boy's fear of rabbits by bringing a rabbit gradually closer while he ate food he enjoyed. This idea underlies **systematic desensitization** and other exposure-based therapies you will meet in Unit 5. **Aversive conditioning** does the reverse, pairing an unwanted behavior's cues with an unpleasant state.

## Biology and cognition limit conditioning (3.7)

Early behaviorists believed any stimulus could be paired with any response equally well. Two lines of research showed otherwise.

**Taste aversion.** Rats in John Garcia and Robert Koelling's 1966 experiments readily learned to avoid a flavored drink that was followed by nausea (induced by radiation or a drug), but did not learn to avoid it when it was followed by a shock. Conversely, they learned to fear a light-and-sound cue followed by shock but not one followed by nausea. Taste aversions also form in **one trial** and with **hours** between the taste and the illness, unlike most conditioning. Many people have avoided a food for years after a single bout of illness that followed eating it, even when they know the food was not the cause.

**Biological preparedness** (a term from Martin Seligman) is the idea that evolution makes some associations easier to learn: taste with illness, fear with snakes, spiders and heights. This helps explain why phobias of snakes and spiders are far more common than phobias of cars, even though cars cause far more deaths today.

**Prediction matters.** Later research (notably by Robert Rescorla) showed that pairing is not enough: the CS must reliably **predict** the US. If the US occurs just as often without the CS, little conditioning occurs. Classical conditioning is therefore partly cognitive: organisms learn what signals what.

## Classical conditioning in everyday life (3.7)

- **Advertising** pairs a product (NS) with attractive people, music or humor (US) so the product comes to evoke good feelings (CR).
- **Fears and anxiety**: a car horn heard before a near-accident can later trigger anxiety; a smell linked with a hospital stay can bring back dread.
- **Drug cues**: places and objects associated with drug use can become CSs that trigger craving, one reason relapse often happens in familiar settings.
- **Emotional associations**: a song from a happy summer can make you smile years later.

## Common mistakes

- **Labeling the response, not the stimulus.** The US and CS are stimuli (things that happen); the UR and CR are responses.
- **Thinking the UR and CR must differ.** They are often the same kind of response (salivation, fear); what differs is the stimulus that triggers it.
- **Treating extinction as forgetting.** Spontaneous recovery shows the association remains.
- **Confusing generalization with discrimination.** Generalization spreads the response; discrimination narrows it.
- **Mixing classical and operant conditioning.** Classical conditioning pairs two stimuli and involves automatic responses; operant conditioning links a voluntary behavior to its consequences (week 19).

## Self-check

1. Every morning Jamal's old alarm clock gave a soft click a second before blaring a piercing alarm that made him jump. Now the click alone makes him tense. Identify the US, UR, CS and CR.
2. Jamal now tenses at the click of a pen in class. Which process is this?
3. He keeps the clock but switches the alarm off, so the click is never followed by the blare, and his tensing at the click fades. Which process is this?
4. Why does a single episode of food poisoning often produce a lasting aversion?
5. Name two ethical problems with the Little Albert study.

*Answers:* 1. US: the piercing alarm; UR: jumping and tensing at the alarm; CS: the click; CR: tensing at the click. 2. Generalization. 3. Extinction. 4. Biological preparedness: taste-illness associations form in one trial even with a long delay. 5. Any two: it caused deliberate distress to an infant, informed consent is doubtful, there was no deconditioning, there was no follow-up.
