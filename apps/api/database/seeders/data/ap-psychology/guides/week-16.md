## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Schema | A mental framework that organizes knowledge about something. |
| Assimilation | Fitting new experience into an existing schema. |
| Accommodation | Changing a schema, or making a new one, to fit new experience. |
| Object permanence | Knowing that objects continue to exist when out of sight. |
| Conservation | Knowing that quantity stays the same despite changes in shape or arrangement. |
| Egocentrism | Difficulty taking another person's point of view. |
| Zone of proximal development | The range between what a child can do alone and what the child can do with help. |
| Theory of mind | Understanding that others have beliefs, desires and knowledge different from one's own. |
| Phoneme / morpheme | Smallest unit of sound / smallest unit of meaning. |
| Syntax | Rules for ordering words into sentences. |

## Piaget's building blocks (3.4)

Swiss psychologist Jean Piaget watched children closely, including his own, and argued that children are active thinkers who build understanding by interacting with the world. The unit of that understanding is the **schema**. When a toddler with a "dog" schema calls a cat a "doggy," she is **assimilating**: fitting the new animal into an existing schema. When she learns that cats meow and dogs bark and makes a separate "cat" schema, she is **accommodating**: changing her schemas to fit experience. Piaget believed children move through stages as accommodation reorganizes their thinking, and that each stage is qualitatively different: a staircase, not a ramp (3.1).

## Piaget's four stages (3.4)

| Stage | Typical ages | Key achievement | Key limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sensorimotor | Birth to about 2 | Learning through senses and actions; object permanence | Before object permanence, out of sight is out of mind |
| Preoperational | About 2 to 7 | Symbolic thought: language, pretend play | Egocentrism; no conservation; centration; animism |
| Concrete operational | About 7 to 11 | Conservation; reversibility; logic about concrete events | Difficulty with abstract and hypothetical problems |
| Formal operational | About 12 and up | Abstract and hypothetical reasoning; systematic testing of ideas | Not reached by everyone or in every domain |

**Sensorimotor.** Infants explore by looking, grasping and mouthing. Early on, a toy hidden under a blanket seems to vanish: the infant stops searching. Piaget placed **object permanence** at around 8 months, when infants start to search for hidden objects.

**Preoperational.** Children use symbols (words, pretend play: a block becomes a phone), but their thinking is not yet logical. **Egocentrism**: a 4-year-old hides by covering her eyes, or nods into the phone, assuming others see what she sees. In Piaget's three-mountains task, young children asked what a doll across the table could see described their own view. **Conservation** is missing: pour juice from a short, wide glass into a tall, thin one and a 4-year-old says there is now more, because she focuses on height alone (**centration**) and cannot mentally reverse the pour (**irreversibility**). **Animism**, giving lifelike qualities to objects ("the sun is following us"), is common.

**Concrete operational.** Children now conserve number, volume and mass; they understand **reversibility** (pouring the juice back would give the same glass) and can sort and order objects logically. Their logic works best on concrete things they can picture.

**Formal operational.** Adolescents can reason about abstractions ("What if people had no need to sleep?"), test hypotheses systematically, and think about thinking. Not everyone uses formal reasoning in every area.

## Testing Piaget (3.4)

Later research supported Piaget's broad sequence but challenged details:

- **Infants know more, earlier.** Using looking-time methods (week 14), researchers found that infants only a few months old look longer at "impossible" events, such as a toy seeming to pass through a solid wall, suggesting some understanding of objects well before 8 months.
- **Development is more continuous** and uneven than strict stages suggest; a child may conserve number before volume.
- **Culture and schooling matter.** Formal operational reasoning depends partly on education and familiarity with the task.

**Lev Vygotsky**, working in Russia at about the same time, emphasized the **social** side of cognitive growth. Children learn through interaction with more skilled people. The **zone of proximal development** is the gap between what a child can do alone and what the child can do with guidance; **scaffolding** is the temporary support (hints, modeling, breaking a task into steps) that an adult or peer gives within that zone and gradually removes. Vygotsky also saw **private speech** (children talking themselves through a task) as thought becoming internal.

**Theory of mind.** Around age 4 or 5, most children pass **false-belief tasks**. A child watches a puppet put a marble in a basket and leave; another puppet moves the marble to a box. Where will the first puppet look? Most 3-year-olds say "the box" (where they know it is); most 5-year-olds say "the basket," showing they understand the puppet has a false belief. Theory of mind develops on different timelines for some children, including many autistic children.

**Adolescent egocentrism.** Teenagers' new ability to think about thinking produces two characteristic distortions: the **imaginary audience** (feeling that everyone is watching and judging) and the **personal fable** (believing one's experiences are unique and that "it won't happen to me").

## Cognition in adulthood (3.4)

From 2.8: **crystallized intelligence** (accumulated knowledge and vocabulary) tends to hold steady or grow into later adulthood, while **fluid intelligence** (fast, flexible reasoning with new problems) and processing speed peak in early adulthood and decline gradually. Older adults are typically slower at learning new information and recalling names, and more vulnerable to interference, but their knowledge and judgment in familiar domains often remain strong. Recognition holds up better than free recall. Large declines in memory and reasoning are not normal aging; they may signal a neurocognitive disorder (week 14).

**Worked example 1: a cross-sectional trap.** A hypothetical cross-sectional study finds 70-year-olds score lower than 30-year-olds on a reasoning test. A longitudinal study of the same people from 30 to 70 finds a much smaller decline. Why the difference? Cohort effects: the 70-year-olds had less schooling and less familiarity with such tests than today's 30-year-olds (scores on such tests have also risen across generations, the Flynn effect from 2.8). The design changes the answer.

## The building blocks of language (3.5)

| Unit | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Phoneme | Smallest distinct sound unit | The sounds s, a and t in "sat" |
| Morpheme | Smallest unit that carries meaning | "Unhappily" = un + happy + ly |
| Semantics | The meaning of words and sentences | Knowing "bank" means a riverside in "We fished from the bank" |
| Syntax (grammar) | Rules for ordering words | "The dog bit the boy" differs from "The boy bit the dog" |
| Pragmatics | Social rules for using language | Speaking more simply to a toddler |

## Milestones (3.5)

- **Before speech:** newborns prefer human speech and can tell apart phonemes from many languages. Infants start to **babble** (repeated syllables like "ba-ba-ba") at around 4 to 6 months. By about 10 months, babbling and perception narrow toward the phonemes of the home language, and infants lose much of their ability to tell apart sounds their language does not use.
- **One-word stage:** around the first birthday, single words often stand for whole ideas ("Juice!" = "I want juice").
- **Two-word stage:** around 18 to 24 months, **telegraphic speech** keeps content words and drops the rest ("Daddy go," "more cookie"), yet already follows the home language's word order.
- **Explosion:** vocabulary grows rapidly in the preschool years, and children begin speaking in complete sentences.
- **Overregularization (overgeneralization):** children apply grammar rules to exceptions ("I goed," "two mouses"), evidence that they are learning rules rather than only copying, since adults rarely say those forms.

## Explaining language acquisition (3.5)

- **Learning (behaviorist) view.** B. F. Skinner argued that children learn language through association, imitation and reinforcement (parents smile at "mama"). This explains vocabulary and accent, but not overregularization or the speed of grammar learning.
- **Nativist view.** Noam Chomsky argued that humans are born with a capacity for grammar, a **universal grammar** or language acquisition device, so children need only exposure to pick up their language's specific rules. This explains universal milestones and the speed of learning.
- **Interactionist view.** Most researchers now combine the two: a biological readiness for language develops through social interaction, such as parents' simplified, sing-song "infant-directed" speech.

**Sensitive period.** Language is learned most easily in childhood. Children who learn a second language early are more likely to speak it without an accent and with native-like grammar. Deaf children who are not exposed to any sign or spoken language until later childhood tend to have lasting difficulties with grammar, which shows that early exposure matters.

**Language and thought.** The **linguistic relativity** hypothesis (associated with Benjamin Lee Whorf) proposes that language shapes thinking. The strong form (language determines what we can think) has little support; a weaker form, in which language influences attention, memory and categorization, has some.

**The brain.** From 1.4: damage to **Broca's area** (left frontal lobe) impairs speech production, and damage to **Wernicke's area** (left temporal lobe) impairs comprehension.

**Worked example 2: an experiment on infant-directed speech.** A hypothetical lab randomly assigns 60 eight-month-olds to hear a new word spoken in infant-directed speech or in adult-directed speech, then measures looking time toward the matching object. Mean looking times are 7.2 and 5.1 seconds. Random assignment means the speech style (independent variable) can be judged a cause of the difference in looking time (dependent variable); a t-test would show whether the difference is statistically significant.

## Common mistakes

- **Swapping assimilation and accommodation.** Assimilation keeps the schema; accommodation changes it.
- **Placing conservation in the preoperational stage.** Preoperational children lack it; concrete operational children have it.
- **Confusing phonemes and morphemes.** Sounds versus meaning units.
- **Calling "I goed" a sign of poor learning.** Overregularization shows a child has learned a rule.
- **Treating Piaget's ages as fixed.** The sequence is the claim; the ages are approximate.

## Self-check

1. A child calls a zebra a "horsey." Which process is this?
2. Which stage begins when a child can conserve volume?
3. A teacher helps a student solve one step of a problem, then lets the student finish. Name Vygotsky's concept.
4. How many morphemes are in "replayed"?
5. Which view of language best explains "two foots"?
6. Why might a 3-year-old fail a false-belief task?

*Answers:* 1. Assimilation. 2. Concrete operational. 3. Scaffolding (within the zone of proximal development). 4. Three: re + play + ed. 5. The nativist (rule-learning) view; reinforcement alone would not produce a form adults never say. 6. The child lacks a developed theory of mind, so assumes others know what she knows.
