## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Attribution | An explanation of the cause of a behavior or an event. |
| Dispositional (internal) attribution | Explaining behavior by the person's traits, abilities, effort or motives. |
| Situational (external) attribution | Explaining behavior by circumstances: the task, other people, luck, pressure. |
| Fundamental attribution error | Overestimating disposition and underestimating situation when explaining *other people's* behavior. |
| Actor-observer bias | Explaining our own actions situationally while explaining the same actions in others dispositionally. |
| Self-serving bias | Taking personal credit for successes and blaming the situation for failures. |
| Explanatory style | A habitual way of explaining bad events: pessimistic (internal, stable, global) or optimistic (external, unstable, specific). |
| Locus of control | A general belief that outcomes are controlled by one's own actions (internal) or by outside forces (external). |
| Mere exposure effect | Repeated exposure to a neutral stimulus increases liking for it. |
| Self-fulfilling prophecy | An expectation that leads to behavior that makes the expectation come true. |
| Social comparison | Judging our own abilities and opinions by comparing ourselves with others; upward (with those better off) or downward (with those worse off). |
| Relative deprivation | Feeling deprived because one has less than a comparison group, whatever one's absolute situation. |

## Two kinds of explanation

Social psychology is the study of how people think about, influence and relate to one another. Its first question is ordinary: *why did they do that?* Fritz Heider, who founded attribution theory in the 1950s, noticed that people act like everyday scientists, sorting causes into two bins.

- A **dispositional attribution** points inside the person: "She snapped at me because she is rude."
- A **situational attribution** points to circumstances: "She snapped at me because she had just been told her shift was doubled."

Most behavior has both kinds of cause; the trouble is that we do not weigh them evenly.

### The fundamental attribution error

When we watch *someone else*, we tend to overweight their disposition and underweight their situation. Lee Ross named this the **fundamental attribution error** in 1977. A classic demonstration came a decade earlier: Edward Jones and Victor Harris (1967) had college students read essays that either praised or criticized Fidel Castro's government in Cuba. Some readers were told the writer had freely chosen the side; others were told a debate coach had assigned it. Even readers who knew the side had been assigned still judged the writer's true attitude to match the essay. Knowing about a powerful situation did not stop them from reading the behavior as a window into the person.

Why? The person is vivid; the situation is often invisible. Correcting for it takes effort and information we often lack.

Culture matters: people in more collectivist cultures generally give more weight to situations and roles, so the error is strongest in individualist cultures, and "fundamental" overstates how universal it is.

### Actor-observer bias

When *we* are the actor, we see the situation we are reacting to, so we explain our own behavior situationally. "I was late because traffic was terrible." When we observe someone else doing the same thing: "He was late because he is careless." The **actor-observer bias** is the difference between those two explanations of the *same* behavior.

### Self-serving bias

The **self-serving bias** is about outcomes, not just perspective. People tend to credit successes to themselves ("I studied hard; I'm good at this") and blame failures on the situation ("the test was unfair"). It protects self-esteem. Unlike the actor-observer bias, it *flips* with whether the outcome was good or bad.

**Worked example 1: name the bias.**

| Scenario | Bias | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Marisol aces her chemistry test and says it was her talent; she fails the next one and blames the teacher's tricky wording. | Self-serving bias | Internal for success, external for failure. |
| Jordan says he skipped practice because his ride fell through, but says a teammate who skipped is lazy. | Actor-observer bias | Same behavior, situational for self, dispositional for the other. |
| A substitute teacher decides a quiet student is shy and unfriendly, without knowing she has just moved to the school this week. | Fundamental attribution error | Disposition overweighted for someone else; the situation is ignored. |

## Explanatory style and locus of control

Attributions about single events add up to habits.

**Explanatory style** describes how a person habitually explains bad events. Martin Seligman and colleagues described three dimensions:

- **internal versus external** ("it's me" versus "it's the circumstances"),
- **stable versus unstable** ("it will always be like this" versus "it's temporary"),
- **global versus specific** ("it ruins everything" versus "it affects just this").

A **pessimistic explanatory style** explains bad events as internal, stable and global: "I failed the driving test because I'm hopeless at everything, and I always will be." An **optimistic explanatory style** explains them as external, unstable and specific: "I failed because I was nervous and the course was new to me; next time will be different." Correlational research links a pessimistic style with lower persistence and higher risk of depression, a connection Unit 5 returns to. Because the evidence is correlational, it cannot show by itself that the style causes the outcomes.

**Locus of control**, from Julian Rotter's work in the 1960s, is a broader belief about whether outcomes in life are controlled by our own actions (**internal locus**) or by luck, fate and powerful others (**external locus**). A student who believes "if I put in the work my grades go up" has an internal locus. Explanatory style is about explaining events after they happen; locus of control is a general expectation about control. It returns in Topic 4.5.

## Person perception

The second half of the topic is how we form impressions of others, and how they loop back to shape behavior.

**Mere exposure effect.** Robert Zajonc showed in the late 1960s that simply being exposed repeatedly to a neutral stimulus (nonsense words, unfamiliar faces, Chinese-like characters shown to people who could not read Chinese) increased how much people liked it. Familiarity breeds liking, at least for things that start out neutral.

**Self-fulfilling prophecy.** An expectation can change behavior so that the expectation comes true. In Robert Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson's well-known 1960s study, teachers were told that certain randomly chosen elementary students were about to "bloom" intellectually; by the end of the year some of those students, especially in the youngest grades, showed larger IQ gains than their classmates. The study has been criticized for its measurement, and later studies found teacher-expectation effects to be real but generally small. The mechanism is the important idea: teachers who expect more may give more attention, wait longer for answers and offer more feedback, and students respond.

**Social comparison.** Leon Festinger proposed in 1954 that when there is no objective yardstick, people evaluate their abilities and opinions by comparing themselves with others. **Upward social comparison** (with people who are better off or more skilled) can motivate, but can also lower satisfaction. **Downward social comparison** (with people who are worse off) tends to make us feel better about ourselves.

**Relative deprivation** follows from social comparison: people feel deprived not because of what they have, but because of what they have compared with their reference group. A student who gets a B can feel worse than one who gets a C if all her friends got As.

**Worked example 2: person perception in a new job.** Devon starts a summer job. In week one he likes his supervisor more each day although they barely talk (*mere exposure*). His supervisor was told Devon was "a slacker" by a former coworker, so she gives him only dull tasks and rarely explains anything; Devon, bored and confused, starts to coast, and she feels proved right (*self-fulfilling prophecy*). Devon earns $12 an hour, more than his last job, but is unhappy after learning that a friend at another store earns $15 (*upward social comparison* producing *relative deprivation*).

## Research methods: studying attributions

**An experiment.** Suppose a hypothetical research team randomly assigns 120 students to read about a student who failed a test. In one condition the description includes a situational detail (the student's family had a medical emergency the night before); in the other it does not. The **independent variable** is whether the situational information is present. The **dependent variable** is the attribution, which needs an **operational definition**, for example "a rating from 1 (entirely due to the situation) to 7 (entirely due to the student's ability or effort)". **Random assignment** spreads other differences (mood, prior beliefs about tests) evenly across groups, so a difference in the means can be attributed to the manipulation. If the no-detail group's mean rating is 5.4 and the detail group's is 4.1, and the difference is **statistically significant** (unlikely to be due to chance alone, conventionally p below 0.05), the researchers can say situational information reduced dispositional blame. **Effect size** (for example Cohen's d) says how large that difference is, which significance alone does not.

**A correlational study.** A team surveys 300 adults on explanatory style and on how many weeks they kept up a new exercise plan, and finds r = −0.32 between pessimism scores and weeks of exercise. That is a moderate negative correlation: more pessimistic people tended to quit sooner. It does *not* show that pessimism causes quitting; a third variable (such as health problems or stress) could produce both, and the direction could even run the other way.

**Ethics.** Attribution experiments often use **deception** (a fictional student, a cover story about "first impressions"). Deception is allowed only when an **institutional review board (IRB)** judges it necessary and low in risk, and participants must be **debriefed** afterward: told the true purpose and why deception was used. **Informed consent** and **confidentiality** of responses still apply.

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing the actor-observer bias with the self-serving bias.** Actor-observer depends on whose behavior it is; self-serving depends on whether the outcome was good or bad.
- **Calling every "blame the person" judgment the fundamental attribution error.** It is an error only when the situation is underweighted; sometimes the dispositional explanation is correct.
- **Mixing up locus of control and explanatory style.** Locus is a general expectancy about control; style is how bad events are explained.
- **Treating the self-fulfilling prophecy as a simple prediction.** The expectation must *cause* behavior that makes it come true.
- **Reading cause into a correlation** between explanatory style and outcomes.
- **Forgetting debriefing** whenever a study uses deception.

## Self-check

1. A coach says a player who missed a free throw "has no nerve", ignoring the screaming crowd. Name the error.
2. After winning a debate you credit your preparation; after losing, the judges' bias. Name the bias.
3. Classify: "I bombed the interview because I'm worthless and I'll never get any job."
4. Someone believes promotions go to whoever the boss happens to like. Internal or external locus of control?
5. Why might students rate a song higher on the fifth hearing than the first?
6. In an attribution experiment, what is the purpose of random assignment?

*Answers:* 1. Fundamental attribution error. 2. Self-serving bias. 3. Pessimistic explanatory style (internal, stable, global). 4. External. 5. The mere exposure effect: repeated exposure to a neutral stimulus increases liking. 6. To spread participant differences evenly across conditions, so that a difference in the dependent variable can be attributed to the independent variable rather than to who ended up in which group.
