## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Psychological disorder | A clinically significant disturbance in thinking, emotion regulation or behavior, usually with distress or dysfunction. |
| DSM-5-TR | The American Psychiatric Association's diagnostic manual (2022), based on symptoms and duration. |
| ICD-11 | The World Health Organization's classification of diseases, used worldwide. |
| Reliability (of diagnosis) | Consistency: different clinicians reach the same diagnosis. |
| Validity (of diagnosis) | Accuracy: the diagnosis captures a real condition and predicts its course. |
| Comorbidity | Two or more disorders in the same person at the same time. |
| Diathesis-stress model | A predisposition plus environmental stress produces a disorder. |
| Medical model | Disorders as illnesses with physical causes that can be diagnosed and treated. |
| Stigma | Negative stereotypes and discrimination attached to a condition. |

## What counts as a disorder?

There is no single test. Clinicians look for a **clinically significant disturbance** in a person's thinking, emotion regulation or behavior that reflects a problem in psychological, biological or developmental functioning. Three ideas help you reason about cases:

- **Dysfunction**: the pattern interferes with daily life, such as school, work, relationships or self-care.
- **Distress**: the person suffers because of it. (Distress is common in disorders but not required in all of them.)
- **Deviance**: the pattern departs from what is typical or culturally expected. Deviance alone is **never enough**: a chess prodigy, a monk who has taken a vow of silence and a protester are all statistically unusual, and cultural norms differ across places and eras.

An expected or culturally approved reaction to a stressful event, such as grief after a death, is not by itself a disorder.

**Worked example 1.** A competitive eater, a person who talks aloud to a deceased grandparent at a family altar during a cultural holiday, and a student who has stopped attending school because she cannot leave her room without overwhelming panic. Only the third shows clear dysfunction and distress; the first two are unusual for some observers but cause no impairment and, in the second case, are normal within the person's culture.

## Classifying disorders: the DSM-5-TR

The **Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR)**, published by the American Psychiatric Association in 2022, lists hundreds of disorders. For each it gives **criteria**: which symptoms, how many, for how long, and what must be ruled out. Outside the United States, clinicians often use the World Health Organization's **ICD-11**, and the two systems are kept broadly compatible.

Three facts about the DSM-5-TR show up on exams:

1. It is **descriptive**: it classifies by **observable symptoms and duration**, not by presumed causes, so clinicians who disagree about theory can still agree on a diagnosis.
2. It does **not prescribe treatment**.
3. It is revised as research changes. For example, DSM-5 moved obsessive-compulsive disorder and posttraumatic stress disorder out of the anxiety disorders into their own groups.

### Reliability and validity of diagnosis

A good diagnostic system must be **reliable** (consistent) and **valid** (accurate). **Inter-rater reliability** asks whether two clinicians who assess the same person independently reach the same diagnosis. **Structured interviews**, which ask everyone the same questions in the same order, raise it. **Validity** asks whether a diagnosis describes a real condition that predicts things such as its course and which treatments help. A diagnosis can be reliable without being valid, but not valid without being reliable.

**Worked example 2: a hypothetical reliability study** (invented for this course). Two clinicians separately diagnosed 50 recorded mock interviews performed by actors.

| Interview type | Interviews | Same diagnosis |
|---|---|---|
| Unstructured | 25 | 15 (60%) |
| Structured | 25 | 22 (88%) |

The structured interview produced more consistent diagnoses. Notice what the data do **not** show: whether the diagnoses were correct (validity). Notice also the ethics: using actors protects real patients' **confidentiality**.

### How common are disorders?

Disorders are common. Federal survey estimates put the share of U.S. adults with a mental illness in a given year at roughly **one in five**. **Comorbidity**, two or more disorders at once, is also common, for example an anxiety disorder with a depressive disorder.

### Culture and diagnosis

Culture shapes how distress is experienced, explained and expressed. The DSM-5-TR describes **cultural concepts of distress**, such as **ataque de nervios** (episodes of intense emotional upset described in some Latin American communities) and **taijin kyofusho** (intense fear of making others uncomfortable, described in Japan). Clinicians must judge symptoms in **cultural context**: hearing a deceased relative's voice during mourning is expected in some traditions.

## Explaining disorders

### The medical model and beyond

The **medical model**, which grew strong in the 1800s and 1900s, treats disorders as illnesses with physical causes that can be diagnosed, treated and often cured. It helped replace older ideas that blamed people or saw disorders as moral failings. But on its own it underplays psychological and social causes.

Today most psychologists use the **biopsychosocial approach**: biological factors (genes, neurotransmitters, brain structure), psychological factors (thought patterns, learned responses, stress, trauma) and social-cultural factors (poverty, discrimination, family conflict, support) all interact.

The **diathesis-stress model** describes one common form of that interaction: a **diathesis** (predisposition, often genetic) combines with **stress** to produce a disorder. Twin studies support it: when one identical twin has a disorder such as schizophrenia, the other twin often does not, even though they share nearly all their genes. Genes load the dice; environment helps decide the roll.

### Seven perspectives on the same symptoms

**Worked example 3.** Mara, 17, has felt persistently sad for two months, has lost interest in soccer and sleeps poorly.

| Perspective | How it would explain Mara's symptoms |
|---|---|
| Biological | A family history of depression; differences in neurotransmitter systems such as serotonin; stress hormones affecting the brain |
| Psychodynamic | Unconscious anger or loss from early relationships turned inward |
| Behavioral | Fewer rewarding activities since an injury, so less positive reinforcement; withdrawal reduces it further |
| Cognitive | Thoughts like "I'm a failure, and it will always be like this": an internal, stable, global explanatory style |
| Humanistic | A gap between her self-concept and her experience, after feeling valued only when she wins |
| Evolutionary | Low mood may once have helped people withdraw and conserve energy after loss |
| Sociocultural | Family financial stress, social media comparison, pressure from cultural expectations |

No single row is the answer. Most clinicians take an **eclectic** approach, drawing on several perspectives to fit the person.

## Labels, stigma and the Rosenhan study

Diagnostic labels help: they give a **shared language** for treatment and research and can bring relief ("other people have this too"). They also carry risks. Labels can **bias perception**, so ordinary behavior is read as a symptom, and they can feed **stigma**: negative stereotypes and discrimination that make people less likely to seek help. Media portrayals and the **availability heuristic** encourage the false belief that people with psychological disorders are usually dangerous; in fact they are more often victims of violence than perpetrators.

In **David Rosenhan's study** (published 1973), eight healthy people each reported briefly hearing voices to gain admission to psychiatric hospitals, then behaved normally. Staff did not detect them, most were discharged with a diagnosis of schizophrenia "in remission", and normal behavior such as note-taking was sometimes read as a symptom. The study raised important questions about how labels shape perception. It also has serious problems:

- **Ethics**: hospital staff were deceived and studied **without informed consent**.
- **Method**: a single false report of a symptom is a weak test of whether diagnosis works, since clinicians reasonably trust patients' reports.
- **Accuracy**: later investigators examining Rosenhan's records found evidence suggesting the study may not have been carried out or reported as described.

Treat it as a historical case that started a debate, not as settled evidence.

### Person-first language

Say **"a person with schizophrenia"**, not "a schizophrenic", and avoid slang such as "psycho" or "so OCD". Person-first language signals that a diagnosis is something a person has, not who they are. Some communities, including many autistic people, prefer identity-first language; the respectful rule is to follow the person's own preference.

**Insanity** is a **legal** term, decided by courts, about whether a person could understand or control their actions when a crime occurred. It is not a DSM-5-TR diagnosis, and the defense is rarely used and rarely succeeds.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating deviance as enough for a disorder.** Look for distress and dysfunction, in cultural context.
- **Saying the DSM-5-TR explains causes or prescribes treatment.** It describes symptoms and duration.
- **Mixing up reliability and validity.** Agreement is reliability; accuracy is validity.
- **Calling insanity a diagnosis.**
- **Reading one perspective as the whole truth.** Use the biopsychosocial approach.
- **Citing Rosenhan without its criticisms.**
