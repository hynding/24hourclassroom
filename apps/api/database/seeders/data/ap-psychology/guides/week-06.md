## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sensation | Detecting physical energy with sense receptors and sending it to the brain. |
| Perception | Organizing and interpreting sensations (Unit 2). |
| Transduction | Converting one form of energy (light, sound waves, chemicals, pressure) into neural signals. |
| Absolute threshold | The weakest stimulus a person can detect half the time. |
| Difference threshold (JND) | The smallest change between two stimuli a person can detect half the time. |
| Weber's law | Two stimuli must differ by a constant proportion, not a constant amount, to be noticed as different. |
| Signal detection theory | Detecting a faint stimulus depends on its strength and on the person's experience, expectations, motivation and alertness. |
| Sensory adaptation | Reduced sensitivity to a constant, unchanging stimulus. |
| Sensory interaction | One sense influencing another, as smell influences taste. |
| Synesthesia | Stimulation of one sense triggers an experience in another, such as seeing colors when hearing music. |

## Thresholds and detection

**Absolute threshold.** Your senses are remarkably sensitive, but each has a minimum. Researchers define the **absolute threshold** as the intensity detected on **50%** of trials, because detection near the limit is not all-or-none.

**Difference threshold and Weber's law.** The **just noticeable difference** grows with the size of the original stimulus. **Weber's law**: the change must be a constant *proportion*. Worked example: suppose a person can just tell 200 g from 204 g (a 2% difference). Then from 600 g they need about 12 g more (2% of 600), not 4 g. One more candle in a dark room is obvious; one more candle in a lit stadium is not.

**Signal detection theory.** Detection is not only about the stimulus. On any trial a faint signal is present or absent, and the person says yes or no, giving four outcomes:

| | Says "yes, present" | Says "no, absent" |
|---|---|---|
| Signal present | Hit | Miss |
| Signal absent | False alarm | Correct rejection |

A person's **response criterion** shifts with motivation and expectations. A lifeguard who expects swimmers in trouble, or a radiologist who fears missing a tumor, says "yes" more readily: more hits but more false alarms. Fatigue lowers hits.

**Adaptation and interaction.** **Sensory adaptation** is why you stop smelling your own house or feeling your watch: receptors fire less to a constant stimulus, freeing attention for change. **Sensory interaction**: with a blocked nose, food tastes flat, because flavor is largely smell. In **synesthesia**, a small minority of people consistently experience one sense through another, such as particular colors for particular letters.

## Vision

Light is electromagnetic energy. Its **wavelength** determines **hue** (color); its **amplitude** (intensity) determines **brightness**.

**The path of light.** Light passes through the **cornea** (which bends it), the **pupil** (an opening whose size the **iris** adjusts), and the **lens**, which changes shape to focus near or far (**accommodation**), onto the **retina** at the back of the eye. If the eye focuses the image in front of the retina, distant objects blur (**nearsightedness**); if behind it, near objects blur (**farsightedness**).

**The retina.** **Transduction** happens in two kinds of receptors:

| | Rods | Cones |
|---|---|---|
| Number | About 120 million per eye | About 6 million per eye |
| Location | Mostly the periphery | Concentrated in the fovea, the center of focus |
| Best for | Dim light, motion, black, white and gray | Color and fine detail in good light |

Rods and cones signal **bipolar cells**, which signal **ganglion cells**, whose axons bundle into the **optic nerve**. Where the nerve leaves the eye there are no receptors: the **blind spot**. You do not notice it because the other eye and the brain fill in the gap. Signals travel through the thalamus to the visual cortex in the occipital lobe.

**Color vision: two theories, two stages.**

- **Trichromatic theory** (Young and Helmholtz): the retina has three kinds of cones, most sensitive to short (blue), medium (green) and long (red) wavelengths. Every color comes from their combined activity.
- **Opponent-process theory** (Hering): after the cones, cells work in opposing pairs (red–green, blue–yellow, black–white); exciting one member inhibits the other. This explains **afterimages**: stare at a green shape, then look at white, and you see red as the fatigued green response rebounds.

Both are right, at different stages: trichromatic at the cones, opponent-process further along.

**When vision differs.** In **color vision deficiency**, one or more cone types are missing or work differently. **Dichromatism** (two working cone types) most often causes red–green confusion and is far more common in males because the relevant genes are on the X chromosome. **Monochromatism**, very rare, means seeing no color at all. **Prosopagnosia** (face blindness) is an inability to recognize faces, even familiar ones, despite normal eyesight. **Blindsight** describes people with damage to the visual cortex who say they see nothing in part of their visual field yet can point to or avoid objects there above chance, showing that some visual processing happens outside awareness.

## Hearing

Sound waves are vibrations in the air. **Frequency** (waves per second, in hertz) determines **pitch**; **amplitude** determines **loudness**, measured in **decibels**. Long, unprotected exposure to sound around 85 decibels and above can damage hearing: about 8 hours a day at 85 decibels is the usual safety limit, and the safe time halves with every 3-decibel rise.

**The path of sound.** The outer ear funnels sound to the **eardrum**, which vibrates three tiny bones of the middle ear (hammer, anvil and stirrup). The stirrup presses on the oval window of the **cochlea**, a fluid-filled, snail-shaped tube in the inner ear. Waves in the fluid ripple the **basilar membrane**, bending **hair cells**, the receptors that transduce vibration into neural signals carried by the **auditory nerve** (through the thalamus) to the auditory cortex in the temporal lobe.

**Pitch theories.**

- **Place theory:** different pitches vibrate different places on the basilar membrane. It explains **high** pitches well; high frequencies peak near the cochlea's entrance.
- **Frequency theory:** the whole membrane vibrates at the sound's frequency, and the auditory nerve fires at the same rate. It explains **low** pitches.
- **Volley theory:** a single neuron cannot fire more than about 1,000 times a second, so groups of neurons take turns (volleys) to code frequencies above that rate.

**Locating sound.** A sound to your right reaches the right ear slightly sooner and slightly louder. The brain uses those tiny differences to find its direction; a sound directly ahead or behind is harder to place.

**Hearing loss.** **Conductive hearing loss** is a mechanical problem in the outer or middle ear (a damaged eardrum or stiff middle-ear bones); hearing aids or surgery often help. **Sensorineural hearing loss** is damage to the hair cells or auditory nerve, from aging, disease or loud noise; hair cells do not regrow in humans. A **cochlear implant** converts sound into electrical signals that stimulate the auditory nerve directly.

## The chemical senses, touch and the body senses

**Taste (gustation).** Taste receptor cells, clustered in taste buds, detect five basic tastes: **sweet, salty, sour, bitter** and **umami** (a savory taste). Taste receptors are replaced every week or two. People vary: **supertasters** have more taste buds and find bitter foods intense; **nontasters** have fewer; most people are **medium tasters**.

**Smell (olfaction).** Receptor cells high in the nasal cavity detect airborne molecules; combinations of receptors identify thousands of odors. Smell is the one sense whose main pathway does not pass through the thalamus first; they reach brain areas tied to emotion and memory, which is why a smell can bring back a vivid memory.

**Touch and pain.** Skin receptors detect **pressure, warmth, cold** and **pain**. Pain is a warning system, and it depends on more than tissue damage. **Gate-control theory** (Melzack and Wall, 1965) proposes a neural "gate" in the spinal cord: small fibers carrying pain signals open it, while activity in larger fibers (rubbing a bumped elbow) and messages from the brain (distraction, calm) can close it. **Phantom limb** sensations, including pain, are often reported after an amputation, because the brain areas that represented the limb keep generating experiences without input from the body.

**Body position and balance.** **Kinesthesis** is the sense of the position and movement of your body parts, from receptors in muscles, tendons and joints; it lets you touch your nose with your eyes closed. The **vestibular sense** monitors head position and movement for balance, through the fluid-filled **semicircular canals** and vestibular sacs of the inner ear. Spinning makes you dizzy because the fluid keeps moving after you stop.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing up sensation and perception.** Detecting is sensation; interpreting is perception.
- **Saying the JND is a fixed amount.** It is a constant proportion (Weber's law).
- **Swapping rods and cones.** Cones: **c**olor and **c**enter (fovea). Rods: dim light and periphery.
- **Treating the color theories as rivals.** Trichromatic works at the cones; opponent-process works later.
- **Mixing up place and frequency theory.** Place: high pitch. Frequency: low pitch.
- **Mixing up the hearing losses.** Conductive: mechanical, outer or middle ear. Sensorineural: hair cells or nerve.

## Self-check

1. What is transduction in the ear, and which cells do it?
2. If the JND for a 50-pound weight is 1 pound, about what is it for a 150-pound weight?
3. You stare at a yellow square, then look at a white wall. What color is the afterimage, and which theory explains it?
4. Why do you see faint stars better when you look slightly to the side of them?
5. A lifeguard is told a child may be in trouble. How do her hits and false alarms change?
6. Which sense tells you that the elevator has started to move, even with your eyes closed?

*Answers:* 1. Converting fluid vibrations into neural signals; hair cells on the basilar membrane. 2. About 3 pounds (the same 2% proportion). 3. Blue; opponent-process theory. 4. The image falls on the periphery of the retina, where the rods, which work in dim light, are concentrated. 5. She says "yes" more readily: more hits and more false alarms. 6. The vestibular sense.
