## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Neuron | A nerve cell: the basic unit that sends and receives messages. |
| Glial cells | Support cells that nourish, protect and insulate neurons (some form myelin). |
| Dendrites | Branching fibers that receive messages from other neurons. |
| Cell body (soma) | Contains the nucleus and keeps the cell alive; combines incoming signals. |
| Axon | The long fiber that carries the action potential away from the cell body. |
| Myelin sheath | Fatty insulation on many axons that speeds transmission. |
| Terminal buttons | Knobs at the axon's end that release neurotransmitters. |
| Synapse | The tiny gap between one neuron's terminal and the next cell. |
| Action potential | A brief electrical charge that travels down the axon. |
| Threshold | The level of stimulation needed to trigger an action potential. |
| Reuptake | The sending neuron reabsorbing leftover neurotransmitter from the synapse. |
| Agonist / antagonist | A substance that mimics or strengthens / blocks or weakens a neurotransmitter's action. |

## The neuron and the reflex arc

A message enters a neuron at the **dendrites**, is combined in the **cell body**, travels down the **axon**, and leaves from the **terminal buttons**. **Myelin**, made by glial cells, wraps many axons in segments; the signal jumps between the gaps, which makes it much faster. **Glial cells**, roughly as numerous as neurons, also supply nutrients, clean up debris and help form connections.

Three kinds of neurons cooperate:

- **Sensory (afferent) neurons** carry information from sense receptors toward the central nervous system.
- **Interneurons**, inside the brain and spinal cord, connect neurons to each other and do the processing.
- **Motor (efferent) neurons** carry commands out to muscles and glands.

A **reflex arc** is the shortest route: touch a hot pan and a sensory neuron carries the signal to the spinal cord, where an interneuron passes it straight to a motor neuron that pulls your hand back. Other interneurons forward the message up to the brain, so you feel the pain a moment *after* the movement has started.

## Neural firing

At rest, the inside of a neuron is negatively charged relative to the outside (about −70 millivolts), the **resting potential**, because of the uneven spread of charged particles (ions). Signals from other neurons nudge this charge up or down. If the combined input pushes the neuron past its **threshold**, gates in the axon membrane open and positively charged **sodium ions** rush in. This **depolarization** is the **action potential**, and it moves down the axon like a row of falling dominoes. Potassium ions then flow out, restoring the negative charge. During the brief **refractory period** that follows, the neuron cannot fire again.

Two consequences matter on the exam:

- **All-or-nothing.** A neuron either fires at full strength or not at all. A stronger stimulus does not make a bigger action potential.
- **Intensity is coded by rate and number.** A strong stimulus makes a neuron fire *more often* and recruits *more* neurons.

**When the wiring fails.** In **multiple sclerosis**, the immune system attacks myelin in the brain and spinal cord; signals slow or stall, causing problems such as weakness, numbness and vision changes. In **myasthenia gravis**, the immune system interferes with the receptors for acetylcholine where nerves meet muscles, so muscles weaken and tire quickly.

## The synapse and neurotransmitters

The action potential is electrical **within** a neuron; communication **between** neurons is chemical. At the terminal buttons the action potential releases **neurotransmitters** into the synapse. They bind to receptor sites on the receiving neuron like keys in locks. **Excitatory** neurotransmitters make the receiving neuron more likely to fire; **inhibitory** ones make it less likely. Leftover molecules are broken down or taken back by **reuptake**.

| Neurotransmitter | Main roles | Links worth knowing |
|---|---|---|
| Acetylcholine | Muscle movement; attention and memory | Lost neurons in Alzheimer's disease; myasthenia gravis |
| Dopamine | Movement, motivation, reward | Too little in Parkinson's disease; excess activity linked to schizophrenia |
| Serotonin | Mood, sleep, appetite | Low activity linked to depression |
| Norepinephrine | Alertness and arousal | Part of the sympathetic response |
| Glutamate | The main excitatory neurotransmitter; learning and memory | Excess can overstimulate the brain (linked to migraines and seizures) |
| GABA | The main inhibitory neurotransmitter | Low activity linked to seizures, anxiety and insomnia |
| Endorphins | Natural pain relief and pleasure | Released during strenuous exercise or injury |
| Substance P | Transmits pain signals | Endorphins can block its effect |

"Linked to" means correlated in research, not the single cause. Depression, for example, involves far more than serotonin.

## Hormones: the slower messengers

The **endocrine system** sends **hormones** through the bloodstream. They act more slowly than neurotransmitters but their effects last longer. The **pituitary gland**, directed by the hypothalamus, regulates many other glands.

| Hormone | Made by | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Adrenaline (epinephrine) | Adrenal glands | Prolongs the sympathetic fight-or-flight response |
| Leptin | Fat cells | Signals fullness; lowers hunger over time |
| Ghrelin | Stomach | Signals hunger |
| Melatonin | Pineal gland | Rises in darkness and promotes sleep (week 5) |
| Oxytocin | Made in the hypothalamus, released by the pituitary | Social bonding and trust; childbirth and nursing |

## Psychoactive drugs

**Psychoactive drugs** change perception, mood or behavior by acting at synapses:

- An **agonist** mimics a neurotransmitter or increases its effect. Opioids such as morphine act on endorphin receptors.
- An **antagonist** blocks receptors. Some antipsychotic medications block dopamine receptors.
- A **reuptake inhibitor** keeps a neurotransmitter in the synapse longer. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), a common antidepressant class, do this for serotonin; cocaine blocks the reuptake of dopamine and other neurotransmitters.

| Class | Effect | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Stimulants | Speed up neural activity: alertness, energy, raised heart rate | Caffeine, nicotine, cocaine, amphetamines |
| Depressants | Slow neural activity: relaxation, slowed reactions | Alcohol (boosts GABA's effect), sedatives |
| Hallucinogens | Distort perception and can cause sensory images without input | LSD, psilocybin; marijuana (THC) is usually grouped here, though its effects are mixed (it also relaxes, like a depressant) |
| Opioids | Relieve pain and produce euphoria; slow breathing | Morphine, heroin, fentanyl |

With repeated use the brain adapts. **Tolerance** means a user needs more of the drug for the same effect. **Withdrawal** is the discomfort and distress that follow stopping. **Addiction** (in clinical terms, a substance use disorder) is compulsive use despite harm. Combining depressants with opioids (or with each other) is especially dangerous because each slows breathing.

**Worked example: a hypothetical caffeine experiment.** A research team (invented for this course) randomly assigns 60 adult volunteers to drink a coffee containing 200 mg of caffeine or an identical decaffeinated coffee (placebo); neither the volunteers nor the tester know which. Thirty minutes later, reaction time is measured as the average time in milliseconds to press a key when a light appears, over 50 trials.

| Condition | Mean reaction time (ms) | SD (ms) |
|---|---|---|
| Caffeine | 268 | 22 |
| Placebo | 289 | 25 |

Caffeine, a stimulant, is linked to faster reactions: 21 ms quicker on average. The design is **double-blind** with a **placebo**, so expectations and experimenter bias are controlled, and random assignment supports a causal conclusion if the difference is statistically significant. The sample is adults only, so generalizing to teens needs caution.

## Common mistakes

- **"A stronger stimulus makes a stronger action potential."** It changes the firing rate, not the size.
- **Reversing the order.** Dendrites receive → cell body → axon → terminal buttons → synapse.
- **Confusing hormones with neurotransmitters.** Hormones travel in blood and act slowly; neurotransmitters cross a synapse in a fraction of a second.
- **Mixing up leptin and ghrelin.** **Gh**relin **g**rowls (hunger); leptin says enough.
- **Calling every drug that affects a neurotransmitter an agonist.** Blocking a receptor is antagonism; slowing reuptake is a third mechanism.

## Self-check

1. Which part of a neuron receives messages, and which part sends them along?
2. Why do a light touch and a hard press feel different if action potentials are all-or-nothing?
3. A drug occupies acetylcholine receptors without activating them. Agonist or antagonist? Predict one effect.
4. Which hormone signals hunger, and which signals fullness?
5. Order the reflex arc: interneuron, motor neuron, sensory neuron.
6. What is tolerance?

*Answers:* 1. Dendrites receive; the axon carries the signal away. 2. The hard press makes neurons fire more often and recruits more neurons. 3. Antagonist; muscle weakness, because acetylcholine triggers muscle contraction. 4. Ghrelin signals hunger; leptin signals fullness. 5. Sensory neuron → interneuron → motor neuron. 6. Needing more of a drug to get the same effect after repeated use.
