## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Concept | A mental grouping of similar objects, events, ideas or people. |
| Prototype | The best, most typical example of a concept. |
| Schema | An organized framework of knowledge that guides interpretation. |
| Assimilation / accommodation | Fitting new information into an existing schema / changing a schema to fit new information. |
| Executive functions | Higher-order skills for planning, inhibiting impulses, shifting attention and holding goals in mind. |
| Algorithm | A step-by-step procedure that guarantees a solution, if slowly. |
| Heuristic | A mental shortcut that is fast but can lead to error. |
| Mental set | A tendency to approach a problem in a way that worked before. |
| Functional fixedness | Thinking of objects only in terms of their usual function. |
| Confirmation bias | Seeking and favoring information that supports what you already believe. |
| Framing | How the wording or presentation of a choice changes the decision. |
| Divergent / convergent thinking | Generating many possible answers / narrowing to the single best answer. |

## Concepts, prototypes and schemas

The mind saves effort by grouping. A **concept** is a mental category: "bird", "fruit", "fair". We decide whether something belongs by comparing it with a **prototype**, the most typical member. A robin matches most people's bird prototype, so "Is a robin a bird?" is answered faster than "Is a penguin a bird?" Items far from the prototype are categorized more slowly and less confidently.

**Schemas** are bigger frameworks: what a restaurant visit involves, what a scientist is like. When new information fits, we **assimilate** it: a child who calls every four-legged animal "doggy" is assimilating a cow into her dog schema. When it does not fit, we **accommodate**: she builds a new "cow" schema. These terms come from Jean Piaget's theory of development, which returns in Unit 3.

## Executive functions

**Executive functions** are the brain's management system: planning steps toward a goal, holding information in mind while working on it, inhibiting an automatic response, and switching between tasks. They depend heavily on the **prefrontal cortex**, the front part of the frontal lobes, which keeps maturing into the mid-twenties. A teen who knows the test is tomorrow but keeps scrolling is showing the limits of a still-developing system for inhibiting an attractive impulse.

## Solving problems: algorithms, heuristics and insight

An **algorithm** is a methodical, step-by-step procedure that guarantees a solution: trying every combination on a three-digit lock will open it eventually (up to 1,000 tries). A **heuristic** is a shortcut: trying birthdays first. Heuristics are usually good enough and much faster, which is why we rely on them, but they also cause predictable errors. Sometimes a solution arrives suddenly after a period of being stuck, an experience called **insight**.

Two heuristics described by Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman in the 1970s are on every AP exam:

- **Availability heuristic.** Judging how likely something is by how easily examples come to mind. After news coverage of a plane crash, people overestimate the danger of flying, because vivid crash stories are easy to recall while ordinary safe flights are not.
- **Representativeness heuristic.** Judging how likely something is by how well it matches a prototype, while ignoring base rates. Told that a quiet person loves poetry, people guess "classics professor" over "truck driver", even though there are vastly more truck drivers.

**Worked example 1: naming the heuristic.** After a friend's phone is stolen at the mall, Sam decides the mall is the most dangerous place in town. One vivid, recent example is easy to recall, so Sam judges theft there as common: **availability**. Later Sam assumes a classmate in a band T-shirt must play guitar, because he matches the musician prototype: **representativeness**.

## Obstacles to good thinking

- **Mental set.** In Abraham Luchins's water-jar studies in the 1940s, people who had solved several problems with a long formula kept using it even when a simpler method would work. A strategy that worked before becomes a rut.
- **Functional fixedness.** In Karl Duncker's candle problem, people asked to fix a candle to a wall using a box of tacks often missed that the box itself could serve as a shelf. Seeing the box only as a container blocked the solution.
- **Confirmation bias.** People search for evidence that supports their beliefs and ignore what would disconfirm them. In Peter Wason's number-rule task, participants guessing the rule behind "2, 4, 6" mostly tested examples that fit their guess rather than examples that could prove it wrong.
- **Priming.** Earlier exposure to a stimulus makes related ideas more accessible, often without awareness, which shapes later judgments.
- **Framing.** The same choice described differently leads to different decisions. A surgery with a "90% survival rate" is chosen more often than one with a "10% death rate", though the two are the same.
- **Gambler's fallacy.** Believing that past random events change the odds of future ones: after five coin tosses come up heads, believing tails is "due". Each toss is still 50–50.
- **Sunk-cost fallacy.** Continuing to invest in something because of what has already been spent, rather than what lies ahead. Staying through a terrible movie because the ticket cost $15 is the classic case.

Overconfidence (being more certain than accuracy warrants) and belief perseverance (clinging to a belief after the evidence for it is discredited) make these biases harder to correct.

**Worked example 2: sorting the errors.** (a) Priya keeps paying for a guitar course she hates because she has already paid for six months: sunk cost. (b) A coach keeps running the play that worked last season against a team that has learned to stop it: mental set. (c) A voter reads only news that praises his candidate: confirmation bias. (d) A shopper buys "80% lean" beef rather than "20% fat" beef: framing.

## Creativity

**Creativity** is the ability to produce ideas that are both novel and useful. **Divergent thinking** generates many possible answers ("List every use for a paper clip"); **convergent thinking** narrows options to one best answer, as on most multiple-choice tests. Creative work uses both: diverge to produce options, converge to choose. Expertise, a tolerance for ambiguity, intrinsic motivation and an environment that supports risk-taking all help.

## Research methods spotlight: studying framing

**Worked example 3: a framing experiment.** A hypothetical class study randomly assigns 120 students to read one of two descriptions of a new school-lunch program. Version A says "70% of students who tried it liked it"; version B says "30% of students who tried it disliked it". Students then answer yes or no: "Should the school adopt the program?"

| Version | Said yes | Said no | Proportion yes |
|---|---|---|---|
| A (positive frame) | 46 | 14 | 46/60 = 0.77 |
| B (negative frame) | 29 | 31 | 29/60 = 0.48 |

- *IV:* the frame (positive or negative wording). *DV:* support for the program, operationally defined as answering yes.
- *Interpretation:* the two descriptions contain identical information, yet support was about 28 percentage points higher (77% against 48%) with the positive frame. That is framing.
- *Why random assignment matters:* students who already like school lunches are equally likely to land in either group, so prior attitudes cannot explain the gap.
- *Significance and effect size:* a statistical test would show whether a gap this large is unlikely by chance. A difference of about 28 points is also a large effect, which is a separate question from significance.
- *Generalizability:* the sample is students at one school; the effect might be smaller or larger with adults or for more important decisions.

**Worked example 4: a correlation, not an experiment.** An invented survey of 200 adults finds that the number of hours per week people spend watching crime news correlates r = +0.41 with their estimate of the local crime rate. The positive correlation fits the availability heuristic (vivid stories make crime easy to recall), but it does not prove that watching causes the high estimates: people who already fear crime may choose to watch more crime news.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing availability with representativeness.** Availability is about *ease of recall*; representativeness is about *resemblance to a prototype*.
- **Calling any stuck feeling functional fixedness.** Functional fixedness is about an *object's* usual use; mental set is about a *strategy* that worked before.
- **Thinking heuristics are always bad.** They are efficient and usually right; the AP exam asks about the errors they cause.
- **Reversing assimilation and accommodation.** Assimilate = fit it into the old schema; accommodate = change the schema.
