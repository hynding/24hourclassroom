## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Operant conditioning | Learning in which a behavior becomes more or less likely depending on its consequences. |
| Law of effect | Behaviors followed by satisfying outcomes become more likely; those followed by unpleasant outcomes, less likely. |
| Reinforcement | Any consequence that increases the behavior it follows. |
| Punishment | Any consequence that decreases the behavior it follows. |
| Positive / negative | Something is added / something is removed. |
| Shaping | Reinforcing successive approximations of a target behavior. |
| Reinforcement schedule | The rule for which responses are reinforced. |
| Learned helplessness | Passive giving-up after repeated experience of uncontrollable aversive events. |

## From Thorndike to Skinner (3.8)

Around 1900, Edward Thorndike put hungry cats in **puzzle boxes** that opened when the cat pressed a lever or pulled a loop. At first the cats escaped by chance; over trials they escaped faster and faster. Thorndike summarized this as the **law of effect**: behaviors followed by satisfying consequences are "stamped in," and those followed by unpleasant consequences are "stamped out."

B. F. Skinner built on this with the **operant chamber** (often called a Skinner box): a box in which an animal, such as a rat or pigeon, can press a bar or peck a key to receive food or water, while a device records each response over time. Skinner called voluntary behaviors that act on the environment **operants**. Unlike classical conditioning, where responses are automatic and triggered by a preceding stimulus, operant conditioning concerns behavior controlled by its **consequences**.

## Reinforcement and punishment (3.8)

The two words that cause the most confusion have simple meanings:

- **Reinforcement** always **increases** a behavior; **punishment** always **decreases** it.
- **Positive** means a stimulus is **added**; **negative** means a stimulus is **taken away**. Neither word means good or bad.

| | Stimulus added | Stimulus removed |
|---|---|---|
| Behavior increases | **Positive reinforcement**: add something desirable (praise after homework) | **Negative reinforcement**: remove something aversive (the seatbelt alarm stops when you buckle up) |
| Behavior decreases | **Positive punishment**: add something aversive (a scolding for teasing) | **Negative punishment**: remove something desirable (losing phone time; a time-out from play) |

**How to classify any scenario.** Ask: (1) Did the behavior become more or less likely afterward? (2) Was something added or taken away? Combine the answers.

**Worked example 1.** A student who has a headache takes a pain reliever; the headache goes away, and next time she takes one sooner. Behavior (taking the pill) increases; something aversive (pain) is removed: **negative reinforcement**. Negative reinforcement includes **escape** (ending an aversive event already happening) and **avoidance** (acting to prevent it, such as leaving early to avoid traffic).

**Types of reinforcers.**

- **Primary reinforcers** satisfy biological needs without learning: food, water, warmth, relief from pain.
- **Secondary (conditioned) reinforcers** gain their power through association with primary reinforcers: praise, grades, a clicker sound in animal training. They are learned by classical conditioning (week 18).
- **Generalized reinforcers** are secondary reinforcers linked with many primary ones, such as money.
- A **token economy** gives tokens (points, stars, chips) for target behaviors, which can be exchanged for rewards; it is used in classrooms, hospitals and residential programs.

**Timing.** Reinforcement works best when it is **immediate**. People often choose a small reward now over a larger one later (delay discounting), which is one reason long-term goals such as saving money are hard.

**Reward circuits.** Reinforcement engages the brain's dopamine pathways (1.3), which signal that an outcome was better than expected.

## Building behavior (3.8)

**Shaping** teaches behaviors an animal or person would rarely produce on their own by reinforcing **successive approximations**. To shape a rat to press a bar, reinforce turning toward the bar, then approaching it, then touching it, then pressing it. Animal trainers, speech therapists and parents all shape behavior this way.

**Chaining** links several behaviors into a sequence, each step reinforced by the chance to do the next, ending in a primary reinforcer.

**Discriminative stimuli** signal when a behavior will be reinforced. A pigeon reinforced for pecking only when a green light is on learns to peck in green and not in red; you learn to ask a parent for a favor when they are in a good mood.

**Generalization and discrimination** work as in week 18, but now about when a **behavior** pays off.

**Operant extinction** is the fading of a behavior that is no longer reinforced. Often the behavior first **increases** in an **extinction burst** before it declines: a vending machine that stops giving snacks gets pressed harder and faster before you give up. **Spontaneous recovery** can occur here too.

## Schedules of reinforcement (3.8)

With **continuous reinforcement** every response is reinforced. Learning is fastest, but extinction is also fast once reinforcement stops. With **partial (intermittent) reinforcement** only some responses are reinforced; learning is slower but far more **resistant to extinction** (the partial reinforcement effect).

| Schedule | Rule | Example | Typical response pattern |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed ratio (FR) | Reinforce after a set number of responses | A free coffee after every 10 purchases | High rate with a brief pause after each reinforcer |
| Variable ratio (VR) | Reinforce after an unpredictable number of responses | Slot machines; fishing | Highest, steadiest rate; most resistant to extinction |
| Fixed interval (FI) | Reinforce the first response after a set time | Checking the oven near the end of baking time; studying before a scheduled test | Responding speeds up as the time nears, then drops after reinforcement (a "scalloped" pattern) |
| Variable interval (VI) | Reinforce the first response after an unpredictable time | Checking for a text from a friend; pop quizzes | Slow, steady responding |

**Memory aid.** *Ratio* depends on the **number** of responses, so responding faster pays; *interval* depends on **time**. *Variable* schedules produce steadier responding because the next reinforcer could come at any time.

**Worked example 2: reading a cumulative record.** In a hypothetical lab, four pigeons each work on one schedule for an hour, and the cumulative number of pecks is recorded every 10 minutes. The VR pigeon's line is the steepest and almost straight (about 900 pecks per 10 minutes); the FR pigeon's line is steep with small flat steps after each reinforcer; the FI pigeon's line shows a curve that is flat after each reinforcer and steepens as the next interval ends; the VI pigeon's line is straight but shallow. The slope of each line is the response rate, so comparing slopes compares schedules.

## Limits of punishment (3.8)

Punishment can stop a behavior quickly, especially if it is **immediate, consistent** and paired with reinforcement for a better behavior. But it has well-documented drawbacks:

- It **suppresses** a behavior without teaching what to do instead, and the behavior may return when the punisher is absent (the child learns to tease only when no adult is around).
- It can produce **fear and anxiety** linked with the punisher or the setting (classical conditioning).
- Physical punishment **models aggression** (week 20) and is associated with more aggression and behavior problems in children, though most of this evidence is correlational.

Most psychologists recommend reinforcing desired behavior and using mild negative punishment (time-out, loss of privileges) over harsh positive punishment.

## Biology and cognition again (3.8)

- **Instinctive drift**: Keller and Marian Breland, former students of Skinner, found that trained animals tend to slide back toward instinctive behaviors. Raccoons trained to drop coins in a box began rubbing the coins together, as they would wash food, even though this delayed their reward.
- **Superstitious behavior**: when reinforcement happens by chance, whatever the organism was doing at the time can be strengthened. Skinner described pigeons fed at fixed times that developed odd rituals, like turning in circles, that had nothing to do with the food. Lucky socks and pre-game rituals follow the same pattern.
- **Learned helplessness**: in experiments in the 1960s, Martin Seligman and colleagues found that dogs that had first experienced shocks they could not escape later failed to escape shocks they could easily avoid; they had learned that their actions did not matter. Seligman proposed this as a model for some forms of depression (Unit 5). Today such studies on animals would face strict ethical review.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading "negative reinforcement" as punishment.** Negative reinforcement increases behavior by removing something unpleasant.
- **Classifying by how the event feels.** Classify by what happens to the behavior and whether a stimulus was added or removed.
- **Mixing ratio and interval.** Ratio = number of responses; interval = time.
- **Saying continuous reinforcement is most resistant to extinction.** It is the least; variable ratio is the most.
- **Confusing shaping with chaining.** Shaping builds one new behavior step by step; chaining links behaviors into a sequence.

## Self-check

1. A teen's parents stop nagging once he takes out the trash, and he takes it out more often. Classify the consequence.
2. A toddler touches a hot pan, feels a painful sting, and stops reaching for pans. Classify the consequence.
3. Which schedule does a salesperson paid a bonus for every fifth sale work under?
4. Why is gambling so resistant to extinction?
5. A dog is rewarded first for looking at a ball, then for nosing it, then for picking it up. What process is this?
6. Why might punishment fail to change a behavior permanently?

*Answers:* 1. Negative reinforcement. 2. Positive punishment: pain is added and the behavior decreases. 3. Fixed ratio. 4. It runs on a variable ratio schedule, so any response might be the one that pays. 5. Shaping. 6. It suppresses the behavior without teaching an alternative, so the behavior may return when the punisher is absent.
