## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Consolidation | The process by which new memories become stable and lasting. |
| Long-term potentiation (LTP) | Lasting strengthening of a synapse after repeated stimulation. |
| Anterograde amnesia | Inability to form new long-term memories after an injury or illness. |
| Retrograde amnesia | Loss of memories formed before an injury or illness. |
| Infantile amnesia | The inability of adults to recall events from roughly the first three years of life. |
| Superior autobiographical memory | A rare ability to recall the events of almost every day of one's life in detail. |
| Recall | Retrieving information without the answer in front of you (fill-in, essay). |
| Recognition | Identifying information as previously learned (multiple choice). |
| Retrieval cue | A stimulus that helps bring a memory back. |
| Encoding specificity | Cues present at encoding are the most effective cues at retrieval. |
| Testing effect | Better retention from actively retrieving information than from restudying it. |
| Metacognition | Thinking about one's own thinking, including judging what one knows. |

## Storage: how memories last

A new memory is fragile at first. **Consolidation** makes it stable. Over hours to years, the **hippocampus** helps reorganize new explicit memories and shift them into lasting storage across the cortex, which is why damage to the hippocampus prevents new memories but often spares old ones. Much of this reorganization happens during **sleep** (week 5).

At the level of neurons, memory is a change in connections. **Long-term potentiation (LTP)** is the lasting increase in a synapse's efficiency after repeated stimulation: the sending neuron releases its neurotransmitter more readily, and the receiving neuron becomes more responsive, sometimes by adding receptor sites. In animal studies, drugs that block LTP interfere with learning, and drugs that enhance it can speed learning. The neurotransmitter **glutamate** is central to LTP; **acetylcholine** pathways also support memory, and their loss is an early feature of **Alzheimer's disease**.

Emotion strengthens storage: stress hormones and **amygdala** activity during an emotional event make it more likely to be consolidated, which is why you remember where you were when you heard big news. But vivid does not mean accurate, as week 12 shows.

## When storage fails: amnesia

| Type | What is lost | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Anterograde amnesia | The ability to form new long-term memories after the damage. | Henry Molaison (week 9) after his 1953 surgery. |
| Retrograde amnesia | Memories from before the damage, often the most recent ones. | A person after a head injury who cannot recall the hour before a car crash. |

Retrograde amnesia usually hits the most recent memories hardest, because they had not yet been fully consolidated. Some people have both forms.

**Infantile amnesia** is universal: adults recall almost nothing from before about age three or four. Likely reasons: the hippocampus and frontal lobes are still maturing, young children lack the language to encode events in words, and their sense of self is not yet developed enough to organize autobiographical memories.

**Alzheimer's disease** is a progressive brain disease that destroys neurons, starting in areas including the hippocampus. Early on, people have trouble forming new memories; as the disease progresses, older memories and other abilities fade. Loss of acetylcholine-producing neurons is part of the picture.

At the opposite extreme, a small number of people have **superior autobiographical memory** (also called hyperthymesia): asked about a random date, they can say what day of the week it was and what they did. Their memory for their own lives is remarkable, but they are not generally better at memorizing word lists, which shows that autobiographical memory is a distinct system.

## Retrieval: getting it back

Psychologists measure retrieval three ways:

- **Recall**: producing information with no answer to choose from ("Name the four lobes of the cortex").
- **Recognition**: identifying information you have seen before ("Which of these is a lobe?"). Recognition is usually easier because the options themselves are cues.
- **Relearning**: learning something a second time takes less time than the first, revealing memory that recall and recognition missed. Ebbinghaus used this "savings" measure.

**Retrieval cues** are the hooks that pull memories out. Memories are stored in webs of associations, so activating one strand (a smell, a word, a place) can **prime** related memories, often without awareness.

**Encoding specificity** is the principle that the best cues are those present when the memory was formed:

- **Context-dependent memory**: recall is better in the same place or setting where you learned. In a well-known 1975 study by Duncan Godden and Alan Baddeley, divers who learned words underwater recalled them better underwater, and those who learned on land recalled them better on land.
- **State-dependent memory**: recall is better when you are in the same internal state (for example, calm or caffeinated) as during learning.
- **Mood-congruent memory**: people tend to recall memories that match their current mood. A sad mood makes sad memories more available, which can deepen a low mood, a point that returns in Unit 5.

**Worked example 1: naming the effect.** (a) Walking back into the kitchen helps you remember why you went there: context-dependent memory. (b) A student who studied after coffee does better if she also has coffee before the test: state-dependent memory. (c) After a fight with a friend, you can think only of the times she let you down: mood-congruent memory.

## Using retrieval to learn

The **testing effect** (or retrieval practice effect) is the finding that actively retrieving information strengthens memory more than restudying it. Taking practice quizzes, covering your notes and explaining the material aloud, and using flashcards that make you produce the answer before flipping are all retrieval practice. Combined with week 10's spacing, it is one of the best-supported study strategies.

**Metacognition** is awareness of your own learning. People often feel they know material after rereading it, because the text seems familiar (a recognition feeling), and then fail to recall it on a test. Testing yourself gives honest feedback about what you know. The **serial position effect** (week 10) is a reminder to give extra attention to the middle of a list or chapter.

## Research methods spotlight: testing context-dependent memory

**Worked example 2: a two-by-two design.** In a hypothetical experiment, 80 students learn 30 terms either in the library or in the gym, and are tested either in the same room or in the other room (20 students per combination, randomly assigned).

| Learned in | Tested in | Mean terms recalled (of 30) |
|---|---|---|
| Library | Library | 18.4 |
| Library | Gym | 13.1 |
| Gym | Gym | 17.9 |
| Gym | Library | 12.6 |

- *Independent variables:* learning room, and whether the test room matched it. *DV:* terms recalled.
- *Result:* students in matching rooms recalled about 5 more terms on average, whichever room they learned in, as context-dependent memory predicts.
- *Why four groups:* if everyone learned in the library, "the gym is distracting" could explain the results. Crossing both rooms shows the match itself matters.
- *Ethics:* minimal risk, but students still give informed consent, can withdraw, and are debriefed.

**Worked example 3: recall versus recognition data.** An invented class demonstration gives 25 students a 20-word list. The next day they first write all the words they recall (mean 6.2) and then pick the list words out of 40 (mean 15.8). Recognition far exceeds recall: much more was stored than recall alone revealed. Because every student did recall first, an order effect is possible: the recall attempt itself may have strengthened the words (a testing effect). A better design would counterbalance or use separate groups.

## Common mistakes

- **Swapping anterograde and retrograde.** Antero- = forward: no new memories from now on. Retro- = backward: losing the past.
- **Calling infantile amnesia a disorder.** It is a normal feature of human memory.
- **Mixing context- and state-dependent.** Context is the external setting; state is the internal condition.
- **Treating rereading as testing.** Recognizing your notes as familiar is not the same as retrieving them.

## Self-check

1. After a concussion, a player cannot remember the plays before the hit. Which amnesia?
2. Why is a multiple-choice test usually easier than a fill-in test on the same material?
3. A student studies in her bedroom, then takes the test in the same bedroom online. Which effect could help her?
4. Why do you remember little from age two?
5. Which strategy uses the testing effect: highlighting or self-quizzing?
6. In worked example 2, why include a group that learned in the gym?

*Answers:* 1. Retrograde amnesia. 2. Recognition provides cues; recall must generate the answer. 3. Context-dependent memory. 4. Infantile amnesia: an immature hippocampus, limited language and a developing sense of self. 5. Self-quizzing. 6. To rule out the gym being a worse place to be tested in; crossing the rooms shows that matching is what helps.
