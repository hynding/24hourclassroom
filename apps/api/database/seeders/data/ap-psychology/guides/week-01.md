## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Nature–nurture issue | The question of how heredity and experience each contribute to a trait, and how they interact. |
| Natural selection | Inherited traits that help an organism survive and reproduce become more common over generations. |
| Heritability | The share of the variation in a trait, within one population, that is linked to genetic differences. |
| Twin study | Compares identical (monozygotic) and fraternal (dizygotic) twins to estimate genetic influence. |
| Adoption study | Compares adopted children with their biological and adoptive parents. |
| Operational definition | The exact procedure used to measure or manipulate a variable. |
| Independent variable (IV) | The factor an experimenter manipulates. |
| Dependent variable (DV) | The outcome measured to see whether the IV had an effect. |
| Confounding variable | A factor other than the IV that differs between groups and could explain the result. |
| Random assignment | Placing participants into conditions by chance, so groups start out similar. |
| Random sample | Choosing participants by chance from the population, so the sample represents it. |
| Correlation coefficient (r) | A number from −1 to +1 giving the direction and strength of a linear relationship. |

## Heredity and environment (1.1)

Every behavior you will study this year, from reflexes to friendships to depression, rests on a body built from genes and shaped by experience. The old **nature–nurture** debate asked which one wins. The modern answer is that the question is badly posed: genes and environments **interact**. Genes are switched on or off by experience (the field that studies this is called epigenetics), and people's genes nudge them toward the environments they choose, such as a musically inclined child who spends more time practicing.

**The evolutionary perspective.** Charles Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859) proposed **natural selection**: individuals vary, some of the variation is inherited, and inherited traits that improve survival and reproduction become more common. Evolutionary psychologists ask how a behavior might once have been adaptive. A fear of snakes that is learned unusually quickly makes sense in an ancestral setting where snakebites killed, and a preference for sweet and fatty foods makes sense where calories were scarce. Use the idea carefully: a story about the past is a hypothesis to test, not a proof, and "natural" never means "right".

**Eugenics: a warning.** In 1883 Francis Galton coined the word *eugenics* for the idea of "improving" humanity by controlling who reproduces. In the early twentieth century it was used to justify forced sterilization laws in many U.S. states, restrictive immigration quotas and, in Nazi Germany, mass murder. It mistook social prejudice for biology, treated group averages as fixed destinies and ignored environment entirely. It is the clearest reason psychology insists that "heritable" does not mean "unchangeable" and that research findings must never be used to rank the worth of people.

**How researchers separate the two influences.**

- **Family studies** check whether a trait runs in families. They cannot separate genes from environment, because families share both.
- **Twin studies** compare identical twins (who share essentially all their genes) with fraternal twins (who share about half, like any siblings). If identical twins are much more alike on a trait than fraternal twins, genes probably matter. The strongest designs study twins **reared apart**, such as the Minnesota study of twins raised in different homes, which began in 1979; reared-apart identical twins were often strikingly similar in personality and interests.
- **Adoption studies** ask whether adopted children resemble their biological parents (genes) or their adoptive parents (environment) more.

**Heritability** summarizes these designs. A heritability of 0.50 for a trait means that about half of the *differences among people in that population* are linked to genetic differences. Three cautions the AP exam loves: heritability describes a **group**, never one person ("half of your height comes from genes" is wrong); it can **change** when the environment changes (when everyone gets good nutrition, more of the remaining variation is genetic); and a high heritability within groups says nothing about the cause of differences **between** groups.

## How psychology does research

**Why not just use common sense?** Hindsight bias (the "I knew it all along" feeling after you learn a result) and overconfidence make everyday judgment unreliable. The **scientific method** replaces it: a **theory** organizes observations and produces **hypotheses**, testable predictions that must be **falsifiable** (some result could show them wrong). Results are checked by **peer review** and by **replication**: other researchers repeating the study with new participants.

**Methods.**

| Method | What it does | What it cannot do |
|---|---|---|
| Experiment | Manipulates an IV, randomly assigns participants, measures a DV | Always generalize beyond the lab and sample |
| Correlational study | Measures two variables to see whether they vary together | Show cause and effect |
| Case study | Studies one person or group in depth | Generalize to others |
| Naturalistic observation | Records behavior in its natural setting without interfering | Explain why the behavior happens |
| Survey | Asks many people about attitudes or behavior | Avoid wording effects and social desirability bias |
| Meta-analysis | Combines the results of many studies of the same question | Fix the flaws of the studies it combines |

Longitudinal studies follow the same people over time; cross-sectional studies compare different age groups at one time. You will meet both again in Unit 3.

**Experiments, step by step.** The **independent variable** is what the researcher changes; the **dependent variable** is what is measured. Both need **operational definitions**: not "stress" but "heart rate in beats per minute during a timed math task". The **experimental group** gets the treatment; the **control group** does not, or gets a **placebo** (an inactive look-alike) so that expectations are equal. **Random assignment** makes the groups similar before the treatment, so a difference afterward can be blamed on the IV. Anything else that differs between groups is a **confounding variable**. Two common confounds come from people: **experimenter bias** (researchers' expectations shape how they treat participants or score results) and **demand characteristics** (participants guess the hypothesis and act on their guess). The fixes are a **single-blind** procedure (participants do not know their condition) or a **double-blind** one (neither participants nor the people collecting data know).

**Sampling.** The **population** is everyone the researcher wants to draw conclusions about; the **sample** is who is actually studied. A **random sample** gives everyone in the population an equal chance of selection and supports **generalization**. A convenience sample (whoever is nearby) invites **sampling bias**. Keep the two "randoms" apart: random *sampling* decides who is in the study (generalizability); random *assignment* decides who gets which condition (cause and effect).

**Ethics.** Research with people must be approved by an **Institutional Review Board (IRB)** and follow guidelines like the American Psychological Association's code:

- **Informed consent** before taking part (for minors, a parent's consent and the child's own assent).
- **Freedom to withdraw** at any time without penalty.
- **Protection from harm**, physical and psychological; risks must be minimal and outweighed by benefits.
- **Confidentiality**: data are kept private and reported without names.
- **Deception** only when no alternative exists and the study is worth it, followed by **debriefing**: telling participants the true purpose afterward.

Animal research has its own committee review and requires humane care. These rules exist because of real abuses, such as the U.S. Public Health Service syphilis study at Tuskegee (1932–1972), in which Black men were left untreated without their knowledge.

## Reading the numbers

**Central tendency.** The **mean** is the average; the **median** is the middle score when they are ordered; the **mode** is the most frequent score. Outliers pull the mean toward them, so the median is the better summary of a **skewed** distribution. In a **positively skewed** distribution (a few very high scores, like household income), the mean is above the median; in a **negatively skewed** one (a few very low scores, like an easy test), it is below.

**Variation.** The **range** is highest minus lowest score; it depends on two scores only. The **standard deviation** (SD) measures how far scores typically fall from the mean: a small SD means scores cluster tightly.

**Worked example: a twin data set.** A hypothetical class measures how many points apart five pairs of twins score on a vocabulary test: 2, 4, 4, 5, 15. Mean = 30 ÷ 5 = 6. Median = 4. Mode = 4. Range = 15 − 2 = 13. The one large gap (15) pulls the mean above the median, a positive skew, so the median (4) better describes a typical pair.

**The normal curve.** Many traits are distributed in a symmetric bell shape. About **68%** of scores fall within 1 SD of the mean, about **95%** within 2 SD and about **99.7%** within 3 SD. A **percentile rank** is the percentage of scores at or below a given score. On a test with mean 100 and SD 15, a score of 115 is 1 SD above the mean, at about the **84th** percentile (50% below the mean plus 34% between the mean and +1 SD).

**Correlation.** A **scatterplot** shows two variables for each participant. The **correlation coefficient** r runs from −1 to +1: the sign gives the direction (positive: both rise together; negative: one rises as the other falls), and the distance from 0 gives the strength. r = −0.70 is stronger than r = +0.40. **Correlation does not show causation**: a third variable may cause both, or the direction may run either way. An **illusory correlation** is a relationship we believe in that the data do not show.

**Inferential statistics.** A result is **statistically significant** when it is unlikely to have happened by chance alone; psychologists usually require a probability (p) less than .05. **Effect size** tells how *large* a difference or relationship is. A huge sample can make a tiny, unimportant effect significant, so report both.

## Common mistakes

- **Saying heritability applies to a person.** It describes variation in a population.
- **Treating "genetic" as "fixed".** Environments change how genes are expressed.
- **Mixing up random sampling and random assignment.** Sampling supports generalizing; assignment supports cause.
- **Calling a correlational study an experiment.** No manipulated IV and no random assignment means no causal claim.
- **Vague operational definitions.** "Happiness" is a concept; "score on a 10-item life-satisfaction scale" is an operational definition.
- **Forgetting debriefing after deception.** Deception without debriefing violates the guidelines.

## Self-check

1. Identical twins reared apart are more alike in shyness than fraternal twins reared together. What does this suggest?
2. A researcher finds r = −0.62 between hours of screen time and hours of sleep. Describe the relationship and say what you cannot conclude.
3. Scores: 3, 5, 5, 6, 21. Give the mean, median, mode and range, and say which center is better.
4. On a test with mean 50 and SD 10, what percentile is a score of 30?
5. Name the IV and DV: students are randomly assigned to study with music or in silence, then take a 20-item quiz.
6. Why does a study using deception require debriefing?

*Answers:* 1. A genetic influence on shyness, since the twins share genes but not homes. 2. A moderately strong negative relationship: more screen time goes with less sleep. It does not show that screen time causes less sleep. 3. Mean 8, median 5, mode 5, range 18; the median, because 21 is an outlier. 4. 30 is 2 SD below the mean, about the 2nd to 3rd percentile (2.5%). 5. IV: music or silence; DV: number of quiz items correct. 6. Participants have a right to know the study's true purpose and to leave without false beliefs or distress caused by the study.
