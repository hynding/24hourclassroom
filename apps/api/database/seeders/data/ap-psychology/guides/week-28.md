## What positive psychology is

For most of the twentieth century, research psychology concentrated on what goes wrong: disorders, prejudice, aggression. In 1998, as president of the American Psychological Association, **Martin Seligman** argued that the field also needed to study what goes right. The result was **positive psychology**, the scientific study of human strengths and of the conditions that let individuals and communities flourish.

It rests on **three pillars**:

1. **Positive emotions**: contentment with the past, happiness in the present, hope for the future.
2. **Positive character**: strengths such as curiosity, courage, kindness and self-control.
3. **Positive (enabling) institutions**: families, schools, workplaces and communities that foster the first two.

Two misunderstandings to avoid. Positive psychology does **not** say negative emotions are bad: fear, sadness and anger are normal and useful. And it is not the same as "think positive" slogans; its claims must survive the same research tests as any other.

Positive psychology shares the humanistic psychologists' interest in growth (Rogers and Maslow from Unit 4), but where humanistic writers relied heavily on clinical insight, positive psychologists emphasize measurement and experiments.

## Subjective well-being and how to measure it

**Subjective well-being** is a person's own evaluation of their life, with three parts: high **life satisfaction**, frequent **positive emotions** and infrequent **negative emotions**. Because it is subjective by definition, it is measured mostly by **self-report**:

- **Life satisfaction scales**: a few statements ("In most ways my life is close to my ideal") rated from strongly disagree to strongly agree.
- **Experience sampling**: a phone prompts people at random moments to report what they are doing and how they feel, which reduces memory errors.
- **Daily diaries** of positive and negative emotions.

Self-reports raise familiar concerns from Week 1: **social desirability** (people may report more happiness than they feel) and **wording effects**. Researchers check **reliability** (does the same person score similarly a week later?) and **validity** (do scores relate to things they should, such as friends' ratings?).

What predicts well-being? Across many studies, **close relationships** are among the strongest predictors. **Income** matters most when it lifts people out of poverty and adds less above that. Health, meaningful work and a sense of purpose also predict higher well-being. Nearly all of these findings are **correlational**.

## Broaden-and-build theory

Barbara **Fredrickson's broaden-and-build theory** asks what positive emotions are for. Negative emotions **narrow** attention toward a specific action: fear focuses you on escape. Positive emotions do the opposite: they **broaden** attention and the range of thoughts and actions we consider (play, exploration, trying new things). Over time, these broadened moments **build** lasting resources: friendships, skills, knowledge and resilience, which in turn make more positive emotion likely, an upward spiral.

**Worked example 1.** After a fun day at a festival with friends, Malik feels open and curious, so he says yes when a classmate invites him to try robotics club. A year later the club is where his closest friends are. The positive emotion broadened his choices (saying yes to something new) and built a resource (friendships).

## Gratitude, kindness and how we know

Two of the best-studied **positive interventions** are gratitude and kindness:

- In randomized studies, people asked to list things they were grateful for each week reported a more positive outlook than people asked to list hassles or neutral events.
- People randomly given a small sum of money to spend **on someone else** reported more happiness that evening than people told to spend it on themselves.

Effects like these are real but usually **modest**, and they depend on the person and on how the exercise is done. That is why reading the data matters.

**Worked example 2: a hypothetical gratitude experiment** (invented for this course). Ninety ninth-graders, with parental permission and their own agreement, were **randomly assigned** to write about three good things each evening, to do one kind act a day, or to list three ordinary activities (control), for three weeks. Well-being was measured before and after with a 10-item questionnaire.

| Group | n | Mean change | Standard deviation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gratitude | 30 | +4.2 | 3.1 |
| Kindness | 30 | +3.6 | 3.4 |
| Control | 30 | +0.5 | 3.0 |

- **Independent variable:** the assigned evening activity. **Dependent variable:** change in questionnaire score (its **operational definition**).
- **Means:** both intervention groups rose more than control. Both differences were statistically significant (p under .01), so they are unlikely to be due to chance alone.
- **Standard deviations** of about 3 points show that students varied: some gained much more than 4 points, and some gained little or nothing.
- **Gratitude versus kindness:** the difference (0.6 points) was not significant (p = .48), so the study cannot say one works better.
- **Generalizability:** volunteers at one school, for three weeks; the effect might not last or might differ elsewhere.
- **Ethics:** minors need **parental consent** and their own **assent**, plus IRB approval and a debriefing.

## Character strengths and virtues

Christopher Peterson and Martin Seligman surveyed moral and philosophical traditions across many cultures and proposed **six virtues** that appear widely, with **24 character strengths** beneath them:

| Virtue | Example strengths |
|---|---|
| Wisdom and knowledge | Creativity, curiosity, judgment, love of learning, perspective |
| Courage | Bravery, perseverance, honesty, zest |
| Humanity | Love, kindness, social intelligence |
| Justice | Teamwork, fairness, leadership |
| Temperance | Forgiveness, humility, prudence, self-regulation |
| Transcendence | Appreciation of beauty, gratitude, hope, humor, spirituality |

Each person's **signature strengths** are the ones they show most and feel most "themselves" using. A common exercise asks people to use a signature strength in a new way each day.

## Resilience and posttraumatic growth

**Resilience** is positive adaptation despite adversity. One of the striking findings of developmental research is how common it is: many children who face serious hardship do well, which the researcher Ann Masten called "ordinary magic". Resilience is not a fixed trait; it grows from **protective factors** such as at least one caring, stable adult, close friendships, problem-solving and self-regulation skills, a sense of purpose, and supportive schools.

**Posttraumatic growth**, a term from Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun, is positive change that some people report after **struggling** with a highly challenging event. People describe five kinds of change: greater **appreciation of life**, closer **relationships**, a sense of **personal strength**, **new possibilities**, and **spiritual or existential** change. Three cautions: growth comes from the struggle, not the event; it can coexist with ongoing distress; and nobody should be pressured to "find the silver lining".

| | Resilience | Posttraumatic growth |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Bouncing back or staying well | Changing beyond where one was before |
| Example | Keeping up school and friendships after a family move | Saying a serious illness led to a new purpose and closer family ties |

## Flow, adaptation and comparison

**Flow**, described by Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, is complete absorption in an activity whose **challenge matches your skill**: too easy is boring, too hard is anxious, and in between attention narrows and time seems to vanish. Musicians, athletes, gamers and writers all report it.

Two ideas explain why happiness is harder to raise than it looks:

- The **adaptation-level phenomenon** (also called hedonic adaptation): we judge experiences against a neutral level set by recent experience. A raise, a new phone or a better grade feels wonderful, then becomes the new normal.
- **Relative deprivation**: we judge what we have by comparing ourselves with others. A student delighted by a B+ may feel let down on learning that most friends got A's.

Together they suggest that lasting well-being comes more from relationships, meaningful activity and gratitude, which do not fade as fast, than from acquiring things.

## Common mistakes

- **"Positive psychology means always being happy."** It studies strengths and flourishing; negative emotions remain normal and useful.
- **Mixing up resilience and posttraumatic growth.** Bouncing back versus growing beyond.
- **Treating self-report well-being scores as perfect measures.** Consider social desirability and reliability.
- **Claiming cause from correlations** between happiness and income, friends, or social media use.
- **Reading a non-significant difference as proof that two treatments are equal.** It means the study could not tell them apart.
- **Placing strengths under the wrong virtue.** Perseverance and honesty are courage; gratitude and hope are transcendence; self-regulation and forgiveness are temperance.

2. Give an operational definition of subjective well-being.
3. A student feels joyful after a concert and then volunteers to help plan the next one, making new friends. Which theory?
4. Under which virtue do kindness and social intelligence belong?
5. Which describes "I'm functioning as well as before the accident", resilience or posttraumatic growth?
6. Why does a new phone stop feeling exciting?
7. In a gratitude experiment, why is the control group asked to write about ordinary activities rather than do nothing?
