## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Attitude | A learned evaluation of a person, group, object or idea that predisposes us to respond in a certain way. |
| Stereotype | A generalized belief about the members of a group (the cognitive part). |
| Prejudice | An unjustified, usually negative attitude toward a group and its members (the feeling part). |
| Discrimination | Unjustified negative behavior toward a group or its members (the action part). |
| Explicit attitude | An attitude a person is aware of and can report. |
| Implicit attitude | An automatic association a person may be unaware of, inferred from indirect measures such as reaction times. |
| In-group bias | Favoring one's own group. |
| Out-group homogeneity bias | Seeing members of other groups as more alike than members of one's own group. |
| Ethnocentrism | Judging other cultures by the standards of one's own and seeing one's own as superior. |
| Just-world phenomenon | The belief that people get what they deserve, which can lead to blaming victims. |
| Belief perseverance | Holding onto a belief even after the evidence for it has been discredited. |
| Cognitive dissonance | The discomfort felt when two thoughts, or a thought and a behavior, conflict; people reduce it, often by changing an attitude. |
| Elaboration likelihood model | Persuasion happens by a central route (careful thought about arguments) or a peripheral route (surface cues). |
| Halo effect | Letting one positive trait (such as attractiveness) color judgments of a person's other traits. |
| Foot-in-the-door | Gaining agreement to a small request first, which makes agreement to a larger one more likely. |
| Door-in-the-face | Making a large request that is refused, then a smaller one, which is then more likely to be accepted. |

## Thinking, feeling and acting toward groups

Psychologists often separate three parts of an attitude toward a group:

- A **stereotype** is a belief: "Members of the robotics club are all shy." Stereotypes are generalizations; they can be positive or negative, and even when they hold on average they are poor guides to any one individual. They grow out of schemas and the representativeness heuristic from Unit 2.
- **Prejudice** is an evaluation: a negative feeling toward the group, such as dislike or distrust, that is not justified by evidence about the individual.
- **Discrimination** is behavior: refusing to rent to, hire, include or fairly grade someone because of their group.

The three often travel together, but not always. A manager may hold a prejudice but follow a fair hiring rubric that prevents discrimination; a policy can discriminate even when no one involved feels prejudice.

### Explicit and implicit attitudes

An **explicit attitude** is one you can report: "I think older drivers are as safe as younger ones." An **implicit attitude** is an automatic association that may differ from what you report. The best-known measure is the **Implicit Association Test (IAT)**, introduced by Anthony Greenwald and colleagues in 1998, which compares how quickly people sort words and images when two concepts share a response key. Faster pairing is taken as a stronger association.

The IAT is a good chance to apply Unit 1 research ideas. A measure needs **reliability** (consistent results; an individual's IAT score can change noticeably between sessions) and **validity** (measuring what it claims; studies find IAT scores predict individual behavior only weakly). Researchers therefore use it mostly to study groups, not to label individuals.

### Us and them

- **In-group bias** is favoring one's own group. Henri Tajfel's "minimal group" studies in the 1970s showed it appears even when groups are formed on trivial grounds (such as which of two abstract painters you supposedly preferred): people still allocated more rewards to their own group.
- **Out-group homogeneity bias** is seeing "them" as all alike ("they're all the same") while seeing "us" as varied individuals.
- **Ethnocentrism** is judging other cultures by the standards of your own and assuming your own is superior.

### Explaining away injustice

The **just-world phenomenon** (studied by Melvin Lerner) is the belief that the world is fair, so people get what they deserve and deserve what they get. It is comforting, but it leads observers to blame victims: "If she was robbed, she must have been careless." Notice the link to week 21: it is a dispositional attribution for someone else's misfortune.

**Belief perseverance** is clinging to a belief after its basis has been discredited. Once people have built explanations for why something is true, those explanations survive even when the original evidence is withdrawn. Asking people to explain why the *opposite* might be true is one of the few things that reliably weakens it.

## Cognitive dissonance

Leon Festinger proposed **cognitive dissonance theory** in the 1950s: when our thoughts conflict with each other or with our actions, we feel uncomfortable tension and are motivated to reduce it. Because a past action cannot be undone, we often change the attitude to fit the action.

**The classic study.** Festinger and James Carlsmith (1959) had male college students spend an hour on deliberately dull tasks, such as turning pegs a quarter turn over and over. Each was then asked to tell the next participant (actually a confederate) that the tasks were enjoyable. Some were paid $1 for this; others were paid $20, a large sum at the time. A control group told no one anything. Later, asked how enjoyable the tasks really were, the $1 group rated them *more* enjoyable than either the $20 group or the control group.

Why? The $20 participants had plenty of **justification** for the lie: "I said it for the money." The $1 participants did not. Holding "the task was boring" alongside "I told someone it was fun for a dollar" created dissonance, and the easiest thought to change was the attitude: "maybe it wasn't so bad." The general lesson: small incentives for a behavior produce more attitude change than large ones, because large ones explain the behavior away.

Everyday dissonance: after choosing between two similar colleges, people tend to rate the chosen one more favorably and the rejected one less (reducing dissonance about the choice). A person who smokes and knows the risks may downplay the evidence.

**Worked example 1: three ways to reduce dissonance.** Kim believes she is honest but copied a friend's homework.

| Strategy | Kim's thought |
|---|---|
| Change the behavior (for the future) | "I'll do my own work from now on." |
| Change the attitude | "Copying homework isn't really dishonest; everyone does it." |
| Add a justifying thought | "It was only a practice assignment, and I'd been sick." |

Dissonance theory predicts the second and third are common because they need no change in behavior.

## Persuasion

### The elaboration likelihood model

Richard Petty and John Cacioppo's **elaboration likelihood model (ELM)** says a message can persuade by two routes:

- The **central route**: the audience thinks carefully about the arguments and evidence. It happens when people are **motivated** (the issue matters to them) and **able** (they have the time, knowledge and attention). Attitude change by this route tends to last and to predict behavior.
- The **peripheral route**: the audience responds to surface cues, such as a speaker's attractiveness or fame, the number of arguments, catchy music, or a confident tone. It happens when people are distracted, uninterested or lack the knowledge to judge the arguments. Change by this route tends to be quicker and more fragile.

The **halo effect** often powers peripheral persuasion: one positive trait, such as attractiveness or celebrity, spills over onto unrelated judgments ("she's charming, so her diet advice must be sound").

**Worked example 2: which route?** A town will vote on a tax for a new library.

- A homeowner who will pay the tax reads the budget breakdown and compares it with the cost of the old building's repairs: **central route** (motivated and able).
- A busy shopper sees a poster of a popular local athlete saying "Vote yes!" and decides to vote yes: **peripheral route**, helped by the **halo effect**.

The model predicts the homeowner's attitude will be more stable if opponents campaign later.

### Compliance techniques

- **Foot-in-the-door.** Start with a small request, then make a larger one. Jonathan Freedman and Scott Fraser (1966) found that homeowners who had first agreed to a small request about safe driving were far more likely, weeks later, to agree to put a large, unattractive "drive carefully" sign in their yard than homeowners asked only the large request. Agreeing to the small request seems to change how people see themselves ("I'm someone who supports this"), which echoes dissonance and self-perception.
- **Door-in-the-face.** Start with a large request that will be refused, then retreat to the smaller request you wanted. In a study by Robert Cialdini and colleagues (1975), students who first refused to volunteer as counselors for two hours a week for two years were much more likely to agree to chaperone a single two-hour zoo trip than students asked only about the zoo trip. The retreat feels like a concession that invites one in return (the reciprocity norm you will meet in 4.3).

## Research methods: measuring attitudes

**Self-report** (surveys, rating scales) is quick and direct, but suffers from **social desirability bias**: people may report the attitudes they think they should hold, especially about groups. Wording matters too (framing, from 2.2). Indirect measures such as the IAT avoid some of this but raise reliability and validity questions.

**A hypothetical experiment.** A research team randomly assigns 200 students to hear a speech on later school start times from either a "sleep scientist" or an "unnamed speaker", with the same arguments. Half of each group is told the change starts next year at their school (high personal relevance); half that it might happen in ten years elsewhere (low relevance). The dependent variable is agreement on a 1–9 scale. If the speaker's title matters only in the low-relevance groups, the results fit the ELM: credentials work as a peripheral cue when motivation is low. With two independent variables, this is a **factorial design**, and "the effect of one variable depends on the level of the other" is an **interaction**.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating stereotype, prejudice and discrimination as synonyms.** Belief, feeling, behavior.
- **Saying the $20 group in Festinger and Carlsmith liked the task more.** It was the $1 group: too little justification creates more dissonance.
- **Mixing up foot-in-the-door and door-in-the-face.** Small then large versus large (refused) then small.
- **Calling celebrity endorsement the central route.** Fame is a cue, not an argument.
- **Confusing in-group bias with out-group homogeneity.** Favoring us versus seeing them as all alike.
- **Reading an individual's IAT score as proof of prejudice.** The measure is noisy for individuals.

## Self-check

1. "That landlord won't rent to students." Stereotype, prejudice or discrimination?
2. Why did $1 participants rate a dull task more enjoyable than $20 participants?
3. A charity first asks for $500 and, when refused, asks for $25. Name the technique.
4. A distracted viewer is persuaded by an ad's upbeat music. Which route?
5. "Those fans from the other school all act the same." Which bias?
6. A study measures attitudes only by asking people directly about a sensitive group. Name one threat to validity.

*Answers:* 1. Discrimination (behavior). 2. Without enough external justification, lying created dissonance, reduced by changing their attitude toward the task. 3. Door-in-the-face. 4. Peripheral. 5. Out-group homogeneity bias. 6. Social desirability bias: people may report what they think is acceptable rather than what they believe.
