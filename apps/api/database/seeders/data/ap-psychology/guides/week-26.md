## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Emotion | A response of the whole organism involving physiological arousal, expressive behavior and conscious experience. |
| James-Lange theory | We experience emotion as our awareness of our body's response to a stimulus: arousal comes first. |
| Cannon-Bard theory | The stimulus triggers arousal and the conscious feeling of emotion at the same time and independently. |
| Two-factor (Schachter-Singer) theory | Emotion requires physical arousal plus a cognitive label for that arousal. |
| Cognitive appraisal | Interpreting a situation (threat, loss, challenge) in a way that shapes which emotion we feel. |
| Facial-feedback hypothesis | Facial expressions can influence the emotions we feel. |
| Broaden-and-build theory | Positive emotions broaden thinking and action and build lasting personal resources. |
| Display rules | Cultural norms for when, where and how strongly emotions should be expressed. |
| Universal emotions | Emotions whose facial expressions are produced and recognized across cultures. |

## The three components

Fear on seeing a large dog running loose involves:

1. **physiological arousal**: a pounding heart, faster breathing, sweating, from the **sympathetic nervous system** and stress hormones such as adrenaline;
2. **expressive behavior**: widened eyes, raised brows, freezing or backing away;
3. **conscious experience**: the feeling "I'm scared" and thoughts about what to do.

Theories of emotion argue about how these three relate, and especially about the order.

## Classic theories

### James-Lange: arousal first

William James and Carl Lange, working separately in the 1880s, proposed that we do not run because we are afraid; we feel afraid because we notice ourselves running. Stimulus → bodily response → awareness of the response = emotion.

Support: people with high-level spinal cord injuries, who feel less from the body, sometimes report less intense emotions, and changing the body (as in facial-feedback studies) can nudge feelings.

### Cannon-Bard: at the same time

Walter Cannon and Philip Bard argued in the 1920s that bodily responses are too similar across emotions (a racing heart comes with fear, anger and excitement) and too slow to *be* the emotion. They proposed that the stimulus is relayed by the **thalamus** simultaneously to the cortex (producing the feeling) and to the sympathetic nervous system (producing arousal). Stimulus → arousal *and* feeling, independently.

### Schachter-Singer two-factor theory: arousal plus a label

Stanley Schachter and Jerome Singer (1962) proposed that emotion has two ingredients: **general physiological arousal** and a **cognitive label** that explains it. In their classic study, participants were injected with epinephrine (adrenaline), which causes arousal, under the cover story of testing a vitamin's effect on vision. Some were told to expect a racing heart and shaky hands; others were not. Each then waited with a confederate who acted either euphoric or angry. Participants who had **no explanation** for their arousal tended to take on the confederate's mood more than those who knew the injection was the cause; the informed group already had a label for their arousal. Later attempts to replicate the results have been mixed, but the study remains the standard illustration of the theory.

A related phenomenon is **misattribution of arousal** (sometimes called spillover). In a well-known 1974 field study by Donald Dutton and Arthur Aron, men who were approached by a female interviewer on a high, swaying suspension bridge were more likely to call her later than men approached on a low, stable bridge, as if some of the arousal from the bridge was labeled as attraction.

### Cognitive appraisal, and the "low road"

Richard Lazarus emphasized **cognitive appraisal**: our interpretation of a situation shapes the emotion. A test can be appraised as a threat (anxiety) or a challenge (excitement). Appraisal can be very fast and need not be conscious.

Robert Zajonc argued that some emotional reactions come before any cognition at all, and neuroscience offers a mechanism: Joseph LeDoux described a fast **"low road"** from the **thalamus** directly to the **amygdala**, which triggers a fear response before the slower **"high road"** through the cortex finishes analyzing the stimulus. That is why you jump at a stick on the path before you recognize it is not a snake.

**Worked example 1: one event, four theories.** Ravi hears a loud bang in a dark parking garage.

| Theory | Account |
|---|---|
| James-Lange | His heart pounds and he jumps; noticing this, he feels fear. |
| Cannon-Bard | The thalamus sends signals at once to his body (pounding heart) and his cortex (fear); neither causes the other. |
| Two-factor | He feels aroused, looks around, sees people diving for cover and labels the arousal "fear". Had he seen confetti falling, he might have labeled it "excitement". |
| Cognitive appraisal / low road | His amygdala triggers a startle before he can think; a moment later he appraises the bang as a tire and relaxes. |

## The face and emotion

### Facial-feedback hypothesis

If James and Lange were partly right, changing the body should change the feeling. The **facial-feedback hypothesis** says expressions can influence emotion. In a well-known 1988 study, Fritz Strack and colleagues had participants hold a pen in their teeth (forcing a smile-like expression) or in their lips (preventing one) while rating cartoons, and reported that the "smiling" group found the cartoons funnier. But a large **registered replication** across 17 laboratories in 2016 did not find the effect. Later meta-analyses suggest facial feedback has real but small effects. This history is a model case of why **replication** matters.

### Universal expressions and display rules

Paul Ekman and colleagues showed photographs of posed facial expressions to people in many cultures, including, in the late 1960s, people in a remote region of Papua New Guinea with little exposure to Western media. People widely matched the same faces to the same emotions. The evidence is strongest for a small set of **basic emotions**, commonly listed as happiness, sadness, fear, anger, surprise and disgust. Children born blind produce typical expressions, which also suggests a biological basis. Some researchers argue that recognition rates vary more across cultures than early studies showed, and that context strongly shapes how faces are read.

What differs by culture is **display rules**: norms for when and how much to show emotion. Many cultures discourage open displays of anger at work; some value restraint in front of elders or strangers. In one often-cited study, Japanese and American students watching an unpleasant film alone showed similar expressions of disgust, but when an experimenter was present, Japanese students more often masked their expressions with smiles. **Elicitors**, the events that trigger an emotion, also differ by culture: a gesture that is a compliment in one place can be an insult in another.

## Positive emotion: broaden and build

Barbara Fredrickson's **broaden-and-build theory** proposes that negative emotions narrow attention toward a specific action (fight, flee), while **positive emotions broaden** attention and thinking, making people more open, creative and flexible. Over time, those broadened moments **build** lasting resources: friendships, skills, resilience. The theory links this week to positive psychology in Unit 5.

**Worked example 2: broaden or narrow?** Two students prepare for a group project. Maya just got good news and feels joyful; she suggests three creative approaches and chats with a new classmate who later becomes a friend (broaden and build). Zack is anxious about a test later that day; he focuses narrowly on finishing his part quickly (narrowed attention, useful for urgent problems but limiting for creative work).

## Research methods: studying emotion

- **Manipulating arousal** (an injection, exercise, a scary bridge) lets researchers test whether arousal causes emotion, but it requires informed consent, attention to risk, and debriefing when deception is used.
- **Confederates** create a standard social context (Schachter and Singer's euphoric or angry partner).
- **Operational definitions** of emotion include self-report ratings, coded facial expressions (Ekman developed a detailed coding system), and physiological measures such as heart rate and skin conductance.
- **Replication** tests whether a finding is real: the facial-feedback story shows how a famous effect can shrink when many labs test it with preregistered methods.
- **Cross-cultural sampling** tests universality: a claim that expressions are universal needs participants from cultures with little shared media.

## Common mistakes

- **James-Lange order.** Body first, then feeling; not the other way around.
- **Cannon-Bard is not "body causes feeling".** It is simultaneous and independent, via the thalamus.
- **Two-factor needs both.** Arousal without a label, or a label without arousal, is not an emotion in this theory.
- **Overstating facial feedback.** The effect is small, and a large replication of the pen study failed.
- **Confusing display rules with universal expressions.** Expressions of basic emotions are widely shared; rules about showing them vary by culture.
- **Forgetting the low road.** Fear can start in the amygdala before conscious appraisal.

## Self-check

1. Which theory says we feel sad because we cry?
2. Which brain structure relays signals simultaneously to cortex and body in Cannon-Bard?
3. In Schachter and Singer's study, who was most influenced by the confederate's mood?
4. What did the 2016 multi-lab replication of the pen study find?
5. Name six commonly listed basic emotions.
6. Why might a person smile while feeling disappointed at a formal dinner?

*Answers:* 1. James-Lange. 2. The thalamus. 3. Participants aroused by epinephrine who had no explanation for their arousal. 4. No reliable facial-feedback effect. 5. Happiness, sadness, fear, anger, surprise and disgust. 6. Display rules: cultural norms about which emotions to show in a given setting.
