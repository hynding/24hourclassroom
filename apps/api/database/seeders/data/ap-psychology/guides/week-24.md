## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Personality | A person's characteristic pattern of thinking, feeling and acting across situations and time. |
| Psychodynamic theory | Explains personality by unconscious processes and conflicts, often rooted in childhood (Freud and successors). |
| Id, ego, superego | Freud's three parts of personality: unconscious drives, the reality-based mediator, and internalized morals. |
| Defense mechanism | An unconscious strategy the ego uses to reduce anxiety by distorting reality. |
| Projective test | A test that presents ambiguous stimuli so people project inner motives onto them (Rorschach inkblots, Thematic Apperception Test). |
| Humanistic theory | Emphasizes free will, personal growth and the drive toward self-actualization (Maslow, Rogers). |
| Unconditional positive regard | Acceptance and valuing of a person without conditions (Rogers). |
| Reciprocal determinism | Behavior, personal factors (thoughts, beliefs) and environment all influence one another (Bandura). |
| Self-efficacy | Belief in one's ability to succeed at a specific task. |
| Trait | A stable, characteristic tendency to behave in certain ways. |
| Big Five | Openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroticism (emotional stability). |
| Factor analysis | A statistical method that finds clusters of items that correlate with one another. |
| Personality inventory | A questionnaire of many items measuring traits, such as the MMPI. |

## Psychodynamic theory (4.4)

Sigmund Freud, a physician in Vienna, developed **psychoanalysis** in the late 1800s and early 1900s from his work with patients. His central claim was that much of mental life is **unconscious**: like an iceberg, most of the mind lies below the surface, and unacceptable wishes and memories there still shape behavior, dreams, slips of the tongue and symptoms.

Freud described personality as three interacting systems:

- the **id**, present from birth, entirely unconscious, operating on the **pleasure principle**: it seeks immediate gratification of basic drives;
- the **ego**, developing in early childhood, operating on the **reality principle**: it tries to satisfy the id in realistic ways and mediates among id, superego and the outside world;
- the **superego**, the internalized standards of parents and society: the conscience, producing guilt and pride.

He also proposed **psychosexual stages** (oral, anal, phallic, latency, genital) in which unresolved conflicts could leave a person **fixated**. These stage claims have received little scientific support.

### Defense mechanisms

When the ego cannot resolve conflict, Freud said, it reduces anxiety by unconsciously distorting reality. These are the ones to know:

| Defense mechanism | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Repression | Pushes anxiety-arousing thoughts and memories out of awareness | Having no memory of a frightening incident |
| Denial | Refuses to acknowledge a painful reality | Insisting a failing grade will "fix itself" |
| Displacement | Redirects impulses toward a safer target | Snapping at a younger sibling after a coach yells at you |
| Projection | Attributes one's own unacceptable feelings to others | A jealous person accusing a friend of being jealous |
| Rationalization | Offers a self-justifying explanation in place of the real reason | "I didn't make the team because I didn't really want to" |
| Reaction formation | Turns an unacceptable impulse into its opposite | Being extremely sweet to someone you resent |
| Regression | Retreats to a more childlike stage | A teen sulking and refusing to speak when frustrated |
| Sublimation | Channels unacceptable impulses into socially valued activity | Turning anger into intense boxing practice |

### Neo-Freudians and modern views

Later psychodynamic theorists kept the unconscious but gave more weight to social forces and the conscious mind: Alfred Adler emphasized striving to overcome feelings of inferiority; Karen Horney challenged Freud's views of women and stressed anxiety in relationships; Carl Jung proposed a **collective unconscious** of shared images (archetypes).

**Evaluating it.** Freud drew attention to unconscious processes, childhood and inner conflict, and modern research does confirm that much processing happens outside awareness (implicit memory from 2.3; implicit attitudes from 4.2). But many of his specific claims are **not falsifiable**: they explain any outcome after the fact, and predict little in advance. They were based on a small, unrepresentative set of patients, and the idea that traumatic memories are routinely repressed conflicts with evidence that trauma is usually remembered all too well.

### Projective tests

Projective tests give ambiguous stimuli on the theory that people will project unconscious motives onto them. In the **Rorschach inkblot test** (Hermann Rorschach, 1921), people describe what they see in ten inkblots. In the **Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)**, developed by Henry Murray and Christiana Morgan in the 1930s, people tell stories about ambiguous pictures. Critics point to weak **inter-rater reliability** (two scorers can interpret the same responses differently) and weak **validity** (scores predict real-world outcomes poorly), though structured scoring systems improve reliability.

## Humanistic theory (4.4)

In the mid-1900s, Abraham Maslow and Carl Rogers reacted against both psychoanalysis (too focused on illness) and behaviorism (too mechanical). Humanistic psychologists emphasize **free will**, conscious experience and **growth**.

**Maslow** proposed that people strive toward **self-actualization**, fulfilling their potential, once more basic needs are met (his hierarchy of needs returns in week 25). He studied people he judged self-actualized and described them as self-accepting, open, spontaneous and focused on problems beyond themselves.

**Rogers** held that people are basically good and growth-oriented, and that growth requires a climate of:

- **genuineness** (being open and honest),
- **acceptance**, especially **unconditional positive regard** (valuing a person even when disapproving of a behavior),
- **empathy** (sharing and reflecting another's feelings).

Central to Rogers is the **self-concept**: all our thoughts and feelings about who we are. When people receive only **conditional** regard ("I love you when you get good grades"), they may distort their experience to fit others' conditions, creating a gap between the real and ideal self. A positive self-concept goes with acting and perceiving positively. **Self-esteem** is how much we value ourselves overall.

**Evaluating it.** Humanistic ideas shaped counseling, parenting and education, and positive psychology (Unit 5) carries them forward. Critics say key concepts are vague and hard to measure (who decides who is "self-actualized"?), and that the theory can underplay people's capacity for harm.

## Social-cognitive theory (4.5)

Albert Bandura's **social-cognitive** perspective sees personality as the product of interaction between people and their situations. In **reciprocal determinism**, three factors influence one another:

- **behavior** (what we do),
- **personal and cognitive factors** (beliefs, expectations, self-efficacy, traits),
- **environment** (the situations we are in and that others create).

**Worked example 1: reciprocal determinism.** Ana, who believes she is good at science (personal), joins the robotics club (behavior). The club surrounds her with challenging problems and supportive mentors (environment), which build her skills and confidence further (personal), so she enters a competition (behavior), which puts her in yet another environment. Each arrow runs both ways.

**Self-efficacy** is a belief in one's ability to succeed at a particular task. It is built by past mastery, watching similar others succeed (observational learning, 3.9), encouragement, and interpreting arousal as readiness rather than fear. Higher self-efficacy predicts persistence. **Locus of control** (Rotter, from 4.1) is a more general belief about control, and also belongs to this perspective.

**Evaluating it.** Social-cognitive theory is built on testable experiments and explains how the same person behaves differently across settings. Critics say it can underweight emotions, unconscious motives and biologically based traits.

## Trait theories (4.5)

Trait theorists aim to **describe** personality rather than explain its origins. Early efforts found thousands of trait words; researchers then used **factor analysis**, which groups test items that correlate highly with one another, to reduce them to a few underlying dimensions.

The most widely supported result is the **Big Five** (remember OCEAN):

| Factor | High scorers tend to be | Low scorers tend to be |
|---|---|---|
| Openness to experience | imaginative, curious, open to variety | practical, conventional, prefer routine |
| Conscientiousness | organized, careful, disciplined | disorganized, careless, impulsive |
| Extraversion | sociable, talkative, energized by others | reserved, quiet, energized by solitude |
| Agreeableness | trusting, helpful, cooperative | suspicious, critical, competitive |
| Neuroticism (low emotional stability) | anxious, moody, easily upset | calm, secure, even-tempered |

Each factor is a **continuum**, not a type, and scores are roughly normally distributed. The Big Five appear across many cultures, are moderately heritable in twin studies (identical twins reared apart resemble each other more than fraternal twins), become more stable through adulthood, and predict outcomes: conscientiousness predicts school and job performance, for example.

**Personality inventories** measure traits with many items. Some are built by **factor analysis** (Big Five inventories). The **Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI)**, first developed in the 1940s, was built **empirically**: items were kept if they distinguished groups (such as people with and without a diagnosed condition), whatever their content. Compared with projective tests, inventories are objectively scored and generally more reliable and valid, though self-report can be distorted by social desirability, and some include validity scales to detect that.

**The person-situation debate.** Walter Mischel pointed out in the 1960s that trait scores predict behavior in any one situation only weakly. The current view: traits predict **average** behavior across many situations well, while the situation strongly shapes any single act. That reconciles weeks 23 and 24.

**Worked example 2: which perspective?**

| Explanation of why Leo is anxious before tests | Perspective |
|---|---|
| Unresolved childhood conflicts about his strict father surface as fear of judgment. | Psychodynamic |
| He scores high on neuroticism, a stable, partly heritable trait. | Trait |
| His parents praised him only for grades, so his self-concept depends on performing. | Humanistic |
| He has low self-efficacy in math after past failures, and his classroom rewards speed over understanding. | Social-cognitive |

## Research methods: testing personality theories

- **Falsifiability**: a scientific theory must make predictions that could be shown wrong; much of Freud's theory could not.
- **Reliability**: consistent results across time (test-retest), across scorers (inter-rater) and across items (internal consistency).
- **Validity**: measuring what it claims and predicting relevant outcomes.
- **Correlation**: most trait research is correlational. A correlation of r = 0.30 between conscientiousness and GPA shows a modest positive relationship, not that being conscientious causes high grades.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing up displacement and projection.** Redirecting a feeling to a safer target versus attributing your feeling to someone else.
- **Mixing up reaction formation and sublimation.** Turning an impulse into its opposite versus channeling it into a valued activity.
- **Calling the Big Five types.** They are continuous dimensions.
- **Confusing self-efficacy with self-esteem.** Task-specific confidence versus overall self-worth.
- **Treating projective tests as reliable.** Name their reliability and validity problems.
- **Forgetting "reciprocal".** In Bandura's model every factor affects every other.

## Self-check

1. Which part of Freud's personality structure operates on the reality principle?
2. A student angry at his boss kicks the trash can. Name the defense mechanism.
3. What does Rogers say growth requires?
4. Name the three factors in reciprocal determinism.
5. A test-taker who is organized, careful and reliable scores high on which Big Five factor?
6. Why are projective tests criticized?

*Answers:* 1. The ego. 2. Displacement. 3. Genuineness, acceptance (unconditional positive regard) and empathy. 4. Behavior, personal/cognitive factors and environment. 5. Conscientiousness. 6. Low inter-rater reliability and weak validity; interpretations depend on the scorer.
