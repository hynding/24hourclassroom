## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Encoding | Getting information into the memory system. |
| Automatic processing | Unconscious encoding of incidental information, such as space, time and frequency. |
| Effortful processing | Encoding that requires attention and conscious effort. |
| Semantic encoding | Encoding meaning; usually the most durable kind. |
| Self-reference effect | Better memory for information related to oneself. |
| Elaborative rehearsal | Linking new information to existing knowledge and meaning. |
| Mnemonic device | A memory aid, often using vivid imagery or organization. |
| Chunking | Grouping items into familiar, manageable units. |
| Method of loci | Imagining items placed along a familiar route, then mentally walking it to recall them. |
| Spacing effect | Better long-term retention from study spread over time than from the same study crammed together. |
| Serial position effect | Better recall of the first (primacy) and last (recency) items in a list. |

## Automatic and effortful processing

A lot of information enters memory without any effort. **Automatic processing** captures where things are (space: you can picture where on the page a fact appeared), when things happened (time: you can reconstruct the order of your day), and how often things happen (frequency: you know which friend texts most). Well-learned skills, such as reading words in your native language, also become automatic: you cannot look at a familiar word and *not* read it.

**Effortful processing** requires attention and work. Learning a new vocabulary list, a formula or a historical timeline takes rehearsal, and with practice some effortful tasks become more automatic, as reading did when you were six.

## How information is encoded

Information can be encoded by what it looks like (**visual encoding**), what it sounds like (**acoustic encoding**) or what it means (**semantic encoding**). As week 9's levels-of-processing studies showed, meaning is the strongest hook. Two refinements:

- **The self-reference effect.** Relating information to yourself ("Does *generous* describe me?") produces especially strong memory, because the self is a rich, well-organized schema with many connections.
- **Imagery.** Concrete words that call up vivid mental images ("volcano") are remembered better than abstract ones ("process"); combining a meaning with an image gives two routes back to the memory.

**Maintenance rehearsal**, simple repetition, keeps information in short-term memory but is weak at moving it into long-term memory. **Elaborative rehearsal** connects new information to what you already know: linking "the hippocampus is shaped like a seahorse" to the Greek word for seahorse, or comparing a new concept with a familiar one. Elaboration is semantic encoding put to work.

**Worked example 1: improving a study habit.** Kai reads the definition of "prospective memory" five times. That is maintenance rehearsal. To elaborate, he could explain it in his own words, connect it to his own life ("remembering to feed the dog before school"), and contrast it with episodic memory. Each step adds meaningful links, the self-reference effect included.

## Mnemonic devices

- **Chunking.** Grouping items into meaningful units extends the limited capacity of short-term memory. "FBICIAUSA" is nine letters; "FBI CIA USA" is three chunks. Experts chunk naturally: a chess master sees a whole familiar arrangement where a novice sees separate pieces.
- **Method of loci.** Picture a familiar route, such as your walk through your house, and place each item to be remembered at a spot along it. To recall, walk the route in your mind. The technique dates back to ancient Greek and Roman speakers.
- **Acronyms and acrostics.** The first letters of items form a word or a sentence: "HOMES" for the five Great Lakes.
- **Hierarchies.** Organizing information into categories and subcategories, as in an outline or a concept map, helps both encoding and retrieval. This guide's own headings are a hierarchy.

**Worked example 2: choosing a mnemonic.** To remember the five monocular depth cues from week 7, a student could use an acrostic ("Really Interesting Rabbits Twirl Lightly" for relative size, interposition, relative clarity, texture gradient, linear perspective), or place each cue at a spot in her kitchen (method of loci). To remember a 10-digit phone number, chunking (3-3-4) is the natural tool.

## The spacing effect

In the 1880s Hermann Ebbinghaus, testing himself on lists of nonsense syllables, found that spreading his practice over several days produced better retention than the same amount of practice in one session. Later research has confirmed this **spacing effect** many times: **distributed practice** (spaced study) beats **massed practice** (cramming) for long-term retention, even when total study time is the same. Cramming can work for a test the next morning but fades fast.

Combining spacing with self-testing (week 11's **testing effect**) is one of the most effective study strategies known.

## The serial position effect

When people learn a list and then recall it in any order, they remember the first items (**primacy effect**) and the last items (**recency effect**) better than items in the middle. Primacy is usually explained by extra rehearsal: the early items got more repetitions and reached long-term memory. Recency is explained by the last items still being in short-term memory at the moment of recall. After a delay of even 30 seconds filled with another task, the recency effect shrinks while the primacy effect remains.

**Worked example 3: reading a serial position curve.** In a hypothetical class demonstration, students heard a list of 15 words and recalled them immediately. The percentage recalling each word, by position: positions 1–3 averaged 72%, positions 6–10 averaged 28%, and positions 13–15 averaged 80%. The U-shaped curve shows both primacy (high at the start) and recency (highest at the end). If the class had counted backward for 30 seconds before recalling, we would expect positions 13–15 to fall toward the middle values, while positions 1–3 stayed high.

## Sleep and encoding

Week 5 introduced sleep's role in memory. After a day of learning, sleep, especially deep sleep and REM sleep, helps **consolidate** new memories, making them more stable. Pulling an all-nighter both cuts the study time spread across days (no spacing) and disrupts consolidation.

## Research methods spotlight: testing a study strategy

**Worked example 4: spaced versus massed study.** In a hypothetical experiment, 80 ninth graders are randomly assigned to learn 30 Spanish vocabulary words. The massed group studies for 60 minutes on one day. The spaced group studies for three 20-minute sessions on three days. A week after the last session, both groups take the same test.

| Group | n | Mean words correct (of 30) | SD |
|---|---|---|---|
| Massed | 40 | 12.5 | 4.2 |
| Spaced | 40 | 19.8 | 3.9 |

- *IV:* the study schedule (massed or spaced). *DV:* words correct on the test a week later.
- *Controlled variables:* same words, same total study time (60 minutes), same test, same delay.
- *Possible confound:* if the spaced group's test came a week after day 3 but the massed group's came a week after day 1, the delay would differ. A careful design times both tests from the last session, as here.
- *Interpretation:* the spaced group scored 7.3 words higher on average, and the SDs show similar spread, so the groups overlap only partly. Random assignment means prior Spanish knowledge should be balanced, supporting a causal conclusion for students like these.
- *Effect size:* a difference of 7.3 words is nearly two standard deviations, a large effect, which matters more for a student choosing a study plan than the p-value alone.

## Common mistakes

- **Equating effortful with deep.** Effortful processing can still be shallow (repeating a word 20 times); depth is about meaning.
- **Swapping primacy and recency.** Primacy: first items. Recency: most recent, last items.
- **Thinking chunking adds capacity.** Short-term memory still holds a few units; chunking makes each unit carry more.
- **Explaining spacing as "more total study".** The spacing effect holds with total time equal.

## Self-check

1. Without trying, you know you've had pizza three times this week. Which kind of processing?
2. Remembering "SCUBA" instead of "self-contained underwater breathing apparatus" is an example of what?
3. Why does relating "introvert" to yourself help you remember its definition?
4. A shopper remembers the first and last items on a spoken grocery list. Name both effects.
5. In worked example 4, why must total study time be equal in the two groups?
6. Which study plan would the spacing effect recommend for a test in two weeks?

*Answers:* 1. Automatic processing (frequency). 2. An acronym (a chunked mnemonic). 3. The self-reference effect: linking to the self produces deep, well-connected encoding. 4. Primacy and recency (the serial position effect). 5. Otherwise more time, not spacing, could explain the difference: a confound. 6. Several shorter sessions spread over the two weeks, with self-testing.
