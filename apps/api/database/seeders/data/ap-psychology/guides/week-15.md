## A note on language

Psychology describes this topic with neutral, precise terms, and so does this guide. Scientific terms change as research and usage develop; the AP exam uses the terms below. When a question describes people, it describes groups and averages, never what any one person must be like.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sex | Biological characteristics: sex chromosomes, gonads, hormones and anatomy. |
| Intersex | A term for people born with sex characteristics (chromosomes, gonads, hormones or anatomy) that do not fit typical definitions of female or male. |
| Gender | The roles, behaviors, expectations and identities a culture associates with being female, male or another gender. |
| Gender identity | A person's internal sense of their own gender. |
| Cisgender / transgender | Gender identity matches / differs from the sex assigned at birth. |
| Nonbinary | A gender identity that is not exclusively female or male. |
| Gender role | A culture's expectations about how people of a gender should behave. |
| Gender expression | How a person outwardly shows gender (clothing, hairstyle, behavior). |
| Gender typing | The process by which children acquire behaviors and preferences their culture links to a gender. |
| Sexual orientation | An enduring pattern of emotional, romantic and/or sexual attraction. |

## Sex and gender (3.3)

**Sex** refers to biology. Most people have XX or XY sex chromosomes, and the matching gonads, hormones and anatomy, which develop before birth and mature at puberty (week 14). A small percentage of people are **intersex**: their chromosomes, gonads, hormones or anatomy differ from typical patterns. **Gender** refers to the psychological and social side: the roles, expectations and identities attached to being female, male or another gender. Sex and gender are related but not the same, and psychologists study both.

**Gender identity** usually becomes clear early: by about age 2 or 3 most children label themselves and others by gender, and by around age 5 to 7 most understand that gender is generally stable over time. A **cisgender** person's gender identity matches the sex they were assigned at birth; a **transgender** person's gender identity differs from it; a **nonbinary** person's identity is not exclusively female or male. **Gender roles** are cultural: which jobs, clothes, chores and emotions are seen as fitting a gender differs widely across cultures and has changed within cultures over time.

## Influences on gender development (3.3)

As in every Unit 3 topic, nature and nurture interact.

**Biological influences.** Before birth, the presence or absence of hormones called androgens (such as testosterone) guides development of the reproductive anatomy, and androgens also affect developing brain circuits. At puberty, hormones released under the control of the hypothalamus and pituitary gland (1.3, 1.4) drive the changes in primary and secondary sex characteristics. Research on children exposed to unusually high prenatal androgen levels has found shifts, on average, in their toy and play preferences, which suggests prenatal hormones contribute to some gender-related behavior.

**Social learning theory.** Children learn gender-typed behavior the way they learn other behavior (week 20): they are reinforced or discouraged for gender-typed play (operant conditioning, week 19), and they **observe and imitate** models, especially same-gender parents, peers and media characters. A boy praised for building blocks and teased for playing with dolls learns which play earns approval.

**Gender schema theory.** Psychologist Sandra Bem proposed that children develop a **gender schema**, a mental framework for organizing information by gender (a schema is a cognitive concept from 2.2 and week 16). Once children know their own gender, they actively seek out information about what "people like me" do and remember schema-consistent information better. A child who believes "cooking is for girls" may misremember a picture of a man cooking as a woman cooking: the schema shapes encoding and retrieval (2.4, 2.6).

**Culture and socialization.** Parents, peers, schools, religion and media all transmit gender roles, and children are often most strictly policed by peers. Cross-cultural research shows that the content of gender roles varies a great deal, evidence that much of it is learned.

## How large are sex and gender differences? (3.3)

Questions about psychological differences between groups are a chance to use statistics carefully.

**Distributions overlap.** Even when two groups differ on average, their distributions almost always overlap heavily. A difference in means says nothing about any one individual.

**Effect size.** Researchers report **effect size** (often Cohen's d: the difference between two means divided by the standard deviation). By convention, d of about 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large. Large reviews that combine many studies (meta-analyses) have found that most psychological differences between women and men, in areas such as math performance, verbal ability and self-esteem, are small or close to zero, while a few, such as some measures of physical aggression and some physical abilities like throwing distance, are larger on average.

**Statistical significance is not size.** With a very large sample, a tiny difference can be statistically significant (unlikely to be due to chance) yet have almost no practical importance. Always ask for the effect size.

**Worked example 1: reading a hypothetical result.** A research team gives a spatial reasoning test to 2,000 high school students. Group means differ by 2 points, the standard deviation within each group is 10 points, and the difference is statistically significant (p under 0.01). The effect size is d = 2 ÷ 10 = 0.2, a small effect: the distributions overlap almost completely, and the large sample is why such a small difference reached significance. The study is also correlational: it cannot say whether biology, practice, expectations or stereotype threat (a concern about confirming a negative stereotype that can lower performance) produced the gap.

## Sexual orientation (3.3)

**Sexual orientation** is an enduring pattern of emotional, romantic and/or sexual attraction. Common terms include **heterosexual** (attraction to people of a different gender), **gay** or **lesbian** (attraction to people of the same gender), **bisexual** (attraction to more than one gender) and **asexual** (little or no sexual attraction to others). Researchers distinguish orientation (attraction) from sexual behavior and from self-identification, and the three do not always line up. In the mid-20th century, Alfred Kinsey's surveys described sexual behavior on a continuum rather than as two boxes.

**What research shows about its development:**

- **Not a disorder.** Homosexuality was removed from the American Psychiatric Association's diagnostic manual in 1973; no sexual orientation is classified as a psychological disorder.
- **Not explained by parenting.** Research has found no good evidence that parenting style, family structure or early experiences determine sexual orientation; studies comparing children raised by same-gender parents with other children have found no reliable difference in the children's sexual orientation.
- **Genetic influence, but no single gene.** Twin studies find that identical twins are more often alike in orientation than fraternal twins, though far from always, which points to a moderate genetic influence plus non-genetic factors. A very large genetic study published in 2019 found that many genetic variants, each with a tiny effect, together account for part of the variation in same-sex sexual behavior, and that there is no single "gay gene."
- **Prenatal influences.** Researchers have proposed that prenatal hormone exposure contributes. One well-replicated finding is the **fraternal birth order effect**: on average, each older biological brother (born to the same mother) slightly raises the probability that a later-born male is gay. It is a small statistical effect seen in large samples, not a rule about any one family, and some researchers link it to the prenatal environment.
- **Not chosen.** Most people report that they did not choose their orientation and that it emerged in childhood or adolescence, consistent with the biological evidence above.

As with gender, the honest summary is that biological and environmental factors both appear to matter, and the specific mechanisms are still being studied.

## Studying sensitive topics (3.1 and 3.3)

**Anonymity and confidentiality.** Surveys on gender identity or orientation ask for private information, so researchers use anonymous responses where possible, store any identifiers separately, and tell participants how data will be protected. An IRB reviews the plan; participants may skip questions or withdraw.

**Self-report bias.** People may underreport stigmatized identities or behavior because of **social desirability bias**. Anonymous online surveys usually yield higher estimates than face-to-face interviews.

**Sampling.** A sample recruited at a single event or through a single website is a convenience sample and may not represent the population. Estimates of how many people hold a particular identity vary with the wording of the question, the age group and the method.

**Worked example 2: twin concordance.** In a hypothetical twin study, both twins in a pair share a trait (are "concordant") in 30% of identical-twin pairs and 15% of fraternal-twin pairs. The higher identical-twin concordance suggests a genetic contribution. But because concordance is far below 100% even for twins with the same genes, non-genetic factors, including the prenatal environment, must also contribute. And because twins raised together share environments, twin data alone cannot cleanly separate genes from shared experience.

## Common mistakes

- **Using sex and gender as synonyms.** Sex is biological; gender is psychological and social.
- **Confusing gender identity with sexual orientation.** Identity is who you are; orientation is whom you are attracted to.
- **Reading a mean difference as true of every individual.** Distributions overlap; report the effect size.
- **Treating "significant" as "large."** A big sample can make a tiny difference significant.
- **Claiming one cause.** Neither gender development nor sexual orientation is explained by a single gene, hormone or experience.

## Self-check

1. Define gender identity and gender role, and give an example of a gender role that differs between two cultures or eras.
2. A child insists that a doctor in a story "must be a man" despite the picture. Which theory explains this best?
3. Two group means differ by 3 points with a standard deviation of 15. What is d, and how large is the effect?
4. Why does identical-twin concordance below 100% show that genes are not the whole story?
5. Why do anonymous surveys on sensitive topics usually give different estimates from face-to-face interviews?

*Answers:* 1. Gender identity is a person's internal sense of their gender; a gender role is a culture's expectations for behavior (for example, which gender is expected to do the cooking or wear certain clothes differs across cultures and decades). 2. Gender schema theory. 3. d = 3 ÷ 15 = 0.2, a small effect. 4. Identical twins share their genes, so any difference between them must come from non-genetic factors. 5. Anonymity reduces social desirability bias, so people report more honestly.
