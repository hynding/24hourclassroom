## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Motivation | A need or desire that energizes and directs behavior. |
| Instinct | A complex, unlearned behavior that is fixed across a species. |
| Drive-reduction theory | A physiological need creates an aroused tension state (a drive) that motivates behavior to satisfy the need. |
| Homeostasis | The tendency to maintain a balanced internal state, such as a stable body temperature. |
| Arousal theory | People are motivated to maintain an optimal level of arousal. |
| Yerkes-Dodson law | Performance rises with arousal up to an optimal point, then falls; the optimal level is lower for difficult tasks. |
| Incentive | A positive or negative environmental stimulus that motivates behavior. |
| Self-determination theory | People are motivated to satisfy needs for competence, autonomy and relatedness. |
| Intrinsic / extrinsic motivation | Doing something for its own sake / for an outside reward or to avoid punishment. |
| Overjustification effect | Rewarding a behavior people already enjoy can reduce their intrinsic motivation for it. |
| Approach-avoidance conflict | One goal with both attractive and unattractive features. |
| Sensation seeking | A trait involving the search for varied, novel, intense experiences and willingness to take risks for them. |

## Theories of motivation

No single theory explains all motivated behavior; each covers part of it.

**Instinct theory.** Early psychologists tried to explain behavior by listing instincts. The approach collapsed into naming rather than explaining ("people are sociable because of a social instinct"). Today the term is reserved for complex, unlearned, species-wide behaviors such as a bird's nest building. The broader idea that genes predispose behavior survives in evolutionary psychology.

**Drive-reduction theory.** Associated with Clark Hull in the 1940s: a physiological **need** (for food, water) creates an aroused tension state, a **drive**, that pushes us to reduce it. The aim is **homeostasis**, a steady internal state, like a thermostat holding a room at one temperature. **Primary drives** are biological (hunger, thirst); **secondary drives** are learned (money, which buys food). The theory explains eating when hungry well, but not why people seek stimulation when all needs are met: climbing mountains, watching horror films, solving puzzles for fun.

**Arousal theory.** We are motivated to keep arousal at an **optimal level**: too little and we seek stimulation, too much and we seek calm. This explains curiosity and thrill seeking that drive-reduction cannot.

**The Yerkes-Dodson law.** Named for a 1908 study by Robert Yerkes and John Dodson, it describes performance as an inverted U: performance improves with arousal up to a point, then declines. The best level depends on the task: **easy or well-learned tasks** are performed best at relatively **high** arousal; **difficult or unfamiliar tasks** at **lower** arousal.

**Worked example 1: reading an inverted U.** A hypothetical study measured students' heart-rate increase before a timed quiz (as an operational definition of arousal) and their scores.

| Arousal group | Mean score on easy quiz | Mean score on hard quiz |
|---|---|---|
| Low | 72 | 61 |
| Moderate | 85 | 74 |
| High | 88 | 58 |

On the easy quiz, scores kept rising into the high-arousal group; on the hard quiz they peaked at moderate arousal and dropped at high arousal. That is the Yerkes-Dodson pattern. Note that the relationship is **curvilinear**: a correlation coefficient, which measures *linear* relationships, could be near zero even though arousal clearly matters.

**Incentive theory.** Behavior is also *pulled* by external stimuli, **incentives**: the smell of fresh bread, a paycheck, a grade. Drives push, incentives pull; the two often work together (a hungry person is more tempted by bread).

**Maslow's hierarchy of needs.** Maslow (week 24) arranged needs as a pyramid: physiological, safety, belongingness and love, esteem, and self-actualization (later adding self-transcendence), with lower needs generally taking priority. It is a memorable framework, but research has found the order is not fixed; people pursue belonging and meaning even when basic needs are unmet. Use it as a description, not a law.

**Self-determination theory.** Edward Deci and Richard Ryan proposed that people thrive when three psychological needs are met:

- **competence** (feeling capable),
- **autonomy** (feeling in control of one's choices),
- **relatedness** (feeling connected to others).

Settings that support these needs, such as a classroom that offers choices, achievable challenges and warm relationships, foster **intrinsic motivation**.

## Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation

**Intrinsic motivation** is doing something because it is interesting or satisfying in itself; **extrinsic motivation** is doing it for outside rewards or to avoid punishment (operant consequences, 3.8). Both have their place: extrinsic rewards can get a behavior started, and rewards that signal competence ("you've mastered this") can even boost intrinsic interest.

The **overjustification effect** is the risk. In a classic 1973 study, Mark Lepper, David Greene and Richard Nisbett observed preschoolers who already liked drawing with markers. Children who were promised a "good player" award for drawing, and received it, later spent *less* free time drawing than children who received no award or an unexpected one. The expected reward seemed to turn play into work: "I draw to get awards." Rewards felt as controlling undermine autonomy, in line with self-determination theory.

## Motivational conflicts

Kurt Lewin described the conflicts that arise when goals compete:

| Conflict | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Approach-approach | Two attractive options; choosing one means losing the other | Two good colleges both offer admission |
| Avoidance-avoidance | Two unattractive options; one must be chosen | Clean the garage or do the dishes |
| Approach-avoidance | One goal with both good and bad features | A summer job that pays well but has a long commute |
| Multiple approach-avoidance | Several options, each with good and bad features | Choosing between three phones, each with its own pros and cons |

Approach-approach conflicts are usually easiest to resolve; approach-avoidance conflicts often produce wavering, because the closer we get to the goal, the more its drawbacks loom.

## Sensation seeking

Marvin Zuckerman described **sensation seeking** as a trait: the search for varied, novel, complex and intense experiences, and willingness to take physical, social or financial risks for them. High sensation seekers are drawn to extreme sports, travel, new foods and loud music; low sensation seekers prefer routine. It fits arousal theory (different people have different optimal levels) and is partly heritable.

## Hunger and eating

**Biological factors.** Early on, Walter Cannon and A. L. Washburn (1912) had Washburn swallow a balloon that recorded stomach contractions, which coincided with his reports of hunger. But people whose stomachs have been removed still feel hungry, so stomach pangs are not the whole story. Key signals:

- **Ghrelin**, released by an empty stomach, signals hunger to the brain.
- **Leptin**, released by fat cells, signals the brain to reduce appetite when fat stores are adequate.
- **Insulin**, from the pancreas, controls blood glucose; low glucose triggers hunger.
- The **hypothalamus** integrates these signals. In classic rat studies, damaging the lateral hypothalamus reduced eating, and damaging the ventromedial hypothalamus produced overeating, though modern research describes interacting circuits rather than simple on and off centers.
- The body may defend a **set point** weight by adjusting metabolism, which helps explain why weight lost by dieting is often regained.

**Psychological and social factors.** People eat by the clock, by habit, under stress and for pleasure. Large portions and "unit bias" (treating one serving, whatever its size, as the right amount) increase intake; people eat more in groups (social facilitation, 4.3) and are influenced by what others around them eat.

**Cultural factors.** Culture shapes which foods are appealing (insects, raw fish, spicy dishes), when meals happen and body ideals. Many taste preferences are learned through exposure (mere exposure, 4.1) and conditioning, and a taste aversion can follow a single bout of illness (3.7).

**Worked example 2: which theory?**

| Behavior | Best explained by |
|---|---|
| A thirsty hiker drinks from a stream until the thirst is gone. | Drive-reduction (homeostasis) |
| A bored teen on a quiet weekend goes looking for something exciting. | Arousal theory |
| A full diner orders dessert after seeing it on the cart. | Incentive theory |
| A student keeps coding because she enjoys it and chose the project herself. | Self-determination theory (intrinsic, autonomy) |

## Research methods: studying motivation

Motivation cannot be observed directly, so every study needs an **operational definition**: minutes spent on a task during free time (intrinsic motivation), grams of food eaten (hunger), heart rate (arousal). Overjustification studies use **random assignment** to reward conditions and measure behavior during an unrewarded free-choice period, so the reward itself is not still driving behavior. Keeping observers **blind** helps when they rate behavior: observers who don't know each child's condition can't bias their ratings (if participants are also unaware, the procedure is **double-blind**).

## Common mistakes

- **Drive-reduction versus incentive.** Drives push from inside (a need); incentives pull from outside.
- **Misreading Yerkes-Dodson.** Hard tasks need *lower* arousal; easy tasks tolerate higher.
- **Saying rewards always reduce motivation.** The overjustification effect applies to activities already enjoyed and to rewards felt as controlling.
- **Mixing up ghrelin and leptin.** Ghrelin from the stomach, "grumbling," increases hunger; leptin from fat reduces it.
- **Approach-avoidance versus avoidance-avoidance.** One goal with mixed features versus two bad options.
- **Treating Maslow's hierarchy as a strict sequence.** The evidence does not support a fixed order.

## Self-check

1. Which theory best explains why people ride roller coasters?
2. Under the Yerkes-Dodson law, which arousal level suits a difficult new task?
3. Name the three needs in self-determination theory.
4. A child who loved reading reads less after being paid per book. Name the effect.
5. Choosing between two equally unpleasant chores is which conflict?
6. Which hormone, released by fat cells, reduces appetite?

*Answers:* 1. Arousal theory (or sensation seeking). 2. Relatively low to moderate. 3. Competence, autonomy and relatedness. 4. The overjustification effect. 5. Avoidance-avoidance. 6. Leptin.
