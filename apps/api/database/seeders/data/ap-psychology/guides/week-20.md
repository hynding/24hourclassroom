## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Observational learning | Learning by watching others; also called social learning or modeling. |
| Model | The person whose behavior is observed. |
| Vicarious reinforcement / punishment | Seeing a model rewarded / punished, which changes the observer's likelihood of imitating. |
| Insight learning | Sudden realization of a problem's solution, often after a period of no progress. |
| Latent learning | Learning that occurs without reinforcement and is not shown until there is a reason to show it. |
| Cognitive map | A mental representation of the layout of an environment. |
| Mirror neurons | Neurons that fire both when an animal acts and when it watches another perform the same action. |

## Observational learning (3.9)

People learn an enormous amount without ever being reinforced themselves: how to use chopsticks, what to say at a funeral, how to throw a curveball. Albert Bandura argued that humans learn by **watching models** and that this social learning cannot be explained by classical or operant conditioning alone.

**The Bobo doll studies.** In a 1961 experiment, Bandura and colleagues had preschool children watch an adult in a playroom. Some children saw the adult hit, kick and shout at an inflatable Bobo doll; others saw an adult play quietly; a control group saw no model. Later, after a mild frustration, each child was left alone in a room with toys including a Bobo doll. Children who had watched the aggressive model were much more likely to imitate the specific aggressive acts and words, and to invent other aggression, than children in the other groups.

**Learning versus performance.** In a 1965 follow-up, children saw a model rewarded, punished or given no consequence for aggression. Children who saw the model punished imitated less. But when the researchers then offered every child a reward for showing what the model did, children in all groups could reproduce the aggressive acts. They had all **learned** the behavior; the consequences they saw affected whether they **performed** it. **Vicarious reinforcement** (seeing a model rewarded) increases imitation; **vicarious punishment** decreases it.

**Four processes of modeling.** Bandura described what observational learning requires:

1. **Attention**: the observer must notice the model (2.1). Models who are similar, admired or powerful get more attention.
2. **Retention**: the observer must remember the behavior (2.4, 2.5).
3. **Reproduction**: the observer must be physically and mentally able to perform it.
4. **Motivation**: the observer must have a reason to perform it, such as expected reinforcement.

**Prosocial and antisocial modeling.** Models teach helpful behavior as well as harmful behavior. Children who see adults donate, share or comfort others are more likely to do so, especially when the model's words and actions match; models who say one thing and do another ("do as I say, not as I do") tend to produce imitation of the actions. Antisocial modeling helps explain why aggression can run in families and why many researchers are concerned about violent media. Laboratory experiments show short-term increases in aggressive thoughts and behavior after violent media; long-term evidence is mostly **correlational** and the size of the effect is debated, so careful conclusions name the design.

**Worked example 1: is it modeling?** Lucia watches her older sister get a laugh from their parents by telling a joke at dinner, and the next night Lucia tells the same joke. This is observational learning with vicarious reinforcement: Lucia was not reinforced herself before imitating. If she then gets a laugh and tells more jokes, operant conditioning (positive reinforcement) has joined in. Real behavior usually involves more than one kind of learning.

## Cognitive factors (3.9)

**Insight learning.** In work published around 1917 to 1925, Wolfgang Köhler presented chimpanzees with bananas out of reach. One chimpanzee, Sultan, after failing with single sticks, appeared to pause and then suddenly joined two short sticks to make a long one; other chimpanzees stacked boxes to reach hanging fruit. Köhler argued that the solutions came from a sudden reorganization of the problem, **insight**, rather than gradual trial and error. Humans report the same "aha" experience (2.2).

**Latent learning and cognitive maps.** In a 1930 study, Edward Tolman and Charles Honzik ran three groups of rats through a maze daily. One group always found food at the end; one never did; a third found no food for the first 10 days and then found food from day 11. The third group's errors dropped dramatically right after food was introduced, quickly matching or beating the always-rewarded group. Tolman concluded that the rats had been learning the maze all along, forming a **cognitive map**, but had no reason to show it until there was a reward: **latent learning**. Learning had happened without reinforcement, a problem for strict behaviorism.

**Expectations and control.** Learned helplessness (week 19) shows that what an organism **expects** about its control over outcomes shapes later learning: having learned that responses do not matter, it stops trying. In humans, beliefs about whether effort pays off influence persistence, a theme Unit 4 returns to with self-efficacy and locus of control.

**Worked example 2: reading latent learning data.** A hypothetical replication tracks mean errors per maze run for three groups of 12 rats.

| Day | Always rewarded | Never rewarded | Rewarded from day 11 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 9.0 | 9.5 | 9.4 |
| 10 | 3.1 | 7.2 | 7.0 |
| 12 | 2.6 | 6.9 | 2.8 |
| 16 | 2.2 | 6.5 | 2.0 |

The independent variable is the reward condition; the dependent variable is errors per run. Before day 11, the third group looks like the never-rewarded group. One day after reward is introduced, its errors fall from 7.0 to 2.8, far faster than gradual reinforcement learning could explain, so the rats must already have known the maze.

## Neurological factors (3.9)

- **Mirror neurons.** In the 1990s, researchers recording from single neurons in the premotor cortex of macaque monkeys found cells that fired both when a monkey grasped an object and when it watched a researcher grasp one. Similar mirroring activity has been measured in humans with brain imaging. Some researchers propose that mirror systems support imitation and understanding others' actions; others argue that their role in human imitation, empathy and language has been overstated. On the exam, describe them as neurons active during both doing and observing, with a debated role.
- **Brain areas.** Fear conditioning depends heavily on the **amygdala**; simple motor conditioning such as eyeblink conditioning depends on the **cerebellum** (1.4); the **hippocampus** supports learning places and facts, including cognitive maps.
- **Dopamine and prediction.** Dopamine neurons respond strongly when a reward is better than expected and dip when an expected reward does not arrive (1.3), a signal that helps the brain learn which actions and cues predict reward (weeks 18 and 19).
- **Long-term potentiation.** Repeated stimulation strengthens synapses, making later signals pass more easily; this strengthening is a leading candidate for the cellular basis of learning and memory (2.5).

## Comparing the three kinds of learning (3.7–3.9)

| | Classical conditioning | Operant conditioning | Observational learning |
|---|---|---|---|
| What is learned | An association between two stimuli | An association between a behavior and its consequence | A behavior seen in a model |
| Kind of response | Automatic, involuntary (salivation, fear) | Voluntary actions | Voluntary actions |
| Key figures | Pavlov, Watson | Thorndike, Skinner | Bandura |
| Role of the learner | Responds to a signal | Acts and experiences consequences | Watches others' acts and consequences |

**A scenario-labeling routine.** Is the response automatic and triggered by a cue? Classical. Is a voluntary behavior becoming more or less likely because of what followed it for the learner? Operant. Did the learner copy someone without being reinforced first? Observational. Did learning appear suddenly or only when needed? Consider insight or latent learning.

## Common mistakes

- **Saying the Bobo doll children were reinforced.** They imitated without direct reinforcement; that is the point.
- **Treating "did not perform" as "did not learn."** The 1965 study showed the difference.
- **Overclaiming mirror neurons.** Their existence in monkeys is established; their role in human empathy and language is debated.
- **Calling latent learning "slow learning."** It is learning that is hidden until there is a reason to show it.
- **Overstating media violence research.** Name the design: lab experiments show short-term effects; long-term evidence is mostly correlational.

## Self-check

1. A teenager learns to skateboard by watching videos of a skater he admires. Which of Bandura's four processes is helped by the skater being admired?
2. Why did the 1965 Bobo doll follow-up separate learning from performance?
3. What made Tolman's day-11 rats important evidence against strict behaviorism?
4. A chimpanzee suddenly stacks boxes to reach a banana after sitting still for a while. What kind of learning is this?
5. Which brain structure is most important for conditioned fear?

*Answers:* 1. Attention (admired models draw more attention, and motivation can rise too). 2. Children who saw the model punished imitated less, but all of them could reproduce the acts when offered a reward, so they had learned without performing. 3. They learned the maze without reinforcement and showed it only once reward appeared. 4. Insight learning. 5. The amygdala.
