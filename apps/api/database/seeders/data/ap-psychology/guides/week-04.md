# Week 04 · The Brain

**Topics:** 1.4 The Brain
**Practices:** 1 (Concept Application), 2 (Research Methods and Design), 3 (Data Interpretation), 4 (Argumentation)
**Framework:** AP Psychology CED (effective fall 2024), Unit 1, Topic 1.4, in our own words
**Spiral:** Week 2 placed the brain at the top of the central nervous system, and week 3 showed how its neurons signal. This week maps what the brain's regions do, from the brainstem that keeps you breathing to the prefrontal cortex that plans your weekend, and how researchers found out. Week 1's methods return in force: much of what we know comes from case studies, lesions and correlational brain-imaging studies, each with limits you must be able to name.

## What you need to be able to do

- **1.4** Describe the functions of the brainstem (medulla, reticular activating system), the cerebellum and the limbic structures (thalamus, hypothalamus, pituitary link, hippocampus, amygdala).
- **1.4** Describe the four lobes of the cerebral cortex and the motor cortex, somatosensory cortex, prefrontal cortex, association areas, and Broca's and Wernicke's areas.
- **1.4** Explain contralateral control, hemispheric specialization and what split-brain studies show.
- **1.4** Explain neuroplasticity and give examples.
- **1.4** Compare research tools: case studies, lesioning, EEG, fMRI and other scans, with the strengths and limits of each.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Brainstem | The oldest part of the brain, where the spinal cord enters the skull; controls automatic survival functions. |
| Medulla | Brainstem structure controlling heartbeat and breathing. |
| Reticular activating system | Nerve network through the brainstem that controls arousal and alertness. |
| Cerebellum | "Little brain" at the back; coordinates movement, balance and timing, and supports motor learning. |
| Thalamus | Relay station that routes incoming sensory messages (all but smell) to the cortex. |
| Hypothalamus | Regulates hunger, thirst, body temperature and sexual behavior; directs the pituitary gland. |
| Hippocampus | Helps form new long-term memories of facts and events. |
| Amygdala | Involved in emotion, especially fear and aggression. |
| Cerebral cortex | The wrinkled outer layer of the cerebrum: thinking, perceiving, planning, language. |
| Corpus callosum | The thick band of axons connecting the two hemispheres. |
| Neuroplasticity | The brain's ability to change its structure and connections with experience or after damage. |

## From the bottom up

**Brainstem.** The **medulla** controls heartbeat and breathing; serious damage to it is life-threatening. Running through the brainstem is the **reticular activating system**, which filters incoming stimuli and controls arousal: it keeps you awake and alert, and damage to it can cause a coma.

**Cerebellum.** It coordinates smooth, timed movement and balance, and it stores learned motor skills. Unsteady walking and slurred speech after heavy drinking reflect, in part, alcohol's effect on the cerebellum.

**Limbic system** (a set of structures around the brainstem):

- The **thalamus** relays sensory information from the eyes, ears, tongue and skin to the right areas of the cortex. Smell is the exception: its main pathway goes more directly to its own areas.
- The **hypothalamus**, tiny but powerful, keeps the body in balance: hunger, thirst, temperature. It also controls the **pituitary gland**, linking the nervous and endocrine systems, and it helps trigger the sympathetic response from week 2.
- The **hippocampus** helps form new explicit memories. People with damage to both hippocampi may remember their childhood but be unable to form lasting memories of new facts and events (Unit 2 returns to this).
- The **amygdala** is involved in emotional responses, especially fear and aggression, and in recognizing threat in faces.

## The cerebral cortex

The cortex wraps the brain in a thin, folded sheet; the folds pack a large surface into the skull. Each hemisphere has four lobes:

| Lobe | Location | Key areas and functions |
|---|---|---|
| Frontal | Front, behind the forehead | Prefrontal cortex (planning, judgment, impulse control, working memory); motor cortex at the back of the lobe; Broca's area (usually left) for producing speech |
| Parietal | Top, toward the back | Somatosensory cortex: touch, pressure, temperature and body position |
| Occipital | Back | Primary visual cortex |
| Temporal | Sides, above the ears | Auditory cortex; Wernicke's area (usually left) for understanding language |

**Motor and somatosensory cortex.** The **motor cortex** is a strip at the rear of the frontal lobe that sends commands for voluntary movement. Just behind it, at the front of the parietal lobe, the **somatosensory cortex** receives touch and body-position information. In both strips, body parts that need fine control or sensitivity (fingers, lips, tongue) get far more cortex than their size suggests.

**Contralateral control.** Each hemisphere mostly controls, and receives sensation from, the **opposite** side of the body. A stroke in the left motor cortex weakens the right arm or leg.

**Association areas** make up most of the cortex. They are not tied to a single sense or movement; they integrate information to support thinking, remembering, recognizing and speaking. The prefrontal cortex is the biggest example.

**Language.** Damage to **Broca's area** produces slow, effortful speech in which grammar suffers, while understanding is largely spared. Damage to **Wernicke's area** produces fluent speech that makes little sense, with poor understanding. These patterns (types of aphasia) are why language production and comprehension are tied to different regions.

## Two hemispheres

**Hemispheric specialization (lateralization).** In most people the left hemisphere leads on language: speaking, reading, grammar. The right hemisphere leads on many spatial tasks, recognizing faces and reading emotional tone. This is specialization, not separation: in a healthy brain the two cooperate constantly through the **corpus callosum**. "Left-brained" and "right-brained" personality types are a myth.

**Split-brain research.** In the 1960s Roger Sperry, Michael Gazzaniga and colleagues studied patients whose corpus callosum had been cut to control severe epilepsy. Information shown briefly to the **right visual field** goes to the **left hemisphere**, so the patient can say what it was. Information shown to the **left visual field** goes to the **right hemisphere**: the patient typically says they saw nothing, yet their left hand (controlled by the right hemisphere) can pick out the object by touch. Sperry shared the 1981 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for this work.

**Worked example.** A split-brain patient stares at a dot. The word KEY flashes to the left of the dot. *Left visual field → right hemisphere.* Prediction: they cannot name it aloud (speech is mainly left-hemisphere), but the left hand can find a key among hidden objects.

## Neuroplasticity

The brain rewires itself. Learning a skill strengthens and adds connections; heavy practice can enlarge the cortex devoted to a well-used body part. After damage, other areas can sometimes take over lost functions, especially in children. Children who have had one hemisphere removed to stop severe seizures often regain much of their language and movement, though some deficits usually remain. Plasticity declines with age but never disappears.

## How we know: research tools

| Tool | What it shows | Strength | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Case study | One person's changes after damage | Reveals rare effects | May not generalize; damage rarely affects one region alone |
| Lesioning | Effects of deliberately destroying tissue (in animals) | Shows cause | Ethical cost; animals differ from humans |
| EEG | Electrical activity through electrodes on the scalp | Precise timing (milliseconds) | Poor at locating where activity happens |
| fMRI | Blood-oxygen changes as regions work | Shows function with good location | Slower than EEG; correlational; costly |
| MRI and CT | Detailed images of structure | Find damage and tumors | Show structure, not activity |
| PET | Activity traced by a radioactive form of glucose | Shows function | Uses a radioactive tracer; slower and less sharp than fMRI |

**Phineas Gage** is the classic case. In 1848, in a railway construction accident, an iron rod passed through his skull and damaged his frontal lobe. Modern reconstructions from his skull disagree on whether one or both frontal lobes were hurt; the most recent ones suggest mainly the left. He survived, but people who knew him reported that he became more impulsive and less reliable. Later retellings exaggerated the change, and some evidence suggests he later held steady work, which is itself a lesson in the limits of case studies. Still, the case pointed toward the frontal lobes' role in self-control and planning.

**Reading imaging data.** A brain scan is usually **correlational**: a region "lights up" during a task, which shows association, not that the region alone causes the behavior. And a difference in brain structure between, say, musicians and non-musicians does not tell you which came first: musical training or the brain difference.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing up hippocampus and hypothalamus.** **Hippo**campus: memory (a hippo never forgets). **Hypo**thalamus: hunger, thirst, temperature, pituitary.
- **Forgetting contralateral control.** Left motor cortex → right side of the body.
- **Mixing up Broca and Wernicke.** Broca: **b**roken speech (production). Wernicke: **w**ords without meaning (comprehension).
- **"We use only 10% of our brains."** False: imaging shows activity throughout the brain.
- **Assuming fMRI shows cause.** It shows which areas are active during a task.

## Self-check

1. A patient has normal speech but cannot recognize objects by sight after a stroke at the back of the brain. Which lobe?
2. Which structure is most involved in a racing fear response to a growling dog?
3. A split-brain patient sees an apple in the right visual field. Can they name it?
4. Which tool would best show *when* the brain responds to a sound, to the millisecond?
5. Why do the fingers take up more motor cortex than the back?
6. A man's left arm is weak after a stroke. Which hemisphere was likely damaged?

*Answers:* 1. Occipital (visual cortex or nearby visual association areas). 2. The amygdala. 3. Yes: the right visual field goes to the left hemisphere, which usually controls speech. 4. EEG. 5. Fine, precise control needs more cortical neurons. 6. The right hemisphere (contralateral control).
