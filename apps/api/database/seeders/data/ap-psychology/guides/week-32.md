## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Psychotherapy | Treatment using psychological techniques in a relationship with a trained professional. |
| Eclectic approach | Combining techniques from several therapies to fit the person. |
| Transference | Redirecting feelings from past relationships onto the therapist. |
| Unconditional positive regard | Accepting the client without conditions or judgment. |
| Systematic desensitization | Pairing relaxation with a graded hierarchy of feared situations. |
| Token economy | Earning exchangeable tokens for desired behaviors. |
| Cognitive therapy | Identifying and challenging distorted automatic thoughts. |

| Biomedical therapy | Treatment that acts directly on the body: medication, brain stimulation, surgery. |

## Insight therapies

### Psychodynamic therapy

Sigmund Freud's **psychoanalysis** aimed to bring **unconscious conflicts** into awareness, so that insight would relieve symptoms. Its tools:

- **Free association**: saying whatever comes to mind without censoring.
- **Resistance**: blocking painful material by changing the subject, going blank or missing sessions, which the analyst treats as a clue.
- **Transference**: the client redirecting feelings about important people, such as a parent, onto the analyst.
- **Interpretation** of resistance, transference, slips and dreams.

Classic psychoanalysis was long and intensive, and its claims about unconscious causes are hard to test. Modern **psychodynamic therapy** is shorter and face to face, and focuses on present relationship patterns and recurring emotional themes.

### Humanistic therapy

Carl Rogers's **person-centered (client-centered) therapy** is **nondirective**: the therapist does not interpret or advise but creates conditions for growth by offering **genuineness**, **unconditional positive regard** and **empathy**. Its main technique is **active listening**: echoing, restating and clarifying what the client says and feels ("It sounds like you felt invisible at that dinner"). The goal is self-acceptance and movement toward self-actualization.

Psychodynamic and humanistic therapies are both **insight therapies**: they aim to increase self-understanding. The next two families aim more directly at changing behavior and thinking.

## Behavior therapies

Behavior therapists treat problem behaviors as **learned**, so they can be **unlearned** with the conditioning principles from Unit 3.

**Classical conditioning techniques**

- **Exposure therapy**: facing the feared object or situation (in real life, imagination or virtual reality) without escaping, so the fear response undergoes **extinction**.
- **Systematic desensitization** (Joseph Wolpe): a gradual form of exposure. (1) Learn deep muscle relaxation. (2) Build an **anxiety hierarchy** from least to most feared. (3) Pair relaxation with each step in turn, moving up only when calm. Because relaxation and anxiety are incompatible, this is **counterconditioning**. An early example: in 1924 Mary Cover Jones reduced a young boy's fear of a rabbit by bringing it closer while he ate food he enjoyed.
- **Flooding**: intense exposure from the start, with consent, until the fear subsides.
- **Aversive conditioning**: pairing an unwanted behavior with something unpleasant, such as a bitter nail polish for nail biting.

**Worked example 1: an anxiety hierarchy for a fear of dogs.**

| Step | Situation |
|---|---|
| 1 | Looking at a cartoon dog |
| 2 | Watching a video of a calm dog |
| 3 | Standing across a park from a leashed dog |
| 4 | Standing three meters from a calm, leashed dog |
| 5 | Petting a calm dog while its owner holds the leash |

The person practices relaxation at each step and moves on only when the current step no longer brings anxiety: step 4 comes only once step 3 feels calm.

**Operant conditioning techniques**

- **Token economies** reward desired behaviors with tokens (**secondary reinforcers**) exchangeable for privileges. They work well inside the program, but behavior may fade once the tokens stop.
- **Behavior modification** more generally uses reinforcement, shaping and extinction to change specific behaviors.

## Cognitive and cognitive-behavioral therapies

Cognitive therapists target the **thoughts** that drive emotions and behavior.

- **Cognitive therapy** (Aaron Beck) helps people notice **automatic negative thoughts** and **cognitive distortions** (catastrophizing, overgeneralizing, all-or-nothing thinking), test them against evidence and replace them with more realistic ones. It grew out of Beck's work on the **cognitive triad** in depression.
- **Rational emotive behavior therapy (REBT)** (Albert Ellis) is more confrontational. In the **ABC model**, an **A**ctivating event leads to emotional **C**onsequences through **B**eliefs; the therapist vigorously **disputes** irrational beliefs such as "I must be liked by everyone" or "I must never fail."

**Worked example 2: disputing a belief.** Jonah is crushed when a friend ignores one message (A) and thinks, "Nobody likes me; I'll always be alone" (B), so he feels hopeless (C). A cognitive therapist asks, "What is the evidence? Who did reply this week?" An REBT therapist challenges the "must": "It's disappointing, not a catastrophe."

- **Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT)** combines changing thoughts with changing behavior, through homework, practice and gradual exposure. It is among the best-supported treatments for anxiety and depressive disorders.
- **Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT)** (Marsha Linehan), developed for people with borderline personality disorder, balances **acceptance** and **change**, teaching mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation and interpersonal skills.

## Group and family therapy

**Group therapy** lets one therapist help several people at once, lowers cost, and shows members they are not alone; members practice social skills and give each other feedback. **Family therapy** treats the family as a **system**: one person's difficulties are linked to patterns of communication among everyone, so the family works on them together. **Support groups**, led by members rather than professionals, offer similar benefits.

## Does therapy work, and how do we know?

In 1952 **Hans Eysenck** claimed that people in therapy improved no more than would be expected through **spontaneous remission** (getting better without treatment). His challenge pushed researchers toward controlled trials. Three traps explain why testimonials are not enough:

- **Placebo effect**: expecting to improve can itself improve mood.
- **Regression toward the mean**: people seek help when symptoms are at their worst, and extreme states tend to drift back toward average.
- **Confirmation bias**: clients and therapists both want to see progress.

The answer is the **randomized controlled trial**: random assignment to treatment or a control group (waitlist, placebo or another treatment), ideally with **blind** raters. Many trials can then be combined in a **meta-analysis**. An early meta-analysis by Mary Lee Smith and Gene Glass (1977) concluded that the average person in therapy was better off than about three-quarters of untreated people. Later research added that:

- some therapies have particularly strong evidence for particular problems (exposure for phobias and OCD, CBT for many anxiety and depressive disorders);
- the **therapeutic alliance**, the bond of trust and shared goals, predicts success across many approaches;
- **cultural humility** (openness to a client's culture, values and identity, and awareness of one's own biases) strengthens the alliance.

**Ethics.** Therapists keep what clients say **confidential**, but must act to protect safety when there is serious risk of harm to the client or others; the 1976 **Tarasoff** decision in California is linked to this duty. Therapists explain these limits at the start, along with informed consent to treatment.

## Biomedical therapies

| Treatment | Main use | How it works / notes |
|---|---|---|
| Antipsychotics | Schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders | Many block dopamine receptors, reducing positive symptoms; long-term use of older drugs can cause **tardive dyskinesia** (involuntary movements) |
| Antidepressants (SSRIs) | Depressive disorders; also anxiety disorders and OCD | Block reuptake of serotonin; mood effects usually take several weeks |
| Antianxiety drugs (benzodiazepines) | Short-term relief of anxiety | Enhance the inhibitory neurotransmitter **GABA**; fast, but risk of dependence |
| Mood stabilizers (lithium) | Bipolar disorders | Reduce manic and depressive episodes; blood levels are monitored |
| Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) | Severe depression that has not responded to other treatments | Brief controlled seizure under general anesthesia; can cause memory loss around the treatment period |
| rTMS | Depression | Magnetic pulses through the scalp; no anesthesia or seizure |
| Psychosurgery | Historical; now very rare | The **lobotomy** (1940s–1950s) damaged frontal-lobe connections and caused severe harm; abandoned |

**Worked example 3: reading a hypothetical drug trial** (invented for this course). One hundred twenty adults with major depressive disorder were randomly assigned to an antidepressant or an identical placebo for eight weeks, **double-blind**.

| Group | Mean reduction | Standard deviation |
|---|---|---|
| Antidepressant | 9.5 | 6.0 |
| Placebo | 6.0 | 6.0 |

- The placebo group improved too: expectations and regression toward the mean.
- The difference is significant (p = .002), so it is unlikely to be chance.
- The **effect size**, d = (9.5 − 6.0) ÷ 6.0 ≈ 0.58, is moderate: real, but far from every person responding.

Most people with moderate to severe disorders are helped most by combining approaches, and treatment plans are individual.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing up transference and resistance.**
- **Calling person-centered therapy directive.** It is nondirective; REBT is the confrontational one.
- **Confusing systematic desensitization with flooding.** Gradual with relaxation versus intense from the start.
- **Saying SSRIs add serotonin.** They block reuptake, so released serotonin stays in the synapse longer.
- **Treating improvement in a placebo group as proof a drug fails.** Compare the groups.
- **Describing ECT as it was decades ago.** Today it is given under anesthesia, for severe, treatment-resistant depression.
