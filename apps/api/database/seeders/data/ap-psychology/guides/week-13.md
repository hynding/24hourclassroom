## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Intelligence | The ability to learn from experience, solve problems and adapt to new situations. |
| General intelligence (g) | A general ability that underlies performance across many kinds of mental tasks. |
| Fluid intelligence | The ability to reason quickly and abstractly, especially on new problems. |
| Crystallized intelligence | Accumulated knowledge and verbal skills. |
| Multiple intelligences | Howard Gardner's theory that there are several relatively independent kinds of intelligence. |
| Psychometrics | The scientific study of psychological measurement. |
| Aptitude test | A test designed to predict future performance. |
| Achievement test | A test designed to measure what a person has already learned. |
| Standardization | Giving a test under uniform conditions and comparing scores with a pretested norm group. |
| Reliability | The consistency of a test's scores. |
| Validity | The extent to which a test measures or predicts what it claims to. |
| Flynn effect | The rise in average intelligence test scores across generations. |
| Stereotype threat | Worry about confirming a negative stereotype, which can lower performance. |

## Theories of intelligence

**One ability or many?** In the early 1900s Charles Spearman noticed that people who score well on one kind of mental test tend to score well on others: the scores are positively correlated. He used **factor analysis**, a statistical method that finds clusters of related items, and proposed a **general intelligence (g)** factor underlying all of them, plus specific abilities for particular tasks.

Raymond Cattell divided general intelligence into **fluid intelligence** (fast, flexible reasoning on new problems, which tends to decline gradually in later adulthood) and **crystallized intelligence** (vocabulary and accumulated knowledge, which tends to hold steady or grow with age). A 70-year-old may solve a new logic puzzle more slowly than a 20-year-old but beat her at a crossword.

Howard Gardner, in the 1980s, argued for **multiple intelligences**: linguistic, logical-mathematical, musical, spatial, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal and naturalist. He pointed to people with savant syndrome, who show extraordinary skill in one area alongside very limited abilities in others, and to brain damage that destroys one ability while sparing others. Critics reply that many of these "intelligences" are better called talents, that measures of them still correlate with g, and that the theory has been hard to test. Gardner's theory is popular in schools, but a common belief based on it, that students learn best when teaching matches their preferred "learning style", has little research support.

## Measuring intelligence

**Alfred Binet**, working in France in the early 1900s, built tests to identify children who needed extra help in school. He compared a child's performance with what was typical at each age, giving a **mental age**. Binet saw the test as a practical tool, not a measure of a fixed, inborn quantity.

At Stanford University, **Lewis Terman** revised Binet's test for American use (the Stanford-Binet) and popularized the **intelligence quotient**: IQ = mental age ÷ chronological age × 100. An 8-year-old who performs like a typical 10-year-old scores 10 ÷ 8 × 100 = 125. The formula breaks down for adults (a 40-year-old does not do twice as well as a 20-year-old), so modern tests, including David Wechsler's widely used scales for adults and children, report a **deviation score**: a person's performance compared with others of the same age.

Early intelligence testing had a dark side. In the **eugenics** movement of the early twentieth century, test scores were used to argue for restricting immigration and for forced sterilization of people labeled "feeble-minded". Tests given in English to people who did not speak it, or who lacked the cultural knowledge the questions assumed, were treated as measures of innate ability. This history is a lasting warning about how tests can be misused.

**Aptitude tests** aim to predict future ability (a test of reasoning to predict success in a course); **achievement tests** measure what has been learned (an AP exam, a driving test). The line blurs, because aptitude tests also depend on learning.

## Building a good test

- **Standardization.** A test is given in the same way to a large, representative **norm group**, so a new person's score can be compared with theirs.
- **The normal curve.** Intelligence test scores are set to fall in a bell-shaped **normal distribution** with a **mean of 100** and a **standard deviation of 15**. About 68% of people score within one SD of the mean (85 to 115), about 95% within two SDs (70 to 130), and about 99.7% within three (55 to 145).
- **Percentile rank.** The percentage of the norm group scoring at or below a given score. A score of 115 is about the 84th percentile; 130 is about the 98th.
- **Reliability** is consistency. **Test-retest reliability**: the same people get similar scores when tested twice. **Split-half reliability**: scores on two halves of the test (for example, odd and even items) agree. Reliability is usually reported as a correlation coefficient; good intelligence tests reach about +0.9.
- **Validity** is accuracy. **Construct validity**: the test measures the concept it claims to (a reasoning test should require reasoning, not mainly reading speed). **Predictive validity**: scores predict the behavior they are meant to predict, such as school grades.

A test can be reliable without being valid (a bathroom scale that always reads 5 pounds light is consistent but wrong), but it cannot be valid without being reliable.

**Worked example 1: reading a normal curve.** Maya scores 85 on a test with mean 100 and SD 15. She is one SD below the mean, at about the 16th percentile: about 16% of her norm group scored at or below her. A classmate scoring 145 is three SDs above the mean, higher than about 99.9% of the group.

## The extremes

**Intellectual disability** is diagnosed when a person has significant limitations in intellectual functioning, typically an intelligence score of about 70 or below, **and** in adaptive behavior (everyday social and practical skills), beginning in the developmental period. A score alone is never enough. Some cases have a known genetic cause, such as **Down syndrome**, caused by an extra copy of chromosome 21. At the other end, people scoring in roughly the top 2% are often described as **gifted**. Long-term studies of children with high scores have found them, on average, healthy and successful as adults, contrary to the stereotype of the fragile genius.

## Heredity, environment and test performance

As in week 1, **heritability** describes how much of the *variation within a group* is due to genetic differences; it says nothing about an individual and does not mean a trait cannot change. Twin and adoption studies show that intelligence scores are substantially heritable: identical twins raised apart score more alike than fraternal twins raised together. But environment matters too: children adopted from poor environments into enriched ones tend to score higher, and schooling raises scores.

The **Flynn effect**, named for researcher James Flynn, is the finding that average scores on intelligence tests rose markedly across the twentieth century in many countries, so tests must be renormed. Genes cannot change that fast, so better nutrition, more schooling and more complex environments are the likely causes.

**Stereotype threat** is worry about confirming a negative stereotype about one's group, which can lower performance. In studies by Claude Steele and Joshua Aronson in the 1990s, Black college students did worse on a difficult verbal test when it was described as a measure of intellectual ability than when it was not. Tests may also carry **cultural bias** when questions assume knowledge more familiar to one group.

Carol Dweck's research on **mindset** contrasts a **fixed mindset** (ability is set) with a **growth mindset** (ability can be developed with effort and good strategies). Students who see mistakes as part of learning tend to persist longer on hard tasks, though the effects of brief mindset programs in schools have generally been modest.

## Research methods spotlight: is a new test reliable and valid?

**Worked example 2: a test-validation study.** A hypothetical school district pilots a new 40-item reasoning test with 300 ninth graders. Each student takes it in September and again in October, and their end-of-year grade point averages are recorded.

| Statistic | Value |
|---|---|
| Mean score (September) | 24.6 |
| Standard deviation | 6.2 |
| Correlation, September with October scores | r = +0.88 |
| Correlation, odd items with even items | r = +0.84 |
| Correlation, September score with year-end GPA | r = +0.47 |

- *Reliability:* the test-retest (+0.88) and split-half (+0.84) correlations are high: the test gives consistent scores.
- *Predictive validity:* +0.47 is a moderate positive correlation with GPA. The test predicts grades somewhat, but much of the variation in GPA comes from other factors, such as effort, attendance and course choice.
- *Caution:* the norm group is one district's ninth graders, so the norms may not fit students elsewhere; and a correlation with GPA does not show that reasoning ability *causes* grades.

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing reliability with validity.** Consistent is not the same as accurate.
- **Reading heritability as destiny.** It describes group variation in one environment, not a limit on an individual.
- **Calling an AP exam an aptitude test.** It measures learning: an achievement test.
- **Using the ratio IQ for adults.** Modern scores compare people with others of their own age.
- **Diagnosing intellectual disability from a score alone.** Adaptive functioning is required too.

## Self-check

1. What evidence led Spearman to propose g?
2. A retired professor knows far more words than her grandson but solves new puzzles more slowly. Name the two kinds of intelligence.
3. A 10-year-old has a mental age of 12. What is her ratio IQ?
4. What percentage of people score between 70 and 130?
5. A test gives the same scores every time but predicts nothing. Reliable, valid, both or neither?
6. Why can't genes explain the Flynn effect?

*Answers:* 1. Scores on different mental tests are positively correlated. 2. Crystallized (vocabulary) and fluid (new puzzles). 3. 12 ÷ 10 × 100 = 120. 4. About 95%. 5. Reliable but not valid. 6. The rise happened over a few generations, far too fast for genetic change.
