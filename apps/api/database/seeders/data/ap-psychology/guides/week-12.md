## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Forgetting curve | A graph of retention over time: rapid loss at first, then leveling off. |
| Encoding failure | Information never entered long-term memory, usually because it was not attended to. |
| Storage decay | The fading of a stored memory over time. |
| Retrieval failure | A stored memory cannot be accessed, often for lack of the right cue. |
| Tip-of-the-tongue phenomenon | The feeling of being about to recall something just out of reach. |
| Proactive interference | Old learning disrupts recall of new information. |
| Retroactive interference | New learning disrupts recall of old information. |
| Repression | In Freud's theory, pushing anxiety-provoking memories out of awareness. |
| Reconstructive memory | Rebuilding a memory at each retrieval from stored pieces plus later knowledge and expectations. |
| Misinformation effect | Misleading information after an event distorts the memory of it. |
| Source amnesia | Remembering information but forgetting or misattributing where it came from. |
| Imagination inflation | Imagining an event increases confidence that it really happened. |

## How fast we forget

In the 1880s Hermann Ebbinghaus memorized lists of nonsense syllables and measured how much he retained after different delays, using the time saved in relearning. The result, the **forgetting curve**, has a distinctive shape: **most forgetting happens soon after learning, then the curve levels off.** Whatever survives the first few days tends to last. Later studies of real-world learning, such as the Spanish people learned in school, show the same pattern: a steep early drop, then a long plateau for what remains.

The practical lesson ties to week 10: reviewing soon after learning, then at spaced intervals, catches material before the steep early drop.

## Where forgetting happens

Forgetting can be traced to each of the three memory processes:

- **Encoding failure.** The information never got in. Most people cannot draw the face of a coin accurately, though they have handled thousands, because they never attended to the details. This is not forgetting a memory; it is never having formed one.
- **Storage decay.** Memories that are not used may fade physically over time, especially unconsolidated ones.
- **Retrieval failure.** The memory is stored but cannot be reached. The **tip-of-the-tongue phenomenon** is the classic case: you know the word, can name its first letter or number of syllables, but cannot produce it. A cue often brings it back instantly, which proves it was stored.

**Interference** is a major cause of retrieval failure:

- **Proactive interference** (old interferes with new, working *forward*): after changing your locker combination, you keep entering the old one.
- **Retroactive interference** (new interferes with old, working *backward*): after learning Spanish, you find your earlier French vocabulary harder to recall.

Sleep reduces interference: information learned just before sleep has fewer new experiences competing with it before consolidation.

**Motivated forgetting** is forgetting that serves a purpose, such as revising a painful memory to protect self-image. Sigmund Freud proposed **repression**, in which the mind pushes anxiety-provoking memories out of awareness. Many researchers now doubt that traumatic memories are commonly repressed: extreme stress often makes memories *more* intrusive, not less. This matters, because claims of "recovered" repressed memories can be false memories created by suggestion.

**Worked example 1: naming the interference.** (a) Moving to a new house, Leila keeps giving her old address: proactive interference. (b) After memorizing this year's class schedule, Owen cannot recall last year's: retroactive interference. (c) Nia cannot recall the name of the actor in a film until a friend says "it starts with M": retrieval failure, solved by a cue.

## Memory is reconstruction

Memories are not stored like video. Each time we retrieve an event, we **reconstruct** it from stored fragments, filling gaps with general knowledge, expectations and later information. That makes memory efficient, and it makes memory changeable.

**The misinformation effect.** In a 1974 study by Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer, people watched film of a car crash and estimated the cars' speed. Those asked how fast the cars were going when they "smashed into" each other gave higher estimates than those asked about cars that "hit" each other. A week later, people in the "smashed" condition were also more likely to report having seen broken glass, though the film showed none. A single word changed what was remembered.

**False memories.** In later research by Loftus and colleagues, participants were given accounts of childhood events, one of them invented (such as being lost in a shopping mall), described as coming from family members. Across repeated interviews, a substantial minority came to recall at least part of the false event, sometimes in detail.

**Source amnesia** (source misattribution) is remembering something but getting its source wrong: being sure you read a fact in the news when a friend told you, or that an event happened to you when you only heard about it.

**Imagination inflation**: repeatedly imagining an event makes it feel familiar, and familiarity is mistaken for memory, increasing confidence that it really happened.

**Flashbulb memories** of surprising, emotional events feel vivid and certain. But when researchers asked people about a major news event the day after it happened and again years later, many later accounts had changed substantially while confidence stayed high. Vividness and confidence are not accuracy.

**Worked example 2: eyewitness testimony.** A witness to a robbery is asked, "Did you see the gun in the robber's left hand?" No gun was mentioned before. The question presupposes a gun, which can be absorbed into the memory (misinformation effect). Later, the witness may be unsure whether the gun was seen or suggested (source amnesia), and imagining the scene repeatedly while preparing to testify could raise confidence (imagination inflation). Good practice: open-ended questions ("Describe what you saw"), interviewing witnesses separately, and recording confidence at the first identification.

## Research methods spotlight: studying the misinformation effect

**Worked example 3: an experiment with a control group.** In a hypothetical study, 90 students watch a short video of a bicycle theft in which the thief wears a gray jacket. They are randomly assigned to read one of three summaries: one says "the thief's blue jacket" (misleading), one says "the thief's gray jacket" (accurate), one does not mention the jacket (neutral). A week later, all are asked, "What color was the thief's jacket?"

| Summary read | n | Reported gray | Reported blue | Other or unsure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Misleading (blue) | 30 | 12 | 14 | 4 |
| Accurate (gray) | 30 | 25 | 1 | 4 |
| Neutral | 30 | 19 | 2 | 9 |

- *IV:* the content of the summary. *DV:* the reported jacket color, operationally defined as the color written in answer to the question.
- *Role of the neutral group:* it shows what memory looks like with no post-event information. Comparing the misleading group with it, and not only with the accurate group, shows that the misleading summary harmed memory (14 of 30 reported blue against 2 of 30).
- *Ethics:* the misleading summary is mild deception. The IRB would weigh the minimal risk against the study's value, and participants must be debriefed, told about the misleading detail, and given the chance to withdraw their data.
- *Generalizability:* a jacket color in a short video is low-stakes; real crimes involve stress, weapons and longer delays, which could make the effect larger or smaller.

## Common mistakes

- **Reversing proactive and retroactive.** Name the direction of the damage: *pro* = forward (old harms new); *retro* = backward (new harms old).
- **Calling encoding failure "forgetting from memory".** The information was never stored.
- **Equating confidence with accuracy.** Flashbulb memories and false memories can both feel certain.
- **Treating repression as established fact.** It is a contested idea, and suggestive "memory recovery" can create false memories.
