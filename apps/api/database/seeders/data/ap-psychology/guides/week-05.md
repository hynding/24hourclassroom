## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Consciousness | Awareness of ourselves and our environment. |
| Circadian rhythm | The body's roughly 24-hour cycle of alertness, temperature and hormone release. |
| Suprachiasmatic nucleus | A cluster of neurons in the hypothalamus that acts as the body's master clock, set by light. |
| NREM sleep | Non-rapid-eye-movement sleep, stages 1 to 3, from light to deep. |
| REM sleep | Rapid-eye-movement sleep: an active brain, vivid dreams and paralyzed large muscles. |
| Sleep cycle | One pass through the NREM stages and REM, about 90 minutes. |
| REM rebound | More REM sleep than usual after a period of REM deprivation. |
| Activation-synthesis theory | Dreams are the brain's attempt to make sense of random neural activity during REM. |
| Consolidation theory of dreams | Dreaming reflects the brain processing and storing the day's memories. |

## Consciousness and the body clock

**Consciousness** is your awareness of yourself and your surroundings. It is not all-or-nothing: you move between alert attention, daydreams, drowsiness, sleep and dreams every day. Sleep is the most regular of these changes.

**Circadian rhythm.** Body temperature, alertness and hormones rise and fall on a roughly 24-hour cycle. Light striking the retina signals the **suprachiasmatic nucleus** (SCN) in the hypothalamus. In the evening, as light fades, the SCN lets the pineal gland release **melatonin**, which makes you sleepy; morning light suppresses it. Bright screens late at night can delay this signal.

**When the clock is out of step.** **Jet lag** happens when you cross time zones faster than your clock can reset; flying east (losing hours) is usually harder than flying west, because it is easier to stay up later than to fall asleep earlier. **Shift work**, especially rotating night shifts, keeps the clock in conflict with daylight and is linked to poorer sleep and more accidents. Teenagers' clocks also shift: during adolescence the release of melatonin tends to come later in the evening, so many teens feel alert late at night and groggy at early school start times.

## The stages of sleep

Sleep stages were mapped by recording the brain's electrical activity with the **EEG**, along with eye movements and muscle tone. In 1953 Eugene Aserinsky and Nathaniel Kleitman reported the periods of rapid eye movement that came to be called REM sleep.

| Stage | What happens | EEG pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Awake, relaxed | Eyes closed, drifting | Alpha waves (slow-ish, regular) |
| NREM 1 | Light sleep, easily woken; may feel falling or see fleeting images (hypnagogic sensations) | Theta waves |
| NREM 2 | Clearly asleep; body temperature and heart rate fall | Sleep spindles (bursts of rapid waves) and K-complexes |
| NREM 3 | Deep, slow-wave sleep; hardest to wake; growth hormone released | Delta waves (large, slow) |
| REM | Rapid eye movements, vivid dreams, large muscles paralyzed; heart rate and breathing irregular | Fast, wake-like waves |

REM is called **paradoxical sleep**: the brain is highly active and the body is almost motionless. The paralysis (atonia) keeps you from acting out dreams.

**The night's pattern.** A sleep cycle lasts about **90 minutes**, so a full night holds four to six of them. Early cycles contain more deep NREM 3; later cycles contain longer REM periods. That is why cutting a night short costs you mostly REM, and why people often wake from a dream in the morning.

**Worked example: a hypnogram in words.** A hypothetical 8-hour night: the sleeper reaches NREM 3 within 30 minutes and spends about 40 minutes there; the first REM period, about 80 to 90 minutes after falling asleep, lasts 10 minutes. By the fifth cycle there is no NREM 3 at all, and the REM period lasts about 35 minutes. Summary: deep sleep front-loaded, REM back-loaded.

## Why we sleep

Several functions are supported by research, and they are not exclusive:

- **Restoration:** sleep helps repair tissue and clear waste products that build up in the brain during waking hours.
- **Memory consolidation:** sleep strengthens and stabilizes memories of the day; people who sleep after learning remember more than those who stay awake for the same time.
- **Growth:** the pituitary releases growth hormone mainly during deep sleep, which matters for children and teens.
- **Protection and energy saving:** an evolutionary account. Sleeping through dark hours kept ancestors still and safe when they could not see well.

**Sleep loss** impairs attention, mood, memory and reaction time; it weakens the immune system and makes drowsy driving dangerous. After a period without enough REM sleep, people show **REM rebound**: they enter REM sooner and spend more time in it, which suggests the brain actively needs REM.

## Why we dream

- **Activation-synthesis theory** (proposed by J. Allan Hobson and Robert McCarley in 1977): during REM, the brainstem sends bursts of random neural activity upward, and the cortex weaves them into a story. Dreams are the brain's attempt to make sense of noise.
- **Consolidation theory:** dreaming is part of processing and storing memories. Dreams often contain fragments of recent experiences, and REM sleep supports some kinds of learning.

The theories can coexist: random activation may supply raw material, and memory processing may shape it. (Freud's view that dreams disguise hidden wishes is discussed with psychodynamic theory in Unit 4; it has little scientific support.)

## Sleep disorders

| Disorder | What it is | Key feature |
|---|---|---|
| Insomnia | Persistent trouble falling or staying asleep | The most common sleep complaint |
| Narcolepsy | Sudden, uncontrollable sleep attacks, often straight into REM | May include brief loss of muscle tone during strong emotion |
| Sleep apnea | Breathing stops repeatedly during sleep, briefly waking the sleeper | Loud snoring; daytime sleepiness; often undiagnosed |
| REM sleep behavior disorder | REM paralysis fails, so the sleeper acts out dreams | Can cause injury to the sleeper or a bed partner |
| Somnambulism (sleepwalking) | Walking or doing routine actions while asleep | Happens in deep NREM 3 sleep; more common in children |

Notice the timing clue: sleepwalking (and night terrors) occur in **NREM 3**, early in the night, while acting out dreams happens in **REM**, mostly later.

**Data practice.** A hypothetical sleep diary (invented for this course) records one student's school-night sleep for 7 nights: 6.0, 6.5, 6.5, 7.0, 5.5, 6.5, 9.0 hours. Ordered: 5.5, 6.0, 6.5, 6.5, 6.5, 7.0, 9.0. Mean = 47 ÷ 7 ≈ 6.7 hours; median = 6.5; mode = 6.5; range = 3.5 hours. The one long night (9.0) pulls the mean above the median: a positive skew. Recommendations for teens are about 8 to 10 hours, so even the mean falls short.

## Common mistakes

- **Putting dreams only in NREM.** Vivid, story-like dreams are mostly in REM (NREM dreams are shorter and vaguer).
- **Mixing up sleepwalking and REM sleep behavior disorder.** Sleepwalking: NREM 3. Acting out dreams: REM.
- **Thinking the cycle repeats identically.** Deep sleep shrinks and REM grows as the night goes on.
- **"REM means the brain is resting."** The REM brain is about as active as when awake.
- **Treating one theory of dreaming as proven.** Each has support and limits.

## Self-check

1. Which stage has the slowest, largest brain waves, and what happens there?
2. Why does REM rebound suggest REM is important?
3. After a flight from Los Angeles to New York, why might you struggle to fall asleep?
4. A man stops breathing repeatedly at night and snores loudly. Which disorder fits?
5. How does the activation-synthesis theory explain a bizarre dream?
6. What is the median of 7, 5, 8, 6, 9 hours of sleep?

*Answers:* 1. NREM 3 (delta waves): deep sleep, growth hormone release, hardest to wake. 2. After being deprived of REM, the brain makes up for it, as if REM meets a need. 3. Your clock is still on Pacific time, three hours behind, so you are not yet sleepy at local bedtime. 4. Sleep apnea. 5. Random brainstem activity during REM is stitched into a story by the cortex, so odd jumps are expected. 6. Ordered 5, 6, 7, 8, 9: median 7 hours.
