## Before you start

As in Week 30, the goal is understanding, not diagnosis: only a trained clinician can diagnose. Several disorders this week are widely misunderstood, often because of films and slang. Schizophrenia is not "split personality"; people with schizophrenia are far more often victims of violence than perpetrators; eating disorders affect people of every gender, body size and background. We describe symptoms in general terms and never give details about harmful behaviors.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Positive symptoms | Added experiences: hallucinations, delusions, disorganized speech or behavior. |
| Negative symptoms | Reductions: flat affect, avolition, alogia, social withdrawal. |
| Hallucination | A perception-like experience with no external stimulus. |
| Delusion | A fixed false belief held despite clear evidence. |
| Dissociation | A break in the usual integration of memory, identity, awareness or perception. |
| Somatic symptom disorder | Distressing physical symptoms with excessive thoughts, feelings or behaviors about them. |
| Personality disorder | An enduring, inflexible pattern of experience and behavior that causes distress or impairment. |
| Neurodevelopmental disorder | A condition whose differences appear early in development, such as ADHD or autism. |

## Schizophrenia spectrum disorders

**Schizophrenia** disrupts thinking, perception, emotion and behavior. A diagnosis requires two or more of the following (at least one from the first three), with signs of disturbance lasting at least six months:

| Symptom | Type | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Delusions | Positive | Believing neighbors are plotting against you (persecutory), or that you have special powers (grandiose) |
| Hallucinations | Positive | Hearing voices no one else hears (the most common kind) |
| Disorganized speech | Positive | Jumping between unrelated ideas ("word salad") |
| Disorganized or catatonic behavior | Positive / motor | Unpredictable actions; holding a rigid pose or not responding |
| Negative symptoms | Negative | Flat affect, avolition (little motivation), alogia (little speech) |

**Positive** means *added* to typical experience, not "good"; **negative** means *taken away*. Negative symptoms often affect daily functioning the most and respond least to medication.

Schizophrenia usually begins in **late adolescence or early adulthood**, somewhat earlier on average in men. The **spectrum** also includes delusional disorder, brief psychotic disorder, schizoaffective disorder and schizotypal personality disorder.

### What contributes to schizophrenia

- **Genes.** If one identical twin has schizophrenia, the other also has it in roughly a third to a half of cases (estimates vary by study, with classic studies near 50% and recent national registries nearer 33%), compared with about 10–15% for fraternal twins. Adoption studies point the same way. But identical twins share nearly all their genes, so the many discordant pairs show that genes are not destiny.
- **Prenatal and early factors.** Maternal infection or malnutrition during pregnancy and complications at birth raise risk.
- **Dopamine.** The **dopamine hypothesis** holds that overactive dopamine signaling in some pathways contributes to positive symptoms: drugs that block dopamine receptors reduce them, and drugs that boost dopamine can produce psychosis-like symptoms.
- **Brain structure and function.** On average, people with schizophrenia show **enlarged ventricles**, less gray matter in some regions and **reduced frontal-lobe activity** during tasks.
- **Stress.** Life stress can trigger onset or relapse in people already vulnerable: **diathesis-stress** again.

**Worked example 1: reading a brain-imaging comparison** (invented for this course). Researchers measured ventricle volume in 40 adults with schizophrenia (mean 24.0 mL, SD 7.5) and 40 comparison adults matched on age, sex and education (mean 18.0 mL, SD 6.0), p = .001.

- **Design.** Diagnosis cannot be randomly assigned, so this is a **nonexperimental comparison**; it shows a difference, not a cause. Medication, years of illness or other factors could contribute.
- **Matching** on age, sex and education removes those as confounds.
- **Overlap.** The means differ by less than one standard deviation, so the two distributions overlap a lot. A scan cannot diagnose an individual; the difference is a **group average**.

## Dissociative disorders

**Dissociation** is a disruption in the usual integration of memory, identity, awareness or perception.

- **Dissociative amnesia**: inability to recall important personal information, usually about a traumatic or stressful event, not explained by brain injury or ordinary forgetting. Compare **retrograde amnesia** (Topic 2.7), memory loss for events before a brain injury. A **dissociative fugue**, unplanned travel with loss of identity, is a rare form.
- **Dissociative identity disorder (DID)**: two or more distinct identities or personality states, with gaps in memory for everyday events. It was formerly called multiple personality disorder and is rare. Researchers debate its origins: one view links it to severe, repeated childhood trauma; a **sociocognitive** view argues that therapist suggestion and cultural portrayals can shape how symptoms appear.
- **Depersonalization/derealization disorder**: persistent feelings of being detached from one's body or mind, or that the world is unreal, while knowing these feelings are not literally true.

## Somatic symptom and related disorders

These involve physical symptoms or health worries with significant distress. The symptoms are **real experiences, not faked**.

- **Somatic symptom disorder**: one or more distressing physical symptoms, with excessive thoughts, feelings or behaviors about them, such as constant worry or many hours spent on them.
- **Illness anxiety disorder**: preoccupation with having or getting a serious illness, with few or no symptoms, and worry that persists despite reassurance.
- **Functional neurological symptom disorder** (conversion disorder): neurological symptoms such as weakness, numbness or loss of vision that are inconsistent with recognized neurological disease.

## Feeding and eating disorders

Eating disorders are serious conditions, not choices or phases.

- **Anorexia nervosa**: restriction of food intake leading to significantly low body weight, an intense fear of gaining weight, and a disturbed view of one's body. It has one of the highest mortality rates of any psychological disorder.
- **Bulimia nervosa**: recurrent episodes of binge eating with loss of control, followed by recurrent compensatory behaviors, with self-worth tied heavily to body shape and weight.
- **Binge-eating disorder**: recurrent binge episodes with loss of control and marked distress, **without** regular compensatory behaviors. It is the most common eating disorder in the U.S.

Contributing factors span the biopsychosocial model: **genetic** influence (eating disorders run in families), **psychological** traits such as perfectionism and low self-esteem, and **sociocultural** pressures such as idealized body images in media and in sports that emphasize weight. They occur across genders, ethnic groups and body sizes; a person does not have to look ill to be seriously ill.

## Personality disorders

A **personality disorder** is an enduring, **inflexible** pattern of inner experience and behavior that departs from cultural expectations, begins by adolescence or early adulthood, stays stable over time, and causes distress or impairment. The DSM-5-TR groups them in three clusters:

| Cluster | Description | Disorders |
|---|---|---|
| A | Odd or eccentric | Paranoid, schizoid, schizotypal |
| B | Dramatic, emotional or erratic | Antisocial, borderline, histrionic, narcissistic |
| C | Anxious or fearful | Avoidant, dependent, obsessive-compulsive |

- **Antisocial personality disorder**: a pervasive pattern of disregard for others' rights (deceitfulness, impulsivity, aggression, lack of remorse). It is diagnosed only at 18 or older, with signs of conduct disorder before age 15. Research links it to genetic influences, low physiological arousal and differences in frontal-lobe functioning, interacting with harsh or chaotic environments.
- **Borderline personality disorder**: instability in relationships, self-image and emotions, marked impulsivity and intense fear of abandonment. Many people with it improve substantially over time, especially with specialized therapy.

Note that **obsessive-compulsive personality disorder** (a rigid preoccupation with order and control) differs from OCD, which involves obsessions and compulsions.

## Neurodevelopmental disorders

These conditions begin in early development.

- **Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)**: a persistent pattern of **inattention** and/or **hyperactivity-impulsivity** that interferes with functioning, with several symptoms before age 12 and in two or more settings. It is strongly heritable. Everyone is distracted sometimes; ADHD involves a persistent, impairing pattern.
- **Autism spectrum disorder**: persistent differences in **social communication and interaction**, plus **restricted, repetitive** behaviors, interests or activities, present from early development. "Spectrum" reflects how widely abilities and support needs vary. Many autistic people prefer identity-first language ("autistic person"); follow the person's own preference.

**Worked example 2: research ethics and the vaccine myth.** In 1998 a medical journal published a small study claiming a link between a childhood vaccine and autism. Investigations found the data had been manipulated; the paper was **retracted** in 2010 and its lead author lost his medical license. Large studies of many children since then have found **no link** between vaccines and autism. The episode shows why science depends on honesty, **replication** and peer review, and how one fraudulent study can do lasting public harm.

## Common mistakes

- **Equating schizophrenia with "split personality".** That confusion mixes up schizophrenia with dissociative identity disorder.
- **Reading "positive symptoms" as good.** Positive means added.
- **Treating somatic symptoms as faked.**
- **Mixing up bulimia nervosa and binge-eating disorder.** The difference is recurrent compensatory behavior.
- **Placing personality disorders in the wrong cluster.** Antisocial and borderline are Cluster B; avoidant and dependent are Cluster C.
- **Inferring cause from a brain difference or a correlation.**

2. Name two pieces of evidence for the dopamine hypothesis.
3. How does dissociative amnesia differ from retrograde amnesia?
4. A person with no physical symptoms worries for months that she has a serious undiagnosed illness, despite normal tests. Which disorder fits best?
5. Which cluster contains avoidant personality disorder?
6. What are the two core features of autism spectrum disorder?
