## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Attachment | An enduring emotional bond between a child and a caregiver. |
| Contact comfort | The comfort an infant gets from soft physical contact. |
| Strange Situation | Ainsworth's laboratory procedure for observing attachment in toddlers. |
| Stranger anxiety | Fear of unfamiliar people, typically emerging around 8 months. |
| Temperament | A person's characteristic emotional reactivity and intensity, visible from infancy. |
| Authoritative parenting | High warmth combined with clear, reasoned rules. |
| Psychosocial stage | One of Erikson's eight stages, each centered on a social crisis. |
| Emerging adulthood | The period from about 18 to the mid-20s between adolescence and full adult independence. |

## Attachment (3.6)

**Harlow's monkeys.** In the 1950s, Harry Harlow raised infant rhesus monkeys with two artificial "mothers": one made of bare wire that held a bottle of milk, and one covered in soft cloth that gave no food. If attachment were simply a response to being fed, the infants should have bonded with the wire mother. Instead they spent most of their time clinging to the cloth mother, went to the wire mother only to feed, and ran to the cloth mother when frightened. Harlow concluded that **contact comfort**, not nourishment, is central to attachment. Monkeys raised in isolation showed serious social and emotional problems later, which also made the research ethically controversial: it caused lasting distress to the animals, and today's animal research guidelines require that any harm be justified and minimized.

**Bowlby and the secure base.** Psychiatrist John Bowlby argued that attachment is an evolved system that keeps infants close to caregivers. A securely attached child uses the caregiver as a **secure base** from which to explore. **Stranger anxiety** (wariness of unfamiliar people) typically appears around 8 months, about when infants form schemas for familiar faces; **separation anxiety** (distress when the caregiver leaves) appears around the same time, usually peaks early in the second year and fades through the toddler years.

**Ainsworth's Strange Situation.** In the 1970s, Mary Ainsworth designed a laboratory procedure for children about 1 year old: the child plays with the caregiver present, a stranger enters, the caregiver leaves briefly and returns, and the child is briefly left alone. Observers code the child's exploration and especially the reunion.

| Attachment pattern | Behavior in the Strange Situation |
|---|---|
| Secure | Explores using the caregiver as a base, may be upset at separation, is quickly comforted at reunion. |
| Insecure-avoidant | Shows little distress at separation and ignores or avoids the caregiver at reunion. |
| Insecure-anxious (resistant or ambivalent) | Clings and explores little, is very distressed at separation, and is hard to comfort, often mixing contact-seeking with anger. |
| Disorganized | Shows confused or contradictory behavior at reunion (a pattern added by later researchers). |

Secure attachment is associated with **sensitive, responsive caregiving**. In longitudinal studies, secure attachment in infancy predicts somewhat better peer relationships and emotional regulation later, but the correlations are modest, and later experiences can change a child's path. Distributions of attachment patterns also differ across cultures, so the Strange Situation may not mean exactly the same thing everywhere.

## Temperament (3.6)

**Temperament** is a baby's characteristic emotional style: activity level, mood, intensity, and how readily the baby adapts to new things. In a classic longitudinal study, Alexander Thomas and Stella Chess described three broad types: **easy** (regular, positive, adaptable), **difficult** (irregular, intense, negative to new things) and **slow-to-warm-up** (mildly negative at first, adapting gradually). Temperament is partly heritable (twin studies, 1.1) and moderately stable (3.1).

Temperament interacts with caregiving. **Goodness of fit**, the match between a child's temperament and the demands of the environment, matters: a difficult baby with patient, consistent caregivers often does well. A child's temperament also shapes how others respond, another gene-environment interaction.

## Parenting styles (3.6)

Psychologist Diana Baumrind described parenting along two dimensions, warmth (responsiveness) and control (demands):

| Style | Warmth | Demands | Typical approach |
|---|---|---|---|
| Authoritative | High | High | Clear rules explained with reasons; open to discussion. |
| Authoritarian | Low | High | Strict rules, obedience expected, little explanation. |
| Permissive | High | Low | Warm, few rules or limits. |
| Uninvolved (neglectful) | Low | Low | Little warmth or supervision; added by later researchers. |

In many studies, mostly of North American families, authoritative parenting is **associated** with higher self-esteem, social competence and school achievement. Two cautions matter for the AP exam. First, these studies are **correlational**: an easygoing child may draw out reasoned, warm parenting (reverse causation), and family income or parents' own traits could affect both parenting and outcomes (third variables). Second, the associations vary by **culture**; strict parenting means different things in different cultural contexts.

**Worked example 1: a hypothetical correlation.** A survey of 600 teenagers finds that parental warmth scores correlate r = +0.40 with self-esteem scores. A headline says, "Warm parents raise confident kids." The data are consistent with that, but confident, cheerful teens may also make it easier for parents to be warm, and stressful family circumstances could lower both. A longitudinal design measuring warmth before self-esteem, or an experiment randomly assigning families to a parenting program, would support a causal claim better.

## Erikson's psychosocial stages (3.6)

Erik Erikson proposed that each stage of life poses a **psychosocial crisis**, a social challenge whose resolution shapes development.

| Stage | Approximate age | Crisis | Healthy outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Infancy (to about 1) | Trust versus mistrust | Trust that needs will be met |
| 2 | Toddlerhood (1–3) | Autonomy versus shame and doubt | Confidence in doing things alone |
| 3 | Preschool (3–6) | Initiative versus guilt | Willingness to start tasks and plans |
| 4 | Elementary school (6–puberty) | Competence (industry) versus inferiority | Pride in skills and work |
| 5 | Adolescence | Identity versus role confusion | A coherent sense of self |
| 6 | Young adulthood | Intimacy versus isolation | Close, committed relationships |
| 7 | Middle adulthood | Generativity versus stagnation | Contributing to the next generation |
| 8 | Late adulthood | Integrity versus despair | Satisfaction looking back on life |

Erikson's theory is broad and influential but hard to test precisely, because its concepts are difficult to operationally define and the ages are approximate.

## Adolescence and emerging adulthood (3.6)

**Identity.** Adolescents try out roles, values and goals. Researcher James Marcia described identity in terms of **exploration** and **commitment**: identity achievement (explored, then committed), moratorium (exploring, not yet committed), foreclosure (committed without exploring, often to parents' values) and diffusion (neither exploring nor committed).

**Peers and parents.** Peer influence grows in adolescence, and conflict with parents over everyday matters often rises in early adolescence, yet most teens remain close to their parents on core values.

**Emerging adulthood.** In many industrialized societies, the years from about 18 to the mid-20s, a phase psychologist Jeffrey Arnett named emerging adulthood, are a time of continued exploration in work and relationships before taking on full adult roles.

## Ecological systems theory (3.6)

Urie Bronfenbrenner described development as shaped by nested systems:

- **Microsystem:** direct settings (family, classroom, peers).
- **Mesosystem:** links among microsystems (parents' contact with teachers).
- **Exosystem:** settings the child is not in that still affect them (a parent's workplace schedule).
- **Macrosystem:** cultural values, laws and economic conditions.
- **Chronosystem:** change over time (a family move, a historical event).

**Adverse childhood experiences** (such as abuse, neglect or household instability) are associated with later health and emotional problems, but many children show **resilience**, adapting well because of supportive relationships and other protective factors.

## Adulthood and later life (3.6)

Adult development centers on **love and work**: forming close relationships, raising or mentoring others and contributing through work (Erikson's intimacy and generativity). Many cultures have a **social clock**, expected timing for events such as marriage or retirement, though it has loosened. Contrary to stereotypes, life satisfaction does not plunge in old age; many older adults report as much or more well-being as younger adults, focusing on fewer, emotionally closer relationships and paying relatively more attention to positive information. Coping with loss and facing death are part of late adulthood (Erikson's integrity versus despair).

**Worked example 2: designing an ethical attachment study.** A research team wants to know whether a home-visiting program for new parents increases secure attachment. It randomly assigns 200 volunteer families to receive visits or standard care, then uses the Strange Situation at 12 months with coders blind to group. Ethical safeguards: informed consent from parents, brief separations that end early if a child becomes very distressed, confidentiality, and offering the program to the control families after the study.

## Common mistakes

- **Saying Harlow showed feeding causes attachment.** He showed the opposite: contact comfort mattered more than food.
- **Mixing avoidant and anxious attachment.** Avoidant children ignore the caregiver at reunion; anxious children cling and resist comfort.
- **Confusing authoritarian with authoritative.** Authoritative is warm and firm; authoritarian is firm without warmth.
- **Calling parenting research causal.** Most of it is correlational.
- **Misordering Erikson.** Learn the order with ages; identity versus role confusion is adolescence.

## Self-check

1. What did the wire and cloth mother studies show?
2. A toddler barely notices when his mother leaves and turns away when she returns. Which pattern is this?
3. Name the parenting style with high warmth and few rules.
4. Which Erikson crisis does a 45-year-old mentoring new employees face?
5. A city cuts its bus routes, so a parent's commute doubles and family dinners stop. Which ecological system is involved?

*Answers:* 1. Infant monkeys preferred the soft cloth mother, especially when frightened, so contact comfort matters more than feeding. 2. Insecure-avoidant. 3. Permissive. 4. Generativity versus stagnation. 5. The exosystem (or macrosystem for the city's policy), affecting the child's microsystem.
