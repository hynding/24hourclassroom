## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Generalized anxiety disorder | Excessive, hard-to-control worry about many things, most days, six months or more. |
| Panic disorder | Recurrent unexpected panic attacks plus persistent fear of more. |
| Specific phobia | Intense, out-of-proportion fear of a particular object or situation. |
| Agoraphobia | Fear of situations where escape or help might be hard to get. |
| Social anxiety disorder | Intense fear of being judged in social situations. |
| Obsessions / compulsions | Intrusive, unwanted thoughts / repetitive acts performed to reduce the anxiety they cause. |
| PTSD | Intrusions, avoidance, negative mood changes and heightened arousal for over a month after trauma. |
| Major depressive disorder | Two weeks or more of depressed mood or lost interest, plus other symptoms. |
| Bipolar I / bipolar II | At least one manic episode / hypomanic plus major depressive episodes, never full mania. |
| Learned helplessness | Giving up after repeated uncontrollable events, even when control returns. |

## Anxiety disorders

Anxiety is a normal, useful emotion: it prepares you to face a threat. In an **anxiety disorder**, fear or worry is out of proportion to the actual danger, persists, and interferes with life.

- **Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD)**: excessive, hard-to-control **worry about many everyday matters** (grades, health, family, money) on most days for at least six months, with restlessness, fatigue, poor concentration, muscle tension or poor sleep.
- **Panic disorder**: recurrent, **unexpected panic attacks** (sudden surges of intense fear peaking within minutes, with pounding heart, breathlessness, dizziness or a sense of losing control) followed by at least a month of worry about the next attack or changes in behavior to avoid one. A single panic attack is not panic disorder; many people have one at some point.
- **Specific phobia**: intense fear of a **particular object or situation** (heights, spiders, needles, flying), out of proportion to the danger, leading to avoidance.
- **Agoraphobia**: fear and avoidance of situations where **escape might be difficult** or help unavailable if panic-like symptoms struck: crowds, public transit, being outside the home alone.
- **Social anxiety disorder**: intense fear of **being scrutinized or judged** in social situations, such as speaking in class or eating in front of others.

**Worked example 1: panic or GAD?** Kai has had several sudden, intense episodes of fear with a racing heart, peaking within minutes, and now worries about the next one: **panic disorder**. Rosa worries steadily, most days, about grades, family and friendships, and feels tense and tired: **GAD**. The clue is the time course: sudden peaks versus a constant hum.

### What contributes to anxiety disorders

- **Learning.** In 1920 John Watson and Rosalie Rayner conditioned an infant known as "Little Albert" to fear a white rat by pairing it with a loud noise; the fear generalized to other furry objects. The study is now viewed as unethical: an infant was deliberately frightened and the fear was never removed. Operant conditioning explains why fears last: **avoidance** brings relief, so it is **negatively reinforced**, and the person never learns the situation is safe. Observational learning adds more: children can pick up fears by watching others.
- **Evolution.** We learn some fears (snakes, heights, spiders) far more readily than others (cars, outlets), an idea called **biological preparedness**: fearing these once helped people survive.
- **Biology.** Anxiety disorders run in families, and twin studies point to moderate heritability. The **amygdala** tends to respond more strongly to threat cues in many people with anxiety disorders.
- **Cognition.** People with anxiety disorders tend to overestimate danger and underestimate their ability to cope, and to pay extra attention to possible threats.

## Obsessive-compulsive and related disorders

DSM-5 moved **obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD)** out of the anxiety disorders into its own group.

- **Obsessions** are recurrent, unwanted, intrusive thoughts, urges or images that cause distress (fear of contamination, fear of having left something unsafe, a need for exactness).
- **Compulsions** are repetitive behaviors or mental acts (washing, checking, counting, ordering) the person feels driven to perform to reduce that distress. They bring brief relief, which negatively reinforces them, and they can take hours a day.

Related disorders include **hoarding disorder** (persistent difficulty discarding possessions, so that living spaces become cluttered and hard to use), **body dysmorphic disorder** (preoccupation with perceived flaws in appearance that others do not notice or see as slight), and trichotillomania (hair-pulling disorder) and excoriation (skin-picking) disorder. Everyday phrases like "I'm so OCD about my desk" trivialize a condition that can be disabling; avoid them.

## Trauma- and stressor-related disorders

**Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD)** can follow exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence, experienced directly, witnessed or learned about happening to someone close. Its four symptom groups, lasting **more than a month**:

1. **Intrusions**: unwanted memories, nightmares, flashbacks.
2. **Avoidance** of reminders.
3. **Negative changes in thinking and mood**: guilt, numbness, detachment.
4. **Heightened arousal and reactivity**: hypervigilance, being easily startled, poor sleep, irritability.

Similar symptoms lasting from three days to a month are called **acute stress disorder**.

Crucially, **most people exposed to trauma do not develop PTSD**. Risk rises with the severity and repetition of the trauma, earlier trauma and little social support, and falls with protective factors. This is the diathesis-stress model and Week 28's resilience research at work.

## Depressive disorders

- **Major depressive disorder**: at least **two weeks** of depressed mood or **loss of interest or pleasure**, plus other symptoms such as changes in sleep or appetite, fatigue, feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt, difficulty concentrating, or recurrent thoughts of death, causing distress or impairment. It is far more than ordinary sadness.
- **Persistent depressive disorder**: a depressed mood on most days for at least **two years** in adults (one year in children and adolescents), often milder but long-lasting.
- A **seasonal pattern** describes depressive episodes that regularly begin at one time of year, most often fall or winter.

On average, women are diagnosed with depression about twice as often as men, a gap that appears in adolescence.

### What contributes to depression

- **Biology**: heritability; differences in neurotransmitter systems such as **serotonin** and **norepinephrine**; stress-hormone activity.
- **Learned helplessness**: Martin Seligman found that dogs exposed to shocks they could not escape later failed to escape even when they could, and proposed that repeated uncontrollable events teach people that effort is pointless. (The animal research itself raises ethical questions about inflicting distress.)
- **Cognition**: Aaron Beck's **cognitive triad** (negative views of the self, the world and the future), and a **pessimistic explanatory style** that explains bad events as internal ("my fault"), stable ("always") and global ("in everything").
- **Rumination**: Susan Nolen-Hoeksema found that repetitively dwelling on distress, without moving toward solutions, predicts longer and more severe depressive episodes.
- **Social factors**: stressful life events, loss, loneliness, poverty and discrimination.

**Worked example 2: a hypothetical longitudinal study** (invented for this course). Two hundred students aged 14 to 16 took a rumination questionnaire in September and a depressive-symptoms checklist a year later.

| September rumination quartile | Share with high symptom scores a year later |
|---|---|
| Lowest 25% | 6% |
| Second 25% | 10% |
| Third 25% | 16% |
| Highest 25% | 30% |

- **Design**: longitudinal and correlational; nothing was manipulated.
- **Percentile rank**: a student at the 80th percentile scored at or above about 80% of the sample, so she is in the top quartile.
- **Interpretation**: rumination came **before** the later symptoms, which rules out one kind of reverse causation, but a third variable (such as ongoing family stress) could still drive both.
- **Ethics**: the consent form told families that any student reporting thoughts of self-harm would be connected with the school counselor. Participant safety outweighs strict confidentiality, and informed consent means saying so in advance.

## Bipolar disorders

- A **manic episode** is at least a week of abnormally elevated, expansive or irritable mood and increased energy, with symptoms such as inflated self-esteem, **decreased need for sleep**, racing thoughts, rapid speech and risky decisions, causing marked impairment.
- **Hypomania** is milder and shorter (at least four days), noticeable to others but without severe impairment.
- **Bipolar I disorder** requires at least one **manic** episode. **Bipolar II disorder** requires at least one **hypomanic** episode and at least one **major depressive** episode, with no full manic episode.

Bipolar disorders are among the most **heritable** psychological disorders. They have nothing to do with "split personalities" or ordinary mood swings.

**Worked example 3.** A person with two past depressive episodes has a ten-day period of soaring mood, sleeping two hours a night without tiredness, rapid speech and spending all his savings, leading to hospitalization. That episode is mania, so the diagnosis is **bipolar I**, not bipolar II.

## A word on safety

Depressive and bipolar disorders raise the risk of suicide. Talk of wanting to die, feeling hopeless or being a burden is always taken seriously. If you notice this in yourself or a friend, tell a trusted adult right away. You are not breaking a friend's trust by getting help; you may be protecting their life.

## Common mistakes

- **Calling one panic attack panic disorder.** The disorder needs recurrent attacks plus persistent worry about them.
- **Swapping obsessions and compulsions.** Thoughts versus acts.
- **Assuming trauma always leads to PTSD.**
- **Mixing up bipolar I and II.** Full mania means bipolar I.
- **Confusing bipolar disorder with dissociative identity disorder.**
- **Treating correlational findings (rumination, explanatory style) as proof of cause.**

## Self-check

1. What distinguishes agoraphobia from social anxiety disorder?
2. A person washes his hands 40 times a day to quiet fears of germs. Which part is the compulsion?
3. How long must PTSD symptoms last?
4. Bipolar II requires which two kinds of episodes?
5. Name the three parts of Beck's cognitive triad.
6. Why does avoidance keep a phobia going?

*Answers:* 1. Agoraphobia is fear of places where escape or help might be hard; social anxiety disorder is fear of being judged by others. 2. The handwashing (the fear of germs is the obsession). 3. More than one month. 4. At least one hypomanic and at least one major depressive episode. 5. Negative views of the self, the world and the future. 6. It is negatively reinforced by relief, and the person never learns the situation is safe.

If you or someone you know is struggling, talk to a trusted adult, or call or text 988 (the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline in the U.S.).
