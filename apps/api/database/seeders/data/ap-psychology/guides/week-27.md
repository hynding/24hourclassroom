## Health psychology and the biopsychosocial approach

Health psychology asks why two people exposed to the same germ, the same exam or the same loss end up in different states of health. Its answer is the **biopsychosocial approach**: health and illness come from biological factors (genes, hormones, immune function), psychological factors (how we appraise events, our emotions, habits such as sleep and exercise) and social-cultural factors (relationships, income, neighborhood safety, discrimination, cultural expectations), all interacting. A student with a strong immune system who sleeps four hours a night and feels isolated may still catch every cold that goes around. No single level explains the outcome.

## What stress is

Psychologists use **stress** for a whole process: a **stressor** occurs, the person **appraises** it, and the body and mind **respond**. Stress is not always bad. **Eustress** is the energizing kind (the nerves before a performance you have prepared for); **distress** is the kind that feels harmful or overwhelming. Recall the Yerkes-Dodson law from Unit 4: moderate arousal often helps performance, while very high arousal hurts it.

### Three kinds of stressors

- **Catastrophes** are large-scale, unpredictable events that threaten many people at once: hurricanes, wildfires, wars, pandemics.
- **Significant life changes** are major personal transitions, welcome or not: moving, a parent's divorce, a death in the family, starting a new school. Holmes and Rahe's **Social Readjustment Rating Scale** gave each event a score in "life change units"; people with high totals over a year were more likely to become ill, a correlational finding.
- **Daily hassles** are the small irritations: a missed bus, a crashed laptop, a noisy home. One at a time they are minor, but they pile up, and their total predicts mood and health surprisingly well.

**Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs)**, such as abuse, neglect or growing up amid household violence or instability, form an important group of stressors. A large health-care study in the 1990s found that adults who reported more ACEs had higher rates of many later health problems. The key words are *associated with*: ACEs raise risk on average; they do not decide anyone's future, and supportive relationships with caring adults buffer their effects.

### Appraisal: the same event, two different stressors

Richard Lazarus argued that events become stressful through **appraisal**. In **primary appraisal** we ask, "Is this a threat, a challenge, or irrelevant to me?" In **secondary appraisal** we ask, "Can I cope with it?"

**Worked example 1: the audition.** Two students sign up for the same theater audition. Ana thinks, "This is my chance; I've practiced the monologue all week" (primary appraisal: challenge; secondary appraisal: I have the resources). Ben thinks, "Everyone will see me forget my lines, and I didn't practice" (threat; I cannot cope). Same event, very different stress responses: Ana's arousal is moderate and helpful, Ben's is high and distracting. Perceived **control** matters too: people who feel some control over a stressor show smaller stress responses than people who feel helpless.

## The body's stress response

When a stressor is appraised as a threat, two systems respond (you met both in Unit 1):

1. **The fast system.** The **sympathetic nervous system** triggers **fight-or-flight**: heart rate, breathing and blood pressure rise, pupils dilate, digestion slows, and the inner part of the adrenal glands (the adrenal medulla) releases **epinephrine** and **norepinephrine**. This happens in seconds.
2. **The slower system: the HPA axis.** The **hypothalamus** signals the **pituitary gland**, which releases a hormone (ACTH) that signals the **adrenal cortex** to release **cortisol**. Cortisol raises blood sugar for energy. This response builds over minutes and lasts longer.

When the stressor passes, the **parasympathetic nervous system** calms the body. Trouble begins when the stressor never passes.

### Selye's general adaptation syndrome

Hans Selye, an endocrinologist who studied animals' reactions to many kinds of stressors, described the same three-stage pattern again and again, which he called the **general adaptation syndrome (GAS)**:

| Stage | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Alarm | Sudden sympathetic arousal; resistance briefly drops as the body mobilizes | A student learns her family must move next month |
| Resistance | The body stays aroused; stress hormones remain high while the person copes | Weeks of packing, goodbyes and paperwork, with steady high energy |
| Exhaustion | Reserves run down; vulnerability to illness, fatigue and low mood rises | After two months she catches every cold and struggles to focus |

### Fight-or-flight is not the only response

Shelley Taylor and colleagues proposed **tend-and-befriend**: under stress, many people (in their account, especially women) respond by caring for others and seeking social connection. The hormone **oxytocin** is linked to this pattern. After a natural disaster, neighbors organizing meals and checking on one another are tending and befriending.

## Stress, the immune system and the heart

**Psychoneuroimmunology** studies how psychological states affect immunity. In brief, emergencies cortisol is useful, but high levels sustained over weeks suppress **lymphocytes**, the white blood cells (B cells and T cells) that fight infection. Researchers have found that:

- volunteers who reported more stress were more likely to develop a cold after being deliberately exposed to a cold virus;
- small wounds healed more slowly in people under long-lasting stress, such as caregivers of a family member with dementia, than in less stressed comparison groups.

Stress also strains the heart. Meyer Friedman and Ray Rosenman described a **Type A** pattern (competitive, hard-driving, impatient, easily angered) and a **Type B** pattern (easygoing, relaxed); Type A men had more coronary heart disease in their early studies. Later research found that the harmful part is mainly **hostility and anger**: repeated anger means repeated surges of blood pressure and stress hormones, which over years can damage blood vessels. Stress can also harm health indirectly, by disrupting sleep, exercise and eating.

## Coping

**Coping** is how we try to reduce stress. Lazarus and Susan Folkman distinguished two broad kinds:

- **Problem-focused coping** acts on the stressor itself: making a study schedule, asking a teacher for help, fixing the flat tire. It works best when the situation is at least partly under our control.
- **Emotion-focused coping** manages our emotional reaction: talking with a friend, exercising, journaling, reframing ("one test does not define me"), mindfulness or music. It is most useful when the stressor cannot be changed, such as a relative's illness. Some emotion-focused strategies, like denial or constant avoidance, help briefly but backfire if they replace action that was possible.

Most people use both. Two buffers show up again and again in the research: **social support** (people with close relationships tend to have lower stress hormone levels and better health) and **aerobic exercise**, which lowers arousal and improves mood.

## Research focus: why stress research is mostly correlational

Researchers cannot ethically assign people to months of serious stress, so most studies measure the stress people already have and relate it to health.

**Worked example 2: hassles and nurse visits.** In a hypothetical study (invented for this course), 120 volunteer juniors logged their daily hassles for eight weeks, and the school nurse's records counted each student's illness visits. The correlation was r = +0.38 (p = .01).

- **Direction and strength:** positive and moderate. Students with more hassles tended to visit the nurse more.
- **Significance:** p = .01 is below .05, so a correlation this large would be unlikely if there were no relationship in the population.
- **Cause?** No. A **third variable** such as short sleep could raise both hassles and illness, and causation could run backward: feeling ill makes small problems feel bigger.
- **Operational definitions:** stress was "average hassles logged per day"; illness was "nurse visits for illness". Each is measurable, though neither captures everything we mean by the words.

When researchers do run **experiments** on stress, they use brief, mild stressors (an unprepared speech, a timed math task) with **informed consent**, the **right to withdraw**, **IRB approval**, **confidentiality** and a full **debriefing**. They also test interventions: a researcher can randomly assign students to an expressive-writing or a neutral-writing condition and compare later health-center visits, which allows a causal conclusion about the writing.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating all stress as bad.** Eustress and moderate arousal can help.
- **Confusing the stressor with stress.** The stressor is the event; stress is the whole appraise-and-respond process.
- **Mixing up the GAS stages.** Alarm is the sudden burst; resistance is sustained coping; exhaustion is the run-down end.
- **Calling cortisol a neurotransmitter.** It is a hormone carried in the blood.
- **Labeling every helpful action problem-focused.** Exercise and talking to friends are healthy, but they are emotion-focused unless they change the stressor itself.
- **Claiming cause from a correlation** between stress and illness.

## Self-check

1. Classify each stressor: a tornado; a sibling borrowing your charger every morning; starting college.
2. Two students hear about a pop quiz. What would make one student's primary appraisal "challenge" and the other's "threat"?
3. Which gland releases cortisol, and which part of the nervous system calms the body afterward?
4. A worker has been under heavy stress for four months and now gets sick often. Which GAS stage fits?
5. Give one problem-focused and one emotion-focused response to a stressful group project.
6. A study finds r = +0.40 between hours of weekly stress and number of colds. Name a third variable.

*Answers:* 1. Catastrophe; daily hassle; significant life change. 2. Feeling prepared and in control (challenge) versus unprepared and helpless (threat). 3. The adrenal cortex; the parasympathetic division. 4. Exhaustion. 5. Problem-focused: divide the tasks and set deadlines with the group; emotion-focused: go for a run or talk it over with a friend. 6. Sleep, workload, income or an existing health condition, any of which could affect both stress and colds.
