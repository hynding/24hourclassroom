## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Social norm | An understood rule for accepted and expected behavior in a group. |
| Normative social influence | Going along with others to be accepted or avoid rejection. |
| Informational social influence | Going along with others because we believe they know better. |
| Conformity | Adjusting behavior or thinking to match a group standard. |
| Obedience | Following the orders of an authority figure. |
| Social facilitation | Better performance on simple or well-learned tasks, worse on hard or new ones, when others are watching. |
| Social loafing | Putting in less effort when working in a group toward a shared goal than when individually accountable. |
| Deindividuation | Loss of self-awareness and self-restraint in groups that offer arousal and anonymity. |
| Group polarization | Group discussion strengthens the group's existing leaning. |
| Groupthink | The desire for harmony overrides realistic appraisal of alternatives in a decision-making group. |
| Diffusion of responsibility | Each person feels less responsible when others are present. |
| Bystander effect | The more bystanders present, the less likely any one is to help. |
| Superordinate goal | A shared goal that requires cooperation between groups. |

## Norms, roles and culture

**Social norms** are the unwritten rules of a setting: face the door in an elevator, whisper in a library. **Social roles** are sets of expectations for a position (student, coach, guard). Cultures differ in emphasis: **individualist** cultures prioritize personal goals and define the self by personal traits; **collectivist** cultures prioritize group goals and define the self by relationships and group memberships. **Multiculturalism** is the coexistence of many cultures within one society, with respect for each. Studies have generally found conformity rates somewhat higher in more collectivist cultures, where agreeing with the group can be seen as a virtue.

The **false consensus effect** is overestimating how many others share our opinions and behaviors ("everyone cheats a little").

## Conformity: Asch's lines

In the early 1950s Solomon Asch asked college men to say which of three comparison lines matched a standard line. The answer was obvious; people alone made almost no errors. But each real participant sat among confederates who, on certain trials, unanimously gave the same wrong answer. Across those critical trials, participants conformed about a third of the time, and roughly three-quarters conformed at least once (a minority never did).

Conformity changed with the situation. It rose as the unanimous group grew to about three or four, then leveled off. It dropped sharply when just one confederate gave the correct answer: a single ally breaks the pressure. It also dropped when participants wrote their answers privately, which suggests much of it was **normative social influence** (wanting to fit in) rather than real belief. Where the correct answer is genuinely uncertain, **informational social influence** dominates: we look to others because they might know.

Factors that increase conformity: a unanimous group, a group of about three or more, a group we admire or belong to, public responses, feeling incompetent or insecure, and a culture that values harmony.

## Obedience: Milgram's studies

Stanley Milgram, at Yale in the early 1960s, recruited men from the community for a "study of memory and learning". A rigged drawing made each participant the "teacher" and a confederate the "learner", strapped into a chair in the next room. The teacher was to give a shock for each wrong answer, moving up a generator's switches from 15 to 450 volts in 15-volt steps. The learner received no real shocks, but his scripted protests grew to screams and then silence. When teachers hesitated, the experimenter used a series of calm prods ("Please continue"; "The experiment requires that you continue").

In the best-known version, 26 of 40 men (65%) continued to the maximum 450 volts, though many showed great distress: sweating, trembling, nervous laughter.

Variations showed the power of the situation:

- obedience fell when the learner was in the **same room**, and further when the teacher had to press the learner's hand onto a plate;
- it fell when the experimenter gave orders **by telephone**;
- it fell when the study moved from Yale to a plain office building, but remained substantial;
- it fell dramatically when two other "teachers" (confederates) **refused** to continue: a model of defiance works like Asch's ally.

**Ethics.** Milgram's work prompted lasting debate. Participants were **deceived** and put under severe stress, and the prods made **withdrawing** feel impossible. Milgram did debrief participants and surveyed them afterward, and most said they were glad to have taken part, but today's guidelines (IRB review, the right to withdraw at any time, protection from harm) would not allow the study as run. Later partial replications stopped at 150 volts, the point after which most who reached it went on to the end.

## The Stanford prison study, and why to read it critically

In 1971 Philip Zimbardo's team assigned 24 male college student volunteers at random to be "guards" or "prisoners" in a mock prison in a Stanford basement. The study was planned for two weeks but was stopped after six days, after some guards became harsh and humiliating and several prisoners showed acute distress. It was long presented as proof that roles alone turn ordinary people cruel.

It is now taught as much for its flaws as its findings:

- **Experimenter involvement.** Zimbardo acted as the prison's superintendent, not a neutral observer, and later admitted this compromised his judgment.
- **Demand characteristics.** Recordings and documents published decades later show guards were briefed on creating fear and some were encouraged to be "tough"; some guards later said they were acting the part they believed researchers wanted.
- **Self-selection.** The newspaper ad asked for volunteers for "a psychological study of prison life"; a later study found that wording attracted people scoring higher on aggressiveness and authoritarianism than an otherwise identical ad without it.
- **Small, narrow sample** of young male college students; tiny numbers per role.
- **Variation in behavior.** Only some guards were abusive; others were fair or kind, which a "roles determine behavior" account cannot explain.
- **No replication.** A televised prison study run by other researchers in 2002 did not produce guard tyranny.
- **Ethics.** Participants suffered real distress, and the researchers were slow to stop it.

Roles and situations do influence behavior. The prison study's evidence for that is weak, and spotting why is exactly the kind of evaluation AP questions ask for.

## Behavior in groups

**Social facilitation.** In 1898 Norman Triplett noticed that cyclists rode faster racing others than alone. Later work (Robert Zajonc) explained the pattern: the presence of others raises arousal, which strengthens the **dominant** (most likely) response. On easy or well-practiced tasks that response is correct, so performance improves; on hard or new tasks it is often wrong, so performance worsens.

**Social loafing.** When individual effort is pooled and not identifiable, people exert less: people shouting or clapping in a group make less noise each than alone. Making each person's contribution identifiable reduces it. Industrial-organizational psychologists apply findings like this to design teams and workplaces.

**Deindividuation.** Crowds, uniforms, masks and darkness bring anonymity and arousal, lowering self-awareness and restraint; behavior can become more impulsive, for better or worse.

**Group polarization.** Discussion with like-minded people pushes a group further in the direction it already leaned. Moderately prejudiced students who talk together become more prejudiced; moderately tolerant ones more tolerant. Online echo chambers are an everyday case.

**Groupthink.** Irving Janis described how cohesive decision-making groups with a strong leader suppress dissent, feel invulnerable and ignore warnings, as in the planning of the 1961 Bay of Pigs invasion. Remedies: invite critics, assign a devil's advocate, seek outside experts.

## Helping and conflict

**The bystander effect.** In 1964 the murder of Kitty Genovese in New York was reported as watched by 38 passive witnesses. That account was later shown to be badly exaggerated (few saw the attack, and some did call for help), but it prompted John Darley and Bibb Latané's research. In their 1968 study, students talking over an intercom heard another participant apparently have a seizure. When students believed they were the only listener, about 85% went for help before the seizure ended; when they believed four others were also listening, only about 31% did. The cause is **diffusion of responsibility**: with others present, each feels less personally responsible. Helping also requires noticing the event and interpreting it as an emergency; passive bystanders make it seem less urgent.

**Prosocial behavior** is behavior that benefits others. **Altruism** is helping with no expectation of reward. Two norms encourage helping: the **reciprocity norm** (help those who help us) and the **social responsibility norm** (help those who depend on us, such as children). We help more when we are in a good mood, have seen others help, feel similar to the person, and are not rushed.

**Conflict and peacemaking.** A **social trap** is a situation in which each party, pursuing its own self-interest, produces an outcome worse for everyone (overfishing a shared lake). In Muzafer Sherif's 1954 Robbers Cave study, two groups of 11- and 12-year-old boys at a summer camp developed fierce rivalry through competitions. Contact alone did not end it; **superordinate goals** that needed both groups (fixing a water supply, pulling a stalled truck) did.

**Worked example: name the concept.**

| Scenario | Concept |
|---|---|
| A skilled pianist plays better at the recital than in practice; a beginner plays worse. | Social facilitation |
| In a five-person project, two members do little because only the group grade counts. | Social loafing |
| A team that already favored a plan becomes certain of it after an hour agreeing with each other. | Group polarization |
| A cabinet's members keep doubts to themselves to preserve unity, and a flawed plan goes ahead. | Groupthink |
| A student follows a crowd onto a field after a win and throws things she never would alone. | Deindividuation |

## Research methods: evaluating classic studies

- **Confederates and deception** let researchers create a controlled situation (Asch's group, Milgram's learner) but require debriefing and IRB justification.
- **Demand characteristics** are cues that tell participants what the researcher expects; they threaten internal validity (the prison study).
- **Generalizability** depends on the sample: Asch and Milgram tested mostly American men in the 1950s and 1960s; later studies with women and in other countries found broadly similar obedience but varied conformity.
- **Operational definitions** made these studies powerful: obedience as the highest voltage reached; conformity as the percentage of critical trials matching the wrong group answer.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing up conformity and obedience.** Peers versus an authority's command.
- **Normative versus informational.** Fitting in versus believing others are right.
- **Saying social facilitation always improves performance.** Only on easy or well-learned tasks.
- **Confusing group polarization with groupthink.** Strengthened leanings versus suppressed dissent in decisions.
- **Treating the Stanford prison study as strong evidence.** Name its specific flaws.
- **Saying "more bystanders, more help".** It is the reverse.
