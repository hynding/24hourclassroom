# Week 02 · The Nervous System's Big Picture

**Topics:** 1.2 Overview of the Nervous System
**Practices:** 1 (Concept Application), 2 (Research Methods and Design), 3 (Data Interpretation)
**Framework:** AP Psychology CED (effective fall 2024), Unit 1, Topic 1.2, in our own words
**Spiral:** Week 1 asked how heredity and environment build behavior, and how researchers test claims. This week looks at the hardware that carries every behavior: the body's communication network. You will keep using week 1's tools, because arousal and calm are measured with operational definitions (heart rate, pupil size, skin conductance) and studied with experiments and correlations.

## What you need to be able to do

- **1.2** Describe the two main parts of the nervous system, central and peripheral, and what each does.
- **1.2** Distinguish the somatic and autonomic divisions of the peripheral nervous system.
- **1.2** Explain how the sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions of the autonomic nervous system work together to arouse and calm the body.
- **1.2** Apply these divisions to scenarios (a scare, a meal, a sprint, an injury) and predict their effects.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Nervous system | The body's fast, electrochemical communication network of nerve cells. |
| Central nervous system (CNS) | The brain and spinal cord: where information is integrated and decisions are made. |
| Peripheral nervous system (PNS) | Every nerve outside the brain and spinal cord, linking the CNS to the body. |
| Nerve | A bundle of axons (long neuron fibers) that carries messages between the CNS and the body. |
| Somatic nervous system | The PNS division that carries sensory input from the body and controls voluntary skeletal muscles. |
| Autonomic nervous system | The PNS division that controls glands and internal organs (heart, lungs, digestion) largely without conscious effort. |
| Sympathetic nervous system | The autonomic branch that arouses the body for action ("fight or flight"). |
| Parasympathetic nervous system | The autonomic branch that calms the body and conserves energy ("rest and digest"). |
| Sensory (afferent) pathway | Carries information from the body toward the CNS. |
| Motor (efferent) pathway | Carries commands from the CNS out to muscles and glands. |

## The map of the system (1.2)

Think of the nervous system as a branching tree.

| Level | Division | Main job |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Central nervous system | Brain and spinal cord: integrate information and issue commands |
| 1 | Peripheral nervous system | Connect the CNS to the rest of the body |
| 2 (in PNS) | Somatic | Senses from skin, muscles and joints in; voluntary movement out |
| 2 (in PNS) | Autonomic | Automatic control of organs and glands |
| 3 (in autonomic) | Sympathetic | Arouse: speed up the heart, release energy |
| 3 (in autonomic) | Parasympathetic | Calm: slow the heart, digest, conserve energy |

**The central nervous system.** The **brain** is the command center; week 4 takes it apart region by region. The **spinal cord** is the main highway between the brain and the body. Sensory messages travel up it and motor commands travel down it. It also handles some fast responses on its own: pull your hand from a hot pan and the withdrawal starts in the spinal cord before the brain registers pain. Week 3 traces that **reflex** pathway neuron by neuron.

**What a spinal cord injury shows.** Because messages pass through the cord, damage to it cuts communication below the injury. A person with a severe injury in the neck region may lose sensation and voluntary movement in the arms, trunk and legs, while a lower injury in the back affects mainly the legs. Some reflexes below the injury can remain, because they do not need the brain. This pattern is good evidence that the cord is a two-way pathway as well as a reflex center.

**The peripheral nervous system.** Everything outside the brain and cord. **Sensory (afferent)** nerves carry information *in*; **motor (efferent)** nerves carry commands *out*. A memory trick: **a**fferent **a**rrives, **e**fferent **e**xits.

## Somatic versus autonomic

The **somatic nervous system** is the part you can direct. When you decide to kick a ball, motor commands travel from the brain, down the spinal cord and out along somatic nerves to the skeletal muscles of your leg. When you feel the grass under your foot, somatic sensory nerves carry that touch back in.

The **autonomic nervous system** runs the body's maintenance: heart rate, breathing rate, blood pressure, digestion, sweating, pupil size and many glands. It works mostly without conscious effort, which is why you cannot simply decide to lower your blood pressure by 20 points. "Mostly" matters: you can influence it indirectly, for example by slow breathing, which tends to calm the heart.

## Sympathetic and parasympathetic: accelerator and brake

The two branches of the autonomic system usually act on the same organs in opposite directions, keeping the body in a steady state (homeostasis) and shifting it when the situation changes.

| Organ or process | Sympathetic (arousing) | Parasympathetic (calming) |
|---|---|---|
| Heart rate | Speeds up | Slows down |
| Breathing | Faster; airways widen | Slower |
| Pupils | Dilate (widen) | Constrict (narrow) |
| Digestion | Slows or stops | Resumes |
| Salivation | Decreases (dry mouth) | Increases |
| Sweating | Increases | Not stimulated |
| Stored energy | Glucose released into the blood | Energy conserved and stored |
| Adrenal glands | Stimulated to release stress hormones | Not stimulated |

**Worked example 1: the surprise quiz.** Your teacher announces an unannounced quiz. Your heart pounds, your mouth goes dry, your palms sweat and your stomach feels like it stops. That cluster is **sympathetic** arousal: faster heart, less saliva, more sweat, digestion put on hold. When the quiz turns out to be easy and the bell rings, your heart slows and you suddenly feel hungry: the **parasympathetic** branch is restoring calm and letting digestion resume.

**Worked example 2: a sprint and lunch.** During a 400-meter race, the sympathetic system raises heart and breathing rates and releases stored glucose for the muscles; the somatic system is running the legs. After lunch, sitting in class, the parasympathetic system dominates: slower heart rate, digestion in progress, and sometimes drowsiness.

Note what "fight or flight" really means: it is a whole-body preparation for effort, useful whether the threat is a predator, a deadline or a stage. The same arousal can feel like fear, excitement or anger; Unit 4's theories of emotion return to that puzzle.

## Studying arousal: operational definitions and data

Arousal cannot be measured directly, so researchers define it by procedures:

- **Heart rate** in beats per minute (a wrist monitor or chest strap).
- **Skin conductance**: sweat makes skin conduct a tiny current better, so a rise means sympathetic activity.
- **Pupil diameter** in millimeters, from a camera.
- **Self-report**: a rating from 1 (calm) to 10 (very tense).

A **polygraph**, the so-called lie detector, records several of these autonomic measures at once. It detects arousal, not lying: an honest but anxious person can look "deceptive", and a calm liar can pass. That is why most U.S. courts do not admit polygraph results as evidence, or admit them only in limited cases, such as when both sides agree in advance. It is a good lesson in **validity**: a measure must actually capture the concept it claims to measure.

**Worked example 3: a hypothetical experiment.** A research team (invented for this course) randomly assigns 40 volunteers to watch either a suspenseful film clip or a calm nature clip for 5 minutes, while heart rate is recorded.

| Condition | n | Mean heart rate (bpm) | SD (bpm) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Suspense clip | 20 | 91 | 8 |
| Nature clip | 20 | 73 | 7 |

- *Method:* an experiment, because the clip is manipulated and assignment is random.
- *IV:* type of clip. *DV:* heart rate in beats per minute.
- *Reading the table:* the suspense group's mean is 18 bpm higher, and the SDs (7 to 8 bpm) show that the two groups' scores overlap only a little. If the difference is statistically significant (p less than .05), it is unlikely to be due to chance, and the most likely explanation is sympathetic arousal caused by the suspense clip.
- *Ethics:* volunteers give informed consent, can stop watching at any time, and are told the purpose afterward.
- *Limit:* 40 volunteers may not represent older adults or people with heart conditions, so generalization is limited.

## Common mistakes

- **Placing the spinal cord in the PNS.** The spinal cord is part of the central nervous system.
- **Mixing up the autonomic branches.** Sympathetic arouses ("stress"), parasympathetic calms ("settle"). Digestion speeds up under the parasympathetic branch.
- **Calling the somatic system "automatic".** Somatic means voluntary skeletal muscles and body senses; *autonomic* is automatic.
- **Treating a polygraph as a lie detector.** It measures arousal, which has many causes.
- **Saying the two branches cannot both be active.** They work in balance; one usually dominates at a time.

## Self-check

1. A hiker sees a snake and freezes, heart pounding. Which branch is active, and name two other changes you would expect.
2. Is typing a text message controlled mainly by the somatic or the autonomic system?
3. After a big dinner, which branch dominates? Give one sign.
4. Which nerves carry information from your fingertips to your spinal cord: afferent or efferent?
5. Name two operational definitions of arousal.
6. Why can an injury to the lower spinal cord leave a person's arms unaffected?

*Answers:* 1. Sympathetic: pupils dilate, breathing speeds up, digestion slows, sweating increases, glucose is released. 2. Somatic (voluntary skeletal muscles). 3. Parasympathetic: slower heart rate, active digestion, more saliva. 4. Afferent (sensory). 5. Heart rate in beats per minute, skin conductance, pupil diameter in millimeters, or a 1-to-10 tension rating. 6. Messages to and from the arms enter and leave the cord above the injury, so they still reach the brain.
