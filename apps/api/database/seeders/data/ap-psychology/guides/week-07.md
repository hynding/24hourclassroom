The eye delivers the same pattern of neural firing whether you expect a friend or a stranger at the door; what you *see* depends on what the brain does with that signal.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Perception | The process of organizing and interpreting sensory information so that it means something. |
| Bottom-up processing | Analysis that starts with the raw sensory features and builds upward to a whole. |
| Top-down processing | Analysis guided by knowledge, expectations and goals. |
| Schema | A mental framework that organizes knowledge about a category of things or events. |
| Perceptual set | A readiness to perceive something one way rather than another, set up by expectation or context. |
| Selective attention | Focusing awareness on a small part of the available sensory input. |
| Inattentional blindness | Failing to notice a fully visible object or event while attention is elsewhere. |
| Change blindness | Failing to notice a change in a scene from one moment to the next. |
| Gestalt | An organized whole; the Gestalt psychologists argued the whole is perceived as more than the sum of its parts. |
| Depth cue | Information that lets the brain judge distance: binocular (needs both eyes) or monocular (works with one). |
| Perceptual constancy | Perceiving an object as unchanging in size, shape or color even as the image on the retina changes. |
| Apparent movement | Perceiving motion when none exists, as when quickly flashed still images look like a moving scene. |

## Two directions of processing

Sensation hands the brain edges, colors, pitches and pressures. **Bottom-up processing** assembles these features into something recognizable: lines become a letter, letters a word. **Top-down processing** runs the other way: what you already know and expect shapes how the features are read. Every act of perception uses both. Reading messy handwriting is the classic case. A scrawled shape that could be an "H" or an "A" is read as "H" in "THE" and as "A" in "CAT", because your knowledge of English words tells you which letter fits.

**Schemas** are the knowledge that drives top-down processing. Your schema for a classroom tells you to expect desks and a board, so you recognize them almost instantly and might not notice a missing clock. A **perceptual set** is a short-term version: a mental readiness created by what you have just seen, been told, or want. If a friend says a blurry photo shows a lake monster, you are more likely to see a neck and a head in a floating log. Four influences on perceptual set are worth naming:

- **Context.** The same spoken sound is heard as "ate" in "I ___ lunch at noon" and as "eight" in "The bus leaves at ___ o'clock"; a gray square looks lighter on a dark background than on a light one.
- **Expectations.** What you have been told to look for primes you to find it.
- **Emotion and motivation.** A hungry person spots food words faster; a frightened person may hear a creak as a footstep.
- **Culture and experience.** People raised among buildings with many right angles have, in some studies, been more prone to certain line-length illusions than people raised among rounded dwellings. The shapes a culture provides shape the assumptions the brain brings to an image.

**Worked example 1: naming the processing.** A birdwatcher hears a call and, before seeing anything, knows it is a cardinal; when the bird appears she sees red even in dim light. Hearing the call and identifying it from its pitch pattern is bottom-up. Seeing red in poor light because she already "knows" the bird is a cardinal is top-down, a perceptual set.

## Attention: you see what you attend to

The senses deliver far more than the brain can process, so **selective attention** picks a small part to bring into awareness. In the **cocktail party effect**, you follow one conversation in a noisy room yet still notice when someone across the room says your name. Research on dichotic listening by Colin Cherry in the 1950s showed that people attending to one ear's message could report almost nothing about the other ear's content, though they did notice a change such as a switch from a male to a female voice.

The price of selection is blindness to the rest. In **inattentional blindness**, a person misses something fully visible because attention is elsewhere. In a well-known 1999 study by Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris, viewers counting basketball passes in a video often failed to notice a person in a gorilla suit walking through the scene. **Change blindness** is failing to notice that something changed, such as a person you are giving directions to being swapped for another person while a door is carried between you.

## Organizing the scene: Gestalt principles

The Gestalt psychologists, working in Germany in the early 1900s, argued that the brain groups sensations into wholes using a few rules:

- **Figure and ground.** We split a scene into an object (figure) standing out from its surroundings (ground). In reversible figures, such as a drawing that is either a vase or two faces, figure and ground can swap.
- **Proximity.** Items close together are grouped. Six dots arranged as three pairs look like three groups.
- **Similarity.** Items that look alike are grouped. A grid of circles and squares is seen as rows of circles and rows of squares.
- **Closure.** We fill in gaps to see a complete shape. A circle with a small gap is still seen as a circle; logos often leave out lines on purpose.

**Worked example 2: applying the principles.** A school map shows the gym, pool and weight room all shaded orange and drawn close together. Visitors see them as one "athletics" area. Shading them the same color uses similarity; placing them near each other uses proximity. If the outline of the athletics area has gaps for doors but people still see one building, that is closure.

## Depth perception

The retina is flat, yet we see a three-dimensional world. Even very young crawling infants hesitate at the edge of an apparent drop: in Eleanor Gibson and Richard Walk's visual cliff studies (published in 1960), most crawling infants would not cross a glass surface over a visible drop even when a parent called them.

**Binocular cues** need two eyes:

- **Retinal disparity.** Each eye sees a slightly different image; the closer the object, the greater the difference. The brain uses the size of the difference to judge distance.
- **Convergence.** The eyes turn inward to look at a near object; the brain reads the strain of the eye muscles as nearness.

**Monocular cues** work with one eye, which is why painters can show depth on a flat canvas:

- **Relative size.** Of two objects assumed to be similar in size, the one that casts the smaller image seems farther away.
- **Interposition (overlap).** An object that blocks part of another is seen as closer.
- **Relative clarity.** Hazy objects seem farther than sharp ones.
- **Texture gradient.** Texture that becomes finer and denser signals increasing distance.
- **Linear perspective.** Parallel lines, such as railroad tracks, seem to converge with distance.

**Worked example 3: identifying cues.** A landscape photo shows a fence whose rails seem to meet at the horizon (linear perspective), mountains that look faint and bluish (relative clarity), and a tree that hides part of a barn (interposition). None of these needs two eyes, so all are monocular.

## Constancy and motion

**Perceptual constancy** keeps the world stable. A friend walking away casts a shrinking image on your retina, but you do not think she is shrinking (**size constancy**). A door swinging open projects a trapezoid, yet you see a rectangle (**shape constancy**). A white shirt looks white in sunlight and in shade, though it reflects far less light in shade (**color and lightness constancy**), because the brain compares it with its surroundings. Illusions are the cases where the assumptions behind constancy fail.

**Apparent movement** is the brain seeing motion that is not there. Lights flashing in sequence on a marquee seem to travel around the sign (the **phi phenomenon**), and a film's still frames shown rapidly look like continuous action (**stroboscopic movement**).

## Research methods spotlight: testing perceptual set

**Worked example 4: an experiment on expectation.** A hypothetical class experiment randomly assigns 60 volunteers to two groups. Group 1 first sees four pictures of animals; group 2 sees four pictures of faces. Everyone then sees one ambiguous drawing that can be read as a duck or as a face and writes what it is.

- *Independent variable:* the type of priming pictures (animals or faces). *Dependent variable:* whether the ambiguous drawing is reported as an animal, operationally defined as writing an animal name.
- *Random assignment* spreads differences such as eyesight and prior experience evenly across groups, so a difference in reports can be attributed to the priming.
- *Results:* 24 of 30 (80%) in the animal group named an animal, against 9 of 30 (30%) in the face group. That large difference is consistent with a perceptual set created by context. A significance test would tell us how unlikely a difference this large is if priming did nothing; a statistically significant result means chance alone is an unlikely explanation, not that the effect is large or important.
- *Ethics:* participants give informed consent, may withdraw at any time, and are debriefed about the hypothesis afterward.

**Worked example 5: reading descriptive statistics.** In another invented study, 40 drivers in a simulator either talked on a hands-free phone or did not. Phone users missed a mean of 4.2 roadside signs (SD 1.1); the others missed 1.8 (SD 0.9). The means show phone users missed more than twice as many signs; the similar, small standard deviations show scores within each group clustered closely, so the groups barely overlap. The finding fits inattentional blindness: eyes on the road do not guarantee attention on the road.

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing sensation and perception.** Detecting light is sensation; recognizing a face is perception.
- **Calling every expectation effect "bottom-up".** If knowledge or expectation is doing the work, it is top-down.
- **Mixing up the two blindnesses.** Inattentional blindness: an unexpected object is never noticed. Change blindness: a change between views is not noticed.
- **Listing binocular cues as monocular.** Only retinal disparity and convergence need two eyes.

## Self-check

1. A reader sees "13" as the letter B in "A 13 C" and as a number in "12 13 14". Which kind of processing explains the difference?
2. Name the Gestalt principle: a crowd at a concert is seen as fans of one band because they all wear the same shirt.
3. Which depth cue tells you a jet high overhead is far away even though you know jets are large?
4. A person watching a magic trick misses a coin moving to the other hand while watching the magician's face. Name the phenomenon.
5. Why does a person walking away not seem to shrink?

*Answers:* 1. Top-down processing: context sets up the reading. 2. Similarity. 3. Relative size (the jet's retinal image is tiny). 4. Inattentional blindness (selective attention to the face). 5. Size constancy: the brain combines image size with perceived distance.
