## The ideas that generate every question

1. **We explain behavior with shortcuts that tilt toward the person (4.1).** Dispositional versus situational attributions; the fundamental attribution error (others), the actor-observer bias (me versus you) and the self-serving bias (success versus failure). Explanatory style (internal, stable, global = pessimistic) and locus of control (internal versus external) are habits of explanation. Mere exposure, self-fulfilling prophecies, social comparison and relative deprivation shape how we see others and ourselves.
2. **Attitudes have parts, and they change by predictable routes (4.2).** Stereotype (belief), prejudice (feeling), discrimination (action); explicit versus implicit. In-group bias, out-group homogeneity, ethnocentrism, the just-world phenomenon and belief perseverance keep attitudes in place. Cognitive dissonance changes attitudes to fit behavior; the central and peripheral routes change them by argument or by cue; foot-in-the-door and door-in-the-face win compliance.
3. **Situations are powerful (4.3).** Asch (conformity to peers), Milgram (obedience to authority) and the bystander effect show ordinary people doing surprising things. Groups facilitate, loaf, deindividuate, polarize and fall into groupthink. Superordinate goals reduce conflict.
4. **Four perspectives describe personality (4.4, 4.5).** Psychodynamic (unconscious conflict, defense mechanisms, projective tests), humanistic (growth, self-concept, unconditional positive regard), social-cognitive (reciprocal determinism, self-efficacy) and trait (the Big Five, factor analysis, inventories). Each has a typical strength and weakness.
5. **Motivation is pushed and pulled (4.6).** Drives push toward homeostasis; incentives pull; arousal has an optimal level (Yerkes-Dodson); self-determination needs competence, autonomy and relatedness. Expected rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation. Hunger is biological, social and cultural.
6. **Emotion joins body, face and mind (4.7).** James-Lange (body first), Cannon-Bard (together, via the thalamus), two-factor (arousal plus label), appraisal and the low road. Basic expressions are widely universal; display rules are cultural. Positive emotions broaden and build.

## Defense mechanisms in one line each

Repression (out of awareness) · denial (refuse reality) · displacement (safer target) · projection (my feeling is yours) · rationalization (a better-sounding reason) · reaction formation (the opposite) · regression (act younger) · sublimation (a valued outlet).

## Research methods that keep coming up

- **Deception, confederates and debriefing.** Asch, Milgram and Schachter-Singer all relied on deception; IRBs now require justification, the right to withdraw, protection from harm and debriefing.
- **Demand characteristics and experimenter involvement** are the core criticisms of the Stanford prison study.
- **Reliability and validity** of personality and attitude measures: projective tests (weak inter-rater reliability), the IAT (unstable individual scores), inventories (more reliable; social desirability bias; validity scales).
- **Correlation is not causation** for trait-outcome links and explanatory style; **curvilinear** relationships (Yerkes-Dodson) can give r near 0.
- **Replication** (the facial-feedback pen study; the prison study) separates durable findings from fragile ones.

## Sentences that earn AP points

- **Attribution:** "The teacher commits the fundamental attribution error by explaining the student's silence as shyness, a disposition, while ignoring that it was his first day, a situational cause."
- **Dissonance:** "Because she was paid little, she lacked external justification for tutoring, so to reduce the dissonance between her effort and her doubts she came to value tutoring more."
- **Bystanders:** "With many people present, responsibility is diffused, so each bystander feels less obligated to help."
- **Personality:** "High self-efficacy in math leads him to choose harder classes (behavior), which places him with stronger peers (environment), which raises his confidence further (personal): reciprocal determinism."
- **Motivation:** "Because the task is difficult and unfamiliar, the Yerkes-Dodson law predicts best performance at moderate, not high, arousal."
- **Emotion:** "Two-factor theory predicts that her unexplained arousal will be labeled using situational cues, such as others' laughter."

## Classic question types and how to attack them

**"Which bias?"** Ask: whose behavior (mine or someone else's)? Was the outcome good or bad? Is a situation being ignored?

**"Which perspective?"** Unconscious conflict → psychodynamic; growth and self-concept → humanistic; beliefs, environment and behavior interacting → social-cognitive; stable dimensions → trait.

**"Which theory of motivation?"** Internal need → drive reduction; external lure → incentive; seeking stimulation → arousal; choice, mastery and connection → self-determination.

**"Which theory of emotion?"** Body then feeling → James-Lange; simultaneous → Cannon-Bard; label from the situation → two-factor.

**AAQ on a social study.** Name the method; give the measured variable's operational definition; interpret the statistic with direction and size; name the ethical guideline (often deception and debriefing); judge generalizability from the sample; tie the result to a named concept.

**EBQ.** A claim that takes a position; evidence quoted precisely from a source; a different concept for each source, each explained.

## Common mistakes

- Actor-observer versus self-serving; conformity versus obedience; normative versus informational influence.
- Saying the $20 group in Festinger and Carlsmith changed more.
- Treating the Stanford prison study as strong evidence without its criticisms.
- Displacement versus projection; reaction formation versus sublimation.
- Calling the Big Five types; confusing self-efficacy with self-esteem.
- Reversing Yerkes-Dodson; mixing up ghrelin and leptin.
- Reversing James-Lange and Cannon-Bard; overstating facial feedback.

## Self-test

1. You blame traffic for your lateness and a coworker's laziness for hers. Which bias?
2. Name the three parts of a group attitude.
3. What reduced conformity most in Asch's studies?
4. A person kicks a door after a breakup. Which defense mechanism?
5. Name the three needs of self-determination theory.
6. Which theory says emotion needs arousal plus a label?

**Answers.** 1. Actor-observer bias. 2. Stereotype (belief), prejudice (feeling), discrimination (action). 3. One dissenting ally. 4. Displacement. 5. Competence, autonomy, relatedness. 6. The Schachter-Singer two-factor theory.
