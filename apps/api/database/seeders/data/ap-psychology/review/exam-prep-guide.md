# Exam Prep Guide · AP Psychology

**Use it:** in week 33, before the practice final in week 34
**Covers:** all five units, every one of which the AP Psychology exam tests
**Framework:** AP Psychology Course and Exam Description (revised, effective fall 2024); exam format from AP Central, May 2027 exam

This guide explains the May 2027 AP Psychology exam, how the practice final rehearses it and how the two free-response questions are scored, then collects the research methods and statistics to know cold, the high-yield concepts of each unit, and a two-week review plan. If you are taking psychology as a regular course, the whole practice final is your final exam.

## The AP exam in May 2027

The exam is on **Friday, May 14, 2027**. It is **fully digital** in the Bluebook app: you select multiple-choice answers and **type** your free responses in Bluebook.

| Section | Questions | Time | Share of score |
|---|---|---|---|
| I: Multiple choice | 75, four options each | 1 hour 30 minutes | about two thirds (66.7%) |
| II: Free response | 2, 7 points each: the Article Analysis Question (AAQ) and the Evidence-Based Question (EBQ) | 1 hour 10 minutes | about one third (33.3%) |

- **Pace for Section I:** 75 questions in 90 minutes is 72 seconds a question. Some questions are stand-alone; others share a stimulus, such as a data table, a graph, a short study summary or a scenario. Read the stimulus once, carefully, then answer its questions together.
- **Pace for Section II:** the College Board suggests about 25 minutes for the AAQ and 45 minutes for the EBQ, reading time included: the EBQ has three sources to read and asks for more writing.
- There is no penalty for a wrong multiple-choice answer, so never leave one blank.
- **No calculator is allowed**: AP Psychology is not one of the exams on the College Board's calculator list (students with an approved accommodation are the exception). Expect to interpret statistics far more than to compute them; any arithmetic is simple (a difference between two means, a percentage, reading a correlation).

### What changed in the revised course

If you borrow an older review book, beware. Before fall 2024 the course had nine units, the multiple-choice section had 100 five-option questions, and the two free-response questions were a concept-application question and a research-design question. The revised course has **five units**, **75 four-option questions**, and the **AAQ and EBQ**. It also names four **science practices** that run through every unit: Concept Application, Research Methods and Design, Data Interpretation, and Argumentation. That is why every quiz in this course had research and statistics questions, whatever the week's topic.

### Typing your free responses

- Label every part exactly as the prompt does: (A), (B), (C) and so on, and for the EBQ (B)(i), (B)(ii). A reader scores each part on its own, and an unlabeled answer is easy to misread.
- Write in full sentences. A bulleted list of terms, or an outline, does not explain anything.
- **Name the concept, then apply it.** "This is diffusion of responsibility: because six other people were present, each participant felt less personally responsible for helping." The term alone earns nothing; the application is the point.
- Do not restate the question or write an introduction. Start each part with the answer.

## How the practice final maps onto it

The practice final has 91 questions. **Questions 1–77 are a full mock AP exam** in the order and timing above.

- **Questions 1–75: Section I.** Seventy-five four-option multiple-choice questions in roughly equal unit shares: 14 on Unit 1, 16 on Unit 2, 17 on Unit 3, 15 on Unit 4 and 13 on Unit 5, touching every one of the 35 topics. Twenty-two of them come in seven shared-stimulus sets: a twin and adoption correlation table (5–7), a signal detection experiment (12–14), a depth-of-processing experiment (19–21), a new reasoning test's reliability and validity data (28–30), a cross-sectional conservation study (33–35), a bystander field experiment (52–54) and a depression treatment trial (70–73).
- **Question 76: the AAQ.** One summary of a hypothetical experiment on napping after learning and memory for word pairs, with the six parts A–F.
- **Question 77: the EBQ.** Three hypothetical sources on physical activity and stress in adolescents (an experiment, a correlational survey and a physiological study), with a claim and two evidence-and-explanation pairs.

Every study in the practice final is invented for this course and says so; the real exam's studies will be new to you too, so practise reading an unfamiliar study quickly. Sit questions 1–77 in one block with the AP timings (2 hours 40 minutes, with a short break after question 75). Do not convert your total into a 1–5 score (cut points are not published in advance); note the topic of every lost point instead.

**Questions 78–91** are not AP format: select-all, true/false, fill-in-the-blank and short-answer items across all five units. Do them on another day, in about 30 minutes.

## The free-response questions and how they are scored

Both questions are worth 7 points, and every point is attached to one specific task. The scoring guides in this course name each one, so read them after every attempt.

### The Article Analysis Question (AAQ)

You read a summary of one study, in the style of a journal article: participants, method, results with a statistic, and usually details about ethics. Then you answer six parts.

| Part | Task | Points | What earns the point |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Identify the research method | 1 | The correct method (experiment, correlational study, case study, naturalistic observation, survey, meta-analysis) |
| B | State the operational definition of a named variable | 1 | Exactly how that variable was measured or manipulated in this study, with the measure ("number of word pairs recalled out of 40") |
| C | Describe what a given statistic shows | 1 | The meaning of the number in context: a mean difference, a correlation's direction and strength, a p-value's significance, an effect size |
| D | Identify an ethical guideline applied or violated | 1 | A named guideline tied to a detail of this study |
| E | Explain how far the findings generalize | 1 | Specific features of the sample or setting (who, how many, from where, how chosen) and what they allow or limit |
| F | Explain how the findings support or refute a claim | 2 | One point for using a specific finding; one for linking it to the claim through a psychological concept |

**AAQ habits that earn points.**

- In (A), name the method only; no explanation is needed. If participants were randomly assigned to conditions, it is an experiment; if variables were only measured, it is correlational.
- In (B), copy the study's own measure. "Memory" is a construct; "the number of second words correctly recalled when shown the first word" is an operational definition.
- In (C), say what the statistic means about these variables. For p = .01: "the difference between the groups is statistically significant: it is unlikely to have happened by chance alone." For r = −0.42: "a moderate negative relationship: as one variable rose, the other tended to fall."
- In (D), name a real guideline (informed consent, assent and parental consent for minors, debriefing, confidentiality, protection from harm, right to withdraw, IRB approval) and point to the sentence in the study that shows it.
- In (E), the answer is almost never simply "yes" or "no". Say who the participants were and whom the result can or cannot be extended to.
- In (F), quote a number from the results, then name a concept and show how it explains that number.

### The Evidence-Based Question (EBQ)

You read three short sources (Source A, B and C), each summarizing a study on one topic, and build an argument.

| Part | Task | Points |
|---|---|---|
| A | Propose a specific, defensible claim that answers the question | 1 |
| B(i) | Support the claim with specific evidence from one source | 1 |
| B(ii) | Explain how that evidence supports the claim, using a psychological concept | 2 |
| C(i) | Support the claim with specific evidence from a different source | 1 |
| C(ii) | Explain how that evidence supports the claim, using a different psychological concept | 2 |

**EBQ habits that earn points.**

- Make the claim something the sources can actually support, and specific enough to argue: "Regular physical activity lowers adolescents' perceived stress" rather than "Exercise is good".
- Cite evidence with its numbers and its source letter: "In Source A, the walking group's mean stress score fell from 22 to 16, compared with 22 to 21 for the video group."
- Each explanation point has two jobs: name an appropriate concept from the course and connect it to the evidence and the claim. A concept dropped in without that link earns at most one of the two points.
- Use **different** sources in (B) and (C), and **different** concepts in (B)(ii) and (C)(ii).
- Research methods count as concepts. Explaining that random assignment in Source A lets you infer cause, or that a correlation in Source B cannot, is a strong way to use a source.

## Research methods and statistics to know cold

Expect these in many multiple-choice questions and in every AAQ.

### Methods and what each can show

| Method | What the researcher does | What it can show | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Experiment | Manipulates an independent variable and randomly assigns participants to conditions | Cause and effect | May be artificial; ethics limit what can be manipulated |
| Correlational study | Measures two or more variables as they occur | Direction and strength of a relationship; prediction | Cannot show cause: a third variable or reversed direction may explain it |
| Naturalistic observation | Records behavior in its real setting without interfering | What people do in everyday life | Observer bias; cannot explain why |
| Case study | Studies one person or a small group in depth | Rich detail about rare cases; ideas for research | May not generalize |
| Survey | Asks many people questions | Self-reported attitudes and behaviors | Wording effects, social desirability bias, unrepresentative samples |
| Meta-analysis | Statistically combines the results of many studies | The overall size of an effect across studies | Only as good as the studies included |
| Cross-sectional and longitudinal designs | Compare different age groups once, or follow the same people over time | Age differences, or change within people | Cohort effects; or attrition, cost and time |

### Variables and design

- **Independent variable:** what the researcher manipulates. **Dependent variable:** what is measured. **Confounding variable:** anything other than the independent variable that differs between conditions and could explain the result.
- **Operational definition:** the exact procedure used to measure or manipulate a variable, so others can replicate the study.
- **Random sampling** (everyone in the population has an equal chance of being chosen) supports **generalizing** to that population. **Random assignment** (chance decides each participant's condition) supports **cause and effect**. Know which one a study used.
- **Experimental and control groups**, **placebo**, **single-blind** (participants do not know their condition) and **double-blind** (neither participants nor the researchers who interact with them know) procedures; the **placebo effect** and **experimenter bias** they control.
- **Hindsight bias, overconfidence and confirmation bias** are why psychology relies on the scientific method rather than intuition.

### Ethics

- An **institutional review board (IRB)** reviews human research before it starts.
- **Informed consent** before the study; for minors, **parental consent plus the minor's assent**.
- **Deception** only when necessary, minimal and justified, always followed by **debriefing**, which reveals the true purpose.
- **Confidentiality** (data cannot be linked to names), **protection from harm**, and the **right to withdraw** at any time without penalty.
- Animal research also needs approval and humane care.

### Statistics

- **Mean, median and mode.** An outlier pulls the mean toward it; the median resists outliers, so it describes a skewed distribution better.
- **Range and standard deviation** describe spread. A larger standard deviation means scores are more spread out around the mean.
- **The normal curve:** about 68% of scores fall within 1 standard deviation of the mean, about 95% within 2, and about 99.7% within 3. On a test with mean 100 and standard deviation 15, a score of 115 is at about the 84th **percentile rank**.
- **Skew:** a positive skew has a long tail of high scores (the mean is above the median); a negative skew has a long tail of low scores.
- **Correlation coefficient (r)**, from −1 to +1: the sign gives the direction, the distance from zero gives the strength. r = −0.60 is stronger than r = +0.40. Correlation does not show causation. A **scatterplot** shows it; an **illusory correlation** is a relationship perceived where none exists.
- **Statistical significance:** a result unlikely to be due to chance alone, conventionally p under .05. Significance is not size: **effect size** (for example Cohen's d: about 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large) tells how big the difference is.
- **Regression toward the mean:** extreme scores tend to be followed by more average ones.
- **Replication** with different participants and settings is what makes a finding trustworthy.

## Know these cold

### Unit 1: Biological Bases of Behavior

- **Heredity and environment:** twin and adoption studies estimate how much variation in a trait is linked to genes (heritability applies to groups, never to one person). Evolutionary psychology and epigenetics (experience changing gene expression) are part of the topic.
- **Nervous system:** central (brain, spinal cord) and peripheral; somatic (voluntary muscles) and autonomic, with sympathetic (arousing, fight or flight) and parasympathetic (calming) divisions. Reflex arcs run through the spinal cord.
- **Neurons:** dendrites, cell body, axon, myelin, terminal buttons, synapse. The action potential is all-or-none; intensity is coded by firing rate. Know the main neurotransmitters (acetylcholine, dopamine, serotonin, norepinephrine, GABA, glutamate, endorphins, substance P) and how agonists, antagonists and reuptake inhibitors act. Hormones of the endocrine system act more slowly through the blood.
- **The brain:** brainstem (medulla, pons, reticular activating system), cerebellum, thalamus, hypothalamus, amygdala, hippocampus; the four lobes, the motor and somatosensory cortices, Broca's and Wernicke's areas, and the prefrontal cortex. Split-brain research and hemispheric specialization. Plasticity. Tools: EEG, fMRI, PET, lesion studies.
- **Sleep:** circadian rhythm and the suprachiasmatic nucleus; NREM stages N1 to N3 and REM, with roughly 90-minute cycles and more REM toward morning. Theories of sleep and dreaming. Sleep disorders: insomnia, narcolepsy, sleep apnea, sleepwalking, REM sleep behavior disorder.
- **Sensation:** transduction, absolute and difference thresholds (Weber's law), signal detection theory, sensory adaptation. Vision (rods, cones, the trichromatic and opponent-process theories), hearing (place and frequency theories, conduction and sensorineural hearing loss), and the chemical, body and skin senses.

### Unit 2: Cognition

- **Perception:** bottom-up and top-down processing, perceptual set, Gestalt principles, monocular and binocular depth cues, perceptual constancy, selective attention, inattentional and change blindness.
- **Thinking:** concepts and prototypes, algorithms and heuristics (availability, representativeness), mental set, functional fixedness, confirmation bias, framing, the sunk-cost fallacy, convergent and divergent thinking.
- **Memory model:** sensory, short-term or working memory (with the phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad and central executive), and long-term memory; explicit (episodic, semantic) and implicit (procedural, priming) memory.
- **Encoding and storage:** levels of processing, the self-reference effect, chunking, mnemonics, spacing, the testing effect; the hippocampus for new explicit memories, the cerebellum and basal ganglia for procedural ones; long-term potentiation.
- **Retrieval and forgetting:** recall versus recognition, retrieval cues, context- and state-dependent memory, mood-congruent memory, the serial position effect; the forgetting curve, encoding failure, interference (proactive and retroactive), anterograde and retrograde amnesia, the misinformation effect, source amnesia and constructive memory.
- **Intelligence:** general intelligence (g) and theories of multiple intelligences; fluid and crystallized intelligence; standardization, norms, reliability and validity; the normal curve of scores; the Flynn effect; stereotype threat; achievement versus aptitude tests.

### Unit 3: Development and Learning

- **Methods:** cross-sectional versus longitudinal designs, and nature and nurture, continuity and stages, stability and change.
- **Physical development:** prenatal stages and teratogens, newborn reflexes, maturation and critical periods, puberty, and the late-maturing prefrontal cortex; physical and cognitive changes in adulthood.
- **Gender and sexual orientation:** the difference between sex, gender identity, gender roles and sexual orientation; social learning and gender schemas.
- **Cognitive development:** Piaget's stages (sensorimotor and object permanence; preoperational, egocentrism and lack of conservation; concrete operational; formal operational), schemas, assimilation and accommodation; Vygotsky's zone of proximal development and scaffolding; theory of mind; fluid intelligence declining and crystallized intelligence holding in later life.
- **Language:** phonemes, morphemes, grammar; cooing, babbling, the one-word and telegraphic stages, overgeneralization; the idea of a critical period for language.
- **Social-emotional development:** attachment (Harlow's contact-comfort research with infant monkeys; Ainsworth's strange situation and secure, avoidant, anxious-ambivalent and disorganized patterns), temperament, parenting styles, Erikson's psychosocial stages, adolescent identity, emerging adulthood.
- **Classical conditioning:** neutral, unconditioned and conditioned stimuli and responses; acquisition, extinction, spontaneous recovery, generalization, discrimination; taste aversion; higher-order conditioning.
- **Operant conditioning:** positive and negative reinforcement, positive and negative punishment (reinforcement increases a behavior, punishment decreases it; "positive" adds, "negative" removes); shaping; primary and secondary reinforcers; the four schedules (fixed and variable ratio and interval), with variable ratio the most resistant to extinction.
- **Social and cognitive learning:** observational learning and modeling (Bandura), vicarious reinforcement, latent learning (Tolman), insight learning, learned helplessness.

### Unit 4: Social Psychology and Personality

- **Attribution and person perception:** dispositional versus situational attributions, the fundamental attribution error, actor-observer bias, self-serving bias, the just-world phenomenon, explanatory style, the mere-exposure effect, self-fulfilling prophecies, social comparison.
- **Attitudes:** stereotypes, prejudice and discrimination, in-group bias, cognitive dissonance, the elaboration likelihood model (central and peripheral routes), foot-in-the-door and door-in-the-face.
- **Social situations:** conformity (Asch's line-judgment studies), obedience (Milgram's studies of obeying an authority's instructions), social facilitation, social loafing, deindividuation, group polarization, groupthink, the bystander effect and diffusion of responsibility, altruism and social norms.
- **Personality:** psychodynamic theory (id, ego, superego, defense mechanisms, projective tests); humanistic theory (Maslow's self-actualization, Rogers's unconditional positive regard and self-concept); social-cognitive theory (reciprocal determinism, self-efficacy, locus of control); trait theory and the Big Five, measured by personality inventories.
- **Motivation:** instinct, drive-reduction, arousal theory and the Yerkes-Dodson law, Maslow's hierarchy of needs, intrinsic and extrinsic motivation and the overjustification effect, hunger (the hypothalamus, ghrelin and leptin), approach and avoidance conflicts.
- **Emotion:** the James-Lange, Cannon-Bard and Schachter-Singer two-factor theories, facial feedback, display rules, universal expressions, the broaden-and-build theory.

### Unit 5: Mental and Physical Health

- **Health psychology:** stressors and the stress response, Selye's general adaptation syndrome (alarm, resistance, exhaustion), the effect of chronic stress on the immune system, problem-focused and emotion-focused coping, tend-and-befriend.
- **Positive psychology:** subjective well-being, character strengths and virtues, gratitude, resilience, posttraumatic growth.
- **Explaining and classifying disorders:** what makes behavior a disorder (distress, dysfunction, deviance), the biopsychosocial approach, the diathesis-stress model, the DSM-5-TR and its purpose, and the risk of stigma from labels. Use person-first language: "a person with schizophrenia".
- **Categories of disorders:** neurodevelopmental (autism spectrum disorder, ADHD); schizophrenia spectrum (positive and negative symptoms); depressive and bipolar disorders; anxiety disorders (generalized anxiety, panic, specific phobia, social anxiety); obsessive-compulsive and related disorders; trauma- and stressor-related disorders (PTSD); dissociative disorders; somatic symptom disorders; feeding and eating disorders; personality disorders. Know the defining features of each, not just the names.
- **Treatment:** psychodynamic, humanistic (client-centered), behavioral (exposure therapies, systematic desensitization, aversive conditioning, token economies), cognitive and cognitive behavioral therapies, group and family therapy; biomedical treatments (antidepressants, antianxiety and antipsychotic drugs, mood stabilizers, electroconvulsive therapy) and their research support; the therapeutic alliance; why treatment research uses random assignment, placebos and double-blind procedures.

If you or someone you know is struggling, talk to a trusted adult, or call or text 988 (the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline in the U.S.).

## A two-week review plan

**Week 33 (this week): rebuild the foundations.**

- Day 1: Unit 1 summary sheet and the research-methods section above. Make a one-page chart of the neurotransmitters and brain regions, then redo ten flashcards you have marked as unknown in each Unit 1 deck.
- Day 2: Unit 2 summary sheet. Draw the memory model from memory and add one example to every box; then twenty minutes of mixed multiple choice from the Unit 2 exam.
- Day 3: Unit 3 summary sheet. Write a one-line example of each of the four operant consequences and each of the four schedules, and a one-line example for each of Piaget's stages.
- Day 4: Units 4 and 5 summary sheets. For each disorder category, write the defining features in one sentence; for each therapy, write the disorder it is best supported for.
- Day 5: Free-response day. Redo one AAQ and one EBQ from two different unit exams under time (25 and 45 minutes), typing your answers, then mark them with the scoring guides.

**Week 34: the practice final.**

- Day 1: Sit questions 1–77 under AP timing, in one sitting, with a short break after question 75.
- Day 2: Score it with the explanations and the 7-point scoring guides. For every lost point, write one line: what the question tested and what you will do differently. Count your losses by unit and by science practice.
- Day 3: Re-teach yourself the two topics that cost you the most points, using their weekly guides and flashcards.
- Day 4: Questions 78–91, timed at 30 minutes.
- Day 5: Rewrite every free-response part that lost a point, naming the concept and applying it to the study, then stop. Rest matters more than one more set.

Until the exam, alternate a timed block of 25 multiple-choice questions with one typed free-response question every other day, and keep up with your flashcards: a few minutes a day of spaced retrieval beats one long cramming session, as the memory unit showed.

## Test-day reminders

- Sleep well the two nights before the exam; the sleep unit's research on memory consolidation applies to you too.
- In Section I, mark any question that takes longer than two minutes and come back to it. Answer everything.
- In Section II, read the whole prompt before the stimulus so you know what to look for, label every part, name a concept and apply it, and cite numbers and source letters as evidence.
