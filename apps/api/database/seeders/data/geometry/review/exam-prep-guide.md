# Geometry · Final Exam Prep Guide

**Practice final:** week 34, 70 questions, about 2 hours 30 minutes · **Built from:** the eight unit summaries and the week-by-week guides of this course, which follow IM 9–12 Math Geometry (Illustrative Mathematics) in our own words · **Use this guide:** in weeks 33 and 34, before and after the practice final.

## The format of the practice final

The practice final is one long paper that samples every unit of the year roughly in proportion to the weight each unit carries. The items run in unit order, so you will feel the course pass by as you work: constructions and rigid motions first, probability last.

| Part | Items | Points each | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|
| Multiple choice | 44 questions, four options each | 1 | Single best answer. Several come in sets of three on one shared figure, table or scenario. No penalty for guessing. |
| Select all that apply | 6 questions | 2 | Four or five statements; mark every true one. Partial credit for partly right answers. |
| True or false | 5 questions | 1 | Graded at once, with a scoring note explaining the answer. |
| Fill in the blank | 4 questions | 1 | One number or word; equivalent forms such as 0.5, 1/2 and 50% are all accepted where they make sense. |
| Numeric | 6 questions | 1 | A number to the stated precision; follow the rounding instruction in the prompt. |
| Short answer | 3 questions | 3 | Three labelled parts, (a), (b), (c), one point each, graded by your teacher. |
| Long answer | 2 questions | 8 | A proof or a multi-step modelling problem with a point-by-point scoring summary. |

That is 70 items and 96 points. Multiple choice, select-all, true/false and numeric items are graded the moment you submit; fill-in-the-blank items are matched automatically; the short answers and the long answers are graded by your teacher against the scoring notes attached to each item.

Sit it in one block if you can: about 75 minutes for the first 44 items, then a short break, then 75 minutes for the rest. Leave the two long answers at least 20 minutes each. If you cannot sit it in one block, split it at the unit boundaries, never in the middle of a stimulus set.

## The formulas and theorems to know cold

You may not bring a formula sheet to the practice final. Everything here was derived during the year, so each line should come with a reason attached, not just a memory.

### Angles and lines (Units 1 and 2)

- Angles on a straight line sum to 180°; vertical angles are congruent.
- Parallel lines cut by a transversal: alternate interior angles congruent, corresponding angles congruent, same-side interior angles supplementary. The converses prove lines parallel.
- The angles of a triangle sum to 180° (proved with a line through one vertex parallel to the opposite side). An exterior angle equals the sum of the two remote interior angles.
- Triangle congruence: SAS, ASA, SSS, and AAS as a consequence of ASA; HL for right triangles. SSA is not a criterion (the swinging-side picture), except when the given angle is right or obtuse, or the side opposite the angle is at least as long as the other given side.
- Perpendicular Bisector Theorem: a point is equidistant from A and B exactly when it lies on the perpendicular bisector of AB. The angle-bisector version: a point is equidistant from the two sides of an angle exactly when it lies on the bisector.
- Parallelogram facts, each with a proof by congruent triangles across a diagonal: opposite sides congruent, opposite angles congruent, diagonals bisect each other. Converses prove a quadrilateral is a parallelogram. A rhombus has perpendicular diagonals; a rectangle has congruent diagonals.

### Similarity and right triangles (Units 3 and 4)

- A dilation with centre O and scale factor k sends P to the point P′ on ray OP with OP′ = k · OP; it multiplies every length by k, preserves every angle, and sends a line not through O to a parallel line.
- Two figures are similar when a sequence of rigid motions and dilations maps one onto the other. For triangles: AA, SAS∼, SSS∼.
- A line parallel to one side of a triangle divides the other two sides proportionally (and conversely).
- Pythagorean Theorem a² + b² = c²; its converse decides whether a triangle is right. In a right triangle the altitude to the hypotenuse makes three similar triangles, which is one proof.
- 45-45-90: legs x, hypotenuse x√2. 30-60-90: short leg x, long leg x√3, hypotenuse 2x.
- sin θ = opposite/hypotenuse, cos θ = adjacent/hypotenuse, tan θ = opposite/adjacent. sin θ = cos(90° − θ). To find an angle, use the inverse: θ = tan⁻¹(opposite/adjacent), and so on.

### Solids (Unit 5)

- Scaling by k multiplies lengths by k, areas by k², volumes by k³. A cross section parallel to the base of a pyramid or cone at a fraction t of the way from the apex is a dilation of the base by t.
- Prism or cylinder: V = Bh (B the base area). Pyramid or cone: V = ⅓Bh. Sphere: V = (4/3)πr³, surface area 4πr². Cylinder lateral area 2πrh; cone lateral area πrℓ with ℓ the slant height.
- Cavalieri's principle: two solids with the same height and equal cross-sectional areas at every level have equal volumes, which is why oblique prisms and cylinders use the same formula as right ones.
- Density = mass ÷ volume; population density = people ÷ area.

### Coordinates (Unit 6)

- Distance between (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂): √((x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²). Midpoint: ((x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2).
- Circle with centre (h, k) and radius r: (x − h)² + (y − k)² = r². Complete the square to read off the centre and radius from x² + y² + Dx + Ey + F = 0.
- Parabola as a locus: the set of points equidistant from a focus and a directrix; set the two distances equal and simplify.
- Slope m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁). Point-slope form y − y₁ = m(x − x₁). Parallel lines have equal slopes; perpendicular lines have slopes whose product is −1 (one vertical, one horizontal is the other case).
- The point a fraction t of the way from A to B is A + t(B − A), a weighted average. The centroid of a triangle is the average of the vertices and divides each median 2 : 1 from the vertex.
- Coordinate proof: place the figure with convenient coordinates (a vertex at the origin, a side on an axis), keep variables for a general figure, and compute lengths, slopes or midpoints.

### Circles (Unit 7)

- A radius to the point of tangency is perpendicular to the tangent. Two tangent segments from an external point are congruent.
- An inscribed angle is half its intercepted arc (half the central angle on the same arc). An angle inscribed in a semicircle is right. Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary.
- Circumcentre: where the perpendicular bisectors meet, equidistant from the vertices. Incentre: where the angle bisectors meet, equidistant from the sides; inradius r = Area ÷ semiperimeter.
- Arc length = (θ/360°) · 2πr; sector area = (θ/360°) · πr². In radians, arc length = rθ and sector area = ½r²θ. One radian is the angle whose arc equals the radius; 180° = π radians.

### Probability (Unit 8)

- P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B). Mutually exclusive: the overlap is 0.
- P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B): the condition names the row or column of a two-way table that becomes the sample space.
- Independent exactly when P(A | B) = P(A), equivalently P(A and B) = P(A) · P(B).
- A game is fair when every player has the same probability of winning; average winnings = Σ (prize × probability).

## How to write a proof and show work for credit

Every proof item in the course is scored against a point-by-point summary, and the points are independent: a correct statement of what is given and what is to be proved earns its point even if the argument stalls afterwards. Use that.

1. **Restate the goal.** "To prove: △ABD ≅ △CBD." If you need a congruence to get a pair of equal parts, say which congruence and which parts.
2. **Name the facts you will use** before you use them: "BD is common," "AB = CB (given)," "∠ABD = ∠CBD because BD bisects ∠ABC."
3. **Cite the criterion.** SAS, ASA, SSS, AA∼, the Perpendicular Bisector Theorem, the inscribed angle theorem. A proof that reaches the right conclusion without naming the criterion usually loses the point for it.
4. **Finish with the thing asked for.** If the question asks for AD = CD, write "so AD = CD (corresponding parts of congruent triangles)." Do not stop at the congruence.
5. **Transformation proofs** must name the transformation precisely: "reflect across line BD" or "rotate 180° about the midpoint of AC," then say what maps to what and why the image lands where you claim.

For calculations:

- Write the formula first, in symbols, then substitute. "V = ⅓πr²h = ⅓π(6²)(10) = 120π ≈ 376.99."
- Keep π and square roots exact until the final line, then round once, to the precision the prompt asks for. Rounding early is the most common cause of a wrong numeric answer.
- Label units and label what each number is. A number without a label is a guess to the grader.
- When a prompt says "to three decimal places," give exactly three; the numeric items accept a small tolerance, but 0.46 is not within tolerance of 0.463.
- On the select-all items, test every statement independently. Partial credit rewards a careful partial answer and penalises a guessed extra.
- Draw the figure from its description before you compute anything. Every figure in this course is given in words with labelled points; sketch it, mark the givens, and you will see the hidden right angle, the shared side or the parallel pair that the problem hinges on.

## Where points are usually lost

- **Denylist thinking in geometry:** assuming a figure is a square or a right triangle because it looks like one in your sketch. Use only what is given or what you have proved.
- **SSA.** Two sides and a non-included angle do not prove congruence. If you cannot find an included angle or a third side, look for a right angle (HL) or a parallel line that gives you another angle.
- **k versus k² versus k³.** Lengths scale by k, areas by k², volumes by k³. A sector or similar-figure probability is a ratio of areas.
- **Reversing a conditional.** P(A | B) and P(B | A) share a numerator and differ in denominator. Read the condition first and point at its row or column.
- **Degrees in a radian formula.** Arc length rθ and sector area ½r²θ need θ in radians. If the angle is given in degrees, use the fraction-of-360° form or convert.
- **Slope of a perpendicular.** Flip and change the sign: a slope of −2/3 becomes 3/2, not 2/3 and not −3/2.
- **Pyramid and cone volumes.** Do not forget the ⅓. Do not use the slant height where the problem needs the perpendicular height, or the reverse.
- **Completing the square.** Half the coefficient, square it, add it to both sides. x² − 6x needs +9, not +36.

## A two-week review plan

The plan assumes about 45 minutes a day. Each day's first step is to re-read one unit summary, not a week guide: the summaries are the night-before sheets, and the week guides are for diving back into a specific idea you have lost.

|---|---|---|

| 3 | Unit 3 summary; dilations, AA, the parallel-side proportion, the Pythagorean proof | Re-take the week 13 quiz; redo the unit 3 exam's long answer |
| 4 | Unit 4 summary; special triangles and the three ratios | Solve ten right triangles of your own making, five for a side and five for an angle; check with a calculator |

## The night before and on the day

Re-read the formula tables and the "where points are usually lost" list, not new content. Sketch one figure of each kind from a description, to warm up the habit. On the day: pace the multiple choice at about a minute and a half each; on a stimulus set, read the whole stimulus once, carefully, before the first question; write the short answers with every part labelled; and start each long answer by stating what is given and what is to be proved or found, because that sentence earns a point on its own and makes the rest of the argument easier to find.
