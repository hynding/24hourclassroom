## What you need to be able to do

- Estimate the size of an angle given in radians without converting (1 radian is a bit less than 60°, π/2 is a right angle, 3 radians is a little less than a half turn), and sketch an angle of a given radian measure.
- Use arc length = rθ and sector area = ½r²θ with θ in radians, in both directions, including angles that are not multiples of π.
- Solve modelling problems that mix arcs, sectors, chords, tangents, inscribed angles, triangle centres and trigonometry, and compare options by cost per unit area.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |

## 1. Radian sense (7.12)

A radian is the angle whose arc is one radius long. Wrap a radius around the circle: it fits a bit more than 6 times (2π ≈ 6.28), so one radian is a little less than a sixth of a turn, about 57°.

```
Figure description: a circle with centre O and radius r. From point A on
the circle, an arc of length exactly r ends at B. The central angle AOB is
1 radian, about 57.3°, a little narrower than the 60° angle of an
equilateral triangle: the chord AB is shorter than the arc, so AB < r.

          B
         ⌒ \
       ⌒    \  r
     ⌒       \
    A─────r───O
```

Quick estimates without a calculator:

| Radians | Think | Degrees |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | half a radian | ≈ 29° |
| 1 | a radian | ≈ 57° |
| π/2 ≈ 1.57 | a right angle | 90° |
| 2 | a bit more than a right angle | ≈ 115° |
| 3 | a bit less than a half turn | ≈ 172° |
| π ≈ 3.14 | a half turn | 180° |
| 4 | between a half turn and three quarters | ≈ 229° |
| 3π/2 ≈ 4.71 | three quarters of a turn | 270° |
| 6 | just short of a full turn | ≈ 344° |

**Worked example 1 — which benchmark is nearest?** 2.5 radians: between π/2 (1.57) and π (3.14), closer to 3π/4 (2.36). About 143°, an obtuse angle.

**Worked example 2 — ordering.** Put in increasing order: 100°, 2 radians, π/3, 1.5 radians. π/3 ≈ 1.05 rad (60°), 1.5 rad ≈ 86°, 100° ≈ 1.75 rad, 2 rad ≈ 115°. Order: π/3, 1.5, 100°, 2.

**Worked example 3 — sketching.** To draw an angle of 4 radians: 4 is between π (half turn) and 3π/2 (three-quarter turn), closer to π + 1. Sketch a half turn, then about 57° more.

**Worked example 4 — fractions of a turn.** What fraction of a turn is 1 radian? 1/(2π) ≈ 0.159, about 16%. What is 0.3 of a turn in radians? 0.3 × 2π = 0.6π ≈ 1.88.

**Try it 1.1** Is 2.2 radians acute, right or obtuse? *(Answer: 2.2 > π/2 ≈ 1.57 and < π, so obtuse, about 126°.)*

**Try it 1.2** Which is larger, 5π/6 or 2.7 radians? *(Answer: 5π/6 ≈ 2.618 < 2.7, so 2.7 radians.)*

## 2. Using radians (7.13)

With θ in radians:

**arc length = rθ    sector area = ½r²θ    (and sector area = ½ · r · arc)**

**Worked example 5.** Radius 10, θ = 1.2. Arc = 12; area = ½ · 100 · 1.2 = 60. No π appears because the angle was not given in terms of π.

**Worked example 6.** Radius 7, θ = 2π/3. Arc = 14π/3 ≈ 14.66; area = ½ · 49 · 2π/3 = 49π/3 ≈ 51.31.

**Worked example 7 — backwards to the angle.** A sector of radius 6 has area 27. ½ · 36 · θ = 27, so θ = 1.5 radians ≈ 86°.

**Worked example 8 — backwards to the radius.** A sector with θ = 0.8 has area 40. ½r² · 0.8 = 40, r² = 100, r = 10. Arc = 8.

**Worked example 9 — a wheel.** A bicycle wheel of radius 35 cm turns through 2.5 radians. The bike moves 35 × 2.5 = 87.5 cm. Through 10 full turns it moves 35 × 20π ≈ 2199 cm ≈ 22 m.

**Worked example 10 — a pendulum.** A 2 m pendulum swings through an arc of 0.5 m. The angle is 0.5/2 = 0.25 radians ≈ 14.3°.

**Worked example 11 — angular speed.** A turntable spins at 2π radians every 1.8 s (33⅓ turns per minute). A point 15 cm from the centre travels 15 × 2π ≈ 94.2 cm each turn, so its speed is 94.2/1.8 ≈ 52 cm/s.

**Try it 2.1** Radius 4, θ = 2.5. Find the arc length and sector area. *(Answer: 10; 20.)*

**Try it 2.2** A sector of radius 9 has arc length 6. Find the angle (radians) and the area. *(Answer: 2/3 radian; ½ · 81 · 2/3 = 27.)*

## 3. Putting it all together (7.14)

**Worked example 12 — which pizza slice is the better deal?** Shop A: a 30 cm diameter pizza cut into 8 equal slices, $4 a slice. Shop B: a 40 cm diameter pizza cut into 12 equal slices, $5 a slice.
A: radius 15, slice area = (1/8)(225π) ≈ 88.4 cm², cost 4/88.4 ≈ 4.5 cents per cm².
B: radius 20, slice area = (1/12)(400π) ≈ 104.7 cm², cost 5/104.7 ≈ 4.8 cents per cm².
A is slightly cheaper per square centimetre, even though B's slice is bigger.

**Worked example 13 — measuring a slice with string.** A slice's curved edge measures 11 cm and its straight edge (the radius) 14 cm. Central angle 11/14 ≈ 0.786 radians ≈ 45°, so the pizza was cut into about 8 slices. Area of the slice: ½ · 14 · 11 = 77 cm².

**Worked example 14 — a round window with a chord.** A circular window of radius 60 cm has a horizontal bar 36 cm below the centre. How long is the bar, and what fraction of the window is below it? Half-chord = √(60² − 36²) = 48, so the bar is 96 cm. The angle from the centre to each end of the bar has cos = 36/60 = 0.6, so the half-angle is arccos 0.6 ≈ 0.927 rad and the full central angle is ≈ 1.855 rad. Sector area ≈ ½ · 3600 · 1.855 ≈ 3339; triangle area ½ · 96 · 36 = 1728; segment below the bar ≈ 1611 cm², about 14% of the window's 3600π ≈ 11,310 cm².

**Worked example 15 — a spotlight on a stage.** A stage backdrop runs from P to Q, 12 m apart. A spotlight must light the backdrop with a beam angle of exactly 60°, that is, ∠PLQ = 60° where L is the lamp. By the Inscribed Angle Theorem, every lamp position that works lies on an arc of a circle through P and Q on which PQ subtends an inscribed angle of 60°: the central angle is 120°, so the circle's radius satisfies 12 = 2R sin 60°, R = 12/√3 ≈ 6.93 m. There are infinitely many positions, all on that arc.

**Worked example 16 — a circular table in a triangular corner.** A triangular corner of a room has walls meeting at 90°, with the two walls 3 m and 4 m long and a 5 m third side. The largest round table that fits touching all three sides is the incircle: r = Area/s = 6/6 = 1 m.

**Worked example 17 — a sector-shaped stage.** A fan-shaped stage has radius 10 m and the front curved edge is 18 m long. Its angle is 1.8 radians ≈ 103°, its area ½ · 10 · 18 = 90 m², and the perimeter to fence is 18 + 10 + 10 = 38 m.

**Try it 3.1** Shop C sells a 36 cm diameter pizza in 6 slices at $6 a slice. Cost per cm²? *(Answer: slice area (1/6)(324π) ≈ 169.6 cm²; 6/169.6 ≈ 3.5 cents per cm², the best of the three.)*

## Common mistakes

- **Converting to degrees when you do not need to.** rθ and ½r²θ want radians; a radian answer like 1.5 is fine as it is.
- **Using rθ with degrees.** 10 × 60 is not an arc length; 60° must become π/3 first.
- **Comparing prices by slice size alone.** Compare cost per unit area (or area per dollar).
- **Mixing units** (a radius in inches with an arc in centimetres). Convert before dividing.
- **Forgetting that sector area = ½ · r · arc** is the fastest route when the arc is measured directly.
- **Rounding π too early** in a multi-step problem; carry π through and round at the end.

## Self-check

1. Without converting, say whether 3.5 radians is less than, equal to or more than a half turn.
2. Radius 8, θ = 0.75. Find the arc length and the sector area.
3. A sector of radius 5 has area 10. Find its angle in radians and in degrees.
4. A pizza of radius 18 cm is cut into 10 equal slices sold at $3 each; another of radius 12 cm into 6 slices at $2 each. Which slice is cheaper per cm²?
5. A beam of light from a lamp must make a 45° angle at the two ends of a 10 m wall. Describe where the lamp can be.

*Answers: (1) more: π ≈ 3.14 < 3.5. (2) 6; 24. (3) ½ · 25 · θ = 10, θ = 0.8 rad ≈ 45.8°. (4) first: (1/10)(324π) ≈ 101.8 cm², 2.9 cents/cm²; second: (1/6)(144π) ≈ 75.4 cm², 2.7 cents/cm²; the second.
