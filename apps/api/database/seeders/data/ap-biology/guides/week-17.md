## What you need to be able to do

The College Board phrases the learning objectives for this week as:

- **5.4** Explain deviations from Mendel's model of the inheritance of traits: incomplete dominance, codominance, multiple alleles, sex-linked genes, linked genes (with recombination frequency used to map them), genes that interact (epistasis), and non-nuclear inheritance.
- **5.5** Explain how the same genotype can result in multiple phenotypes under different environmental conditions.

In plain language: when a cross does not give 3:1 or 9:3:3:1, you must be able to say which assumption failed, predict the ratio that replaces it, and, for linked genes, turn the numbers into a map. Then you must be able to argue, from an experiment, that an environmental variable and not a genetic one caused a phenotype.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Incomplete dominance | The heterozygote is intermediate (pink snapdragons). F2 is 1:2:1 by phenotype. |
| Codominance | Both alleles are fully expressed in the heterozygote (MN blood group, AB blood). |
| Multiple alleles | More than two alleles of a gene in the population; any individual still has two. |
| Allelic series | A dominance hierarchy among multiple alleles (rabbit coat: C⁺ > cᶜʰ > cʰ > c). |
| X-linked gene | A gene on the X chromosome with no Y counterpart. |
| Hemizygous | Carrying one copy, as a male does for X-linked genes. |
| Linked genes | Genes close together on one chromosome, inherited together more often than chance. |
| Recombination frequency | Recombinants ÷ total × 100%. One percent = 1 map unit (centimorgan). |
| Epistasis | One gene masks or modifies another. Ratios such as 9:3:4 and 12:3:1. |
| Lethal allele | An allele that kills in the homozygote (2:1 among survivors) or, rarely, in the heterozygote. |
| Non-nuclear inheritance | Genes in mitochondria and chloroplasts, passed through the egg's cytoplasm. |
| X inactivation | One X per cell in female mammals is condensed into a Barr body. |
| Phenotypic plasticity | One genotype producing different phenotypes in different environments. |

## When the heterozygote gives the game away

Mendel's peas showed **complete dominance**: the heterozygote looked exactly like the dominant homozygote. Many genes do not behave that way. In **incomplete dominance** the heterozygote is intermediate because one working allele makes half the product: red × white snapdragons give pink, and selfing pink gives 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white. In **codominance** both alleles are expressed in full: a person with Lᴹ and Lᴺ has both M and N antigens on every red cell. In both cases the F2 phenotype ratio is 1:2:1 because the heterozygote can be told apart. The heterozygote decides: a blend is incomplete dominance; both products side by side is codominance.

A gene can have **multiple alleles** in a population even though each individual carries two. The ABO gene has three: Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant with each other and both are completely dominant to i. So type A is IᴬIᴬ or Iᴬi, type B is IᴮIᴮ or Iᴮi, AB is IᴬIᴮ and O is ii. An A parent and a B parent can have an O child only if both carry i, and then each of the four blood types is equally likely in the next child.

**Lethal alleles** change the ratio by removing a class. Yellow coat in mice is dominant for colour but lethal when homozygous; yellow × yellow gives 2 yellow : 1 non-yellow among the living. A dominant lethal that acts only after reproductive age can persist because carriers have already passed it on.

## Genes on the X

Females are XX, males XY, and most X genes have no partner on the Y. A male is **hemizygous**: his single allele is expressed whether it is dominant or recessive, and he passes his X to every daughter and never to a son. This produces the patterns Morgan found with white-eyed flies and that pedigrees show for colour blindness and haemophilia:

- Affected males greatly outnumber affected females; with allele frequency q, males are affected at q and females at q².
- An affected male's mother must be a carrier (or affected); his father is irrelevant.
- An affected female must have an affected father and a carrier or affected mother.
- Reciprocal crosses differ: white-eyed female × red-eyed male gives red daughters and white sons.
- Father-to-son transmission never happens.

Carrier mother × unaffected father: half the sons affected, no daughters affected, half the daughters carriers; one child in four affected overall.

Female mammals compensate for having two X chromosomes by **X inactivation**: early in development each cell condenses one X at random into a Barr body, and every descendant of that cell keeps the same X silent. A female heterozygous for an X-linked coat-colour gene is a patchwork, which is why tortoiseshell and calico cats are almost all female.

## Genes that travel together

Mendel's second law assumes genes are on different chromosomes. Genes near each other on one chromosome are **linked** and tend to be inherited as a block. In a dihybrid test cross the two **parental** classes outnumber the two **recombinant** classes, and the only way to make recombinants is a crossover between the genes in prophase I.

Sturtevant's insight was that the chance of a crossover between two genes grows with the distance between them, so **recombination frequency measures distance**: recombinants ÷ total × 100, with 1% defined as 1 map unit. Frequencies are additive, so pairwise values order the genes: the largest distance is between the outer two. Fifty percent is the ceiling, produced by independent assortment; genes on different chromosomes give 50%, and so do genes so far apart on one chromosome that crossovers between them are nearly certain. A 50% result therefore means "unlinked", not "different chromosome".

## Genes that mask genes

In **epistasis** one gene's product is needed for another gene's phenotype to show. Mouse coat colour needs gene C to make pigment at all; gene A then decides agouti (A_) versus solid black (aa). Cross AaCc × AaCc and the 3/16 A_cc and 1/16 aacc classes are both albino, so the ratio is **9 agouti : 3 black : 4 albino** (recessive epistasis). In summer squash a dominant W allele makes fruit white whatever gene Y says, ww Y_ is yellow and wwyy is green: **12 : 3 : 1** (dominant epistasis). Every epistatic ratio still sums to 16 because independent assortment is intact; only the mapping from genotype to phenotype has changed.

## Genes outside the nucleus

Mitochondria and chloroplasts carry small circular genomes and are inherited with the cytoplasm, which comes almost entirely from the egg. A mitochondrial disorder passes from an affected mother to all her children and from an affected father to none, with no Mendelian ratio. In variegated four o'clock plants a seedling's colour depends only on the branch that bore the seed.

## Environment and phenotype (5.5)

A genotype sets the range of possible phenotypes; the environment picks one. The **Himalayan rabbit** and **Siamese cat** carry a temperature-sensitive tyrosinase that works only below about 35 °C, so pigment forms on the cool extremities and not on the warm trunk; chill a shaved back patch and it regrows dark. **Hydrangeas** flower blue in acidic soil (aluminium dissolves and is taken up) and pink in neutral soil, on the same plant. **Arctic foxes** and snowshoe hares switch from brown to white fur as day length shortens. In many **turtles and crocodilians** incubation temperature, not a chromosome, decides sex. Human height is **polygenic** and continuous, and identical twins raised apart still differ.

The experimental logic is always the same: hold genotype constant (clones, cuttings, identical twins, one animal in two conditions), vary the environment, and attribute any difference to the environment.

## Worked example 1: from Morgan's counts to a map distance

F1 females heterozygous for body colour and wing shape (b⁺ vg⁺ / b vg) were test-crossed to black vestigial males: 965 gray normal, 944 black vestigial, 206 gray vestigial, 185 black normal; 2,300 in all.

1. Identify the parental classes: the two large ones, gray normal and black vestigial, which match the chromosomes the F1 inherited.
2. Recombinants = 206 + 185 = 391.
3. Recombination frequency = 391 ÷ 2,300 × 100 = **17.0%**, so the genes are 17 map units apart.
4. Check against independent assortment: 1:1:1:1 would give about 575 per class. The deficit of recombinants is the evidence of linkage.

If the F1 had carried b⁺ vg / b vg⁺ instead, the large classes would be gray vestigial and black normal, and the frequency would still be 17%: distance is a property of the chromosome, "parental" is a property of the parents.

## Worked example 2: ordering three genes

Pairwise recombination frequencies: a–b 6%, b–c 14%, a–c 20%. The largest value, a–c, is between the outer genes, so b is in the middle: **a — 6 — b — 14 — c.** Check: 6 + 14 = 20. In real data the long interval often comes out slightly short of the sum, because a double crossover between a and c restores the parental a–c combination and goes uncounted, while each single crossover is still counted in its own short interval.

## Worked example 3: an X-linked pedigree

A woman with normal vision whose father was colour-blind marries a man with normal vision.

- Genotypes: she is XᴮXᵇ (her father could only give her Xᵇ); her father XᵇY; her husband XᴮY.
- Sons: 1/2 colour-blind. Daughters: 0 colour-blind, 1/2 carriers.
- Any child: 1/2 (son) × 1/2 = **1/4**.

Why more men? One Xᵇ is enough for a man; a woman needs two, including one from an affected father.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Codominance and incomplete dominance are the same."** Pink is a blend (incomplete); AB blood shows both antigens (codominance).
- **"Multiple alleles means an individual has many alleles."** The population does; each diploid individual has two.
- **"A father can pass an X-linked trait to his son."** A son gets his father's Y. X-linked alleles go father to daughter.
- **"50% recombination proves different chromosomes."** It proves independent assortment, which far-apart genes on one chromosome also show.
- **"Epistasis breaks independent assortment."** The genes still assort independently; the ratio still sums to 16.
- **"The environment changes the genotype."** A chilled rabbit's DNA is unchanged; its enzyme's activity is.
- **"Recombinants are whichever classes the question lists first."** They are the two smallest classes of a test cross, with combinations neither parental chromosome carried.
