## What you need to be able to do

- **1.3** Describe how the structure of monomers determines the properties of the polymers they form, and explain how polymers are built and broken (dehydration synthesis and hydrolysis).
- **1.4** Describe the structure of carbohydrates and explain how the linkage between monomers determines a polysaccharide's function (storage versus structure).
- **1.5** Describe the structure of lipids, and explain how fatty-acid saturation and the amphipathic nature of phospholipids relate to their roles.

The exam asks you to reason from **structure to function**: "this bond geometry means this shape, which means this property." Memorising names of sugars is less valuable than being able to say *why* cellulose is a wall and starch is a pantry.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Monomer / polymer | A single building block / a chain of them joined by covalent bonds. |
| Dehydration synthesis | Joining two monomers by removing a water molecule: the –H of one and the –OH of the other leave together. Needs energy and an enzyme. |
| Hydrolysis | Breaking a bond by adding water: one fragment gets –H, the other –OH. Releases energy; digestion is hydrolysis. |
| Monosaccharide | A single sugar, (CH₂O)ₙ, three to seven carbons. Glucose, fructose and galactose are all C₆H₁₂O₆. |
| Isomer | Same formula, different arrangement. Glucose and fructose differ in where the carbonyl sits (aldose versus ketose). |
| Glycosidic linkage | The covalent bond between two sugars made by dehydration synthesis. |
| α and β linkage | In a ring, the –OH on carbon 1 points down (α) or up (β). α 1-4 links build starch and glycogen; β 1-4 links build cellulose. |
| Starch | Plant glucose store: amylose (unbranched α 1-4, coils into a helix) and amylopectin (branched with α 1-6 links). |
| Glycogen | Animal glucose store in liver and muscle. Like amylopectin but more branched, so glucose can be released fast. |
| Cellulose | Plant cell-wall polymer: straight β 1-4 chains that hydrogen-bond side by side into rigid fibres. Humans cannot hydrolyse the β link. |
| Chitin | A nitrogen-containing polysaccharide: arthropod exoskeletons and fungal cell walls. |
| Triglyceride | Glycerol plus three fatty acids joined by ester bonds; three waters released. Energy store. |
| Saturated / unsaturated | A fatty acid with no C=C double bonds (straight, packs tightly, solid at room temperature) / with one or more (kinked if cis, packs loosely, liquid). |
| Trans fat | An unsaturated fatty acid whose double bond is trans, so the chain stays straight and packs like a saturated one. |
| Phospholipid | Glycerol with two fatty acids and a phosphate head: a hydrophilic head and hydrophobic tails on one molecule (amphipathic). |
| Steroid | Four fused carbon rings. Cholesterol stiffens animal membranes and is the precursor of steroid hormones and vitamin D. |

## Building and breaking polymers

Every polymer in this unit is assembled by the same reaction and taken apart by its reverse.

**Dehydration synthesis**: monomer–OH + H–monomer → monomer–monomer + H₂O. Energy goes in; an enzyme speeds it.

**Hydrolysis**: monomer–monomer + H₂O → monomer–OH + H–monomer. Energy comes out; this is what amylase, lipase and the proteases in your gut do.

Two things to notice. First, the *same* monomer (glucose) can make polymers with opposite jobs depending on how the links are made. Second, because water is the molecule removed or inserted, a cell's water chemistry from week 1 is never far away.

## Carbohydrates: one monomer, three different polymers

| Polymer | Monomer and linkage | Shape | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amylose (starch) | glucose, α 1-4 | loose helix | plant energy store |
| Amylopectin (starch) | glucose, α 1-4 with α 1-6 branches | branched | plant energy store, faster release |
| Glycogen | glucose, α 1-4 with many α 1-6 branches | highly branched | animal energy store, very fast release |
| Cellulose | glucose, β 1-4 | straight chains, hydrogen-bonded into fibres | plant cell wall |

The α link lets a chain curl; the β link, which flips every other glucose over, makes a straight ribbon. Straight ribbons lie flat against each other and hydrogen-bond along their whole length (week 1 again), which is why cellulose is strong and insoluble and why only organisms with cellulase, such as the microbes in a cow's rumen or a termite's gut, can digest it.

Branches matter for speed. A branched store has many chain ends, and enzymes that release glucose work from the ends, so glycogen can supply a sprinting muscle far faster than an unbranched polymer could.

Carbohydrates yield about 4 kcal per gram, less than half of fat, but they are the quickest fuel to mobilise and, as fibre, they do structural and digestive work that fat cannot.

## Lipids: not polymers, but still structure-to-function

Lipids are grouped by behaviour (they are hydrophobic) rather than by a shared monomer.

**Triglycerides** store energy at about 9 kcal per gram, more than twice carbohydrate, because their long C–H chains are electron-rich and nonpolar; they also pack without the water that hydrates glycogen, so the store is light. The fatty-acid tails decide the physical state:

- Saturated tails are straight and nestle together: butter, lard, solid at room temperature.
- Cis-unsaturated tails have a kink at each double bond and cannot pack: olive and canola oils, liquid.
- Trans-unsaturated tails are straight despite the double bond, so they behave like saturated fats; artificial hydrogenation makes them and they raise LDL cholesterol.

**Phospholipids** are the week's most important molecule because of what water does to them. The phosphate head is charged and hydrophilic; the two tails are hydrophobic. In water the only arrangement that satisfies both ends is a **bilayer**, heads out, tails in. That self-assembly is the basis of every membrane in Unit 2.

**Steroids** are four fused rings. Cholesterol sits among the phospholipid tails of animal membranes and tunes their fluidity; it is also the starting material for testosterone, estradiol, cortisol and vitamin D.

**Waxes** are fatty acids esterified to long-chain alcohols: the waterproof coat on leaves and feathers.

## Worked example 1: predicting a polymer's properties from its linkage

*Question:* A newly discovered polysaccharide is made of glucose joined entirely by β 1-4 linkages with no branches. Predict two properties and justify each.

*Reasoning:* β 1-4 links produce straight chains (as in cellulose). Prediction 1: the chains will hydrogen-bond to one another and form insoluble fibres, so the polymer is likely structural. Prediction 2: it will resist human digestive enzymes, which recognise only α links. Both predictions come from one fact about bond geometry.

## Worked example 2: reading a melting-point table

| Fatty acid | Carbons | C=C double bonds | Melting point (°C) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stearic | 18 | 0 | 69 |
| Oleic (cis) | 18 | 1 | 13 |
| Linoleic (cis) | 18 | 2 | −5 |
| Elaidic (trans) | 18 | 1 | 45 |

*Question:* What does the table show about the effect of double bonds, and what is the role of elaidic acid in the argument?

*Reasoning:* All four have 18 carbons, so chain length is controlled. Each cis double bond lowers the melting point sharply (69 → 13 → −5): kinks prevent packing, so less energy is needed to separate the molecules. Elaidic acid has the same single double bond as oleic acid but in the trans geometry, and its melting point is close to the saturated acid's. That comparison isolates *geometry* from *number* of double bonds: it is the kink, not the double bond itself, that lowers the melting point.

## Worked example 3: energy per gram

A gram of fat releases about 9 kcal; a gram of carbohydrate about 4.3 kcal. A migrating bird that stores 20 g of fuel for a flight carries roughly 180 kcal as fat but only 86 kcal as glycogen, and the glycogen would also bring about three times its own mass in bound water. That is why long-distance animals store fat and why glycogen is for the next few minutes, not the next few days.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Hydrolysis builds, dehydration breaks."** Reversed. Dehydration *removes* water to *build*; hydrolysis *adds* water to *break*. Remember "lysis" means splitting.
- **"Glucose and fructose are different because they have different formulas."** Both are C₆H₁₂O₆. They differ in arrangement: isomers.
- **"Cellulose is indigestible because it is too big."** Starch is just as big. The difference is the β link, which human enzymes do not fit.
- **"Unsaturated fats are liquid because they have fewer hydrogens."** The hydrogen count is a symptom; the cause is the kink from each cis double bond preventing close packing.
- **"Lipids are polymers of fatty acids."** A triglyceride has exactly three fatty acids on a glycerol; it is not a repeating chain. Lipids are grouped by solubility, not by monomer.
- **"The phosphate head of a phospholipid is the hydrophobic part."** The head is charged and loves water; the fatty-acid tails are the hydrophobic part.
