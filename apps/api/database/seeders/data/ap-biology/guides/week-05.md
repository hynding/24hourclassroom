## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Fluid mosaic model | Singer and Nicolson, 1972: a bilayer of phospholipids with proteins, cholesterol and glycolipids scattered through it, all free to move sideways. |
| Amphipathic | Having a hydrophilic region and a hydrophobic region; phospholipids and integral proteins both are. |
| Integral protein | Embedded in the bilayer, with 20-25 nonpolar amino acids per membrane-spanning segment. |
| Peripheral protein | Attached to one surface of the membrane, not threaded through it. |
| Glycoprotein, glycolipid | Membrane protein or lipid with sugar chains attached, always on the outer face; together the glycocalyx. |
| Cholesterol | A steroid between the tails that keeps a cold membrane fluid and a warm one from loosening. |
| Selective permeability | Letting some substances cross and not others, set by the hydrophobic core and the proteins present. |
| Concentration gradient | A difference in concentration across a membrane; stored potential energy. |
| Electrochemical gradient | The combined concentration and electrical forces acting on an ion. |
| Membrane potential | The voltage across the membrane, inside negative (about −70 mV in a neuron). |
| Passive transport | Movement down a gradient with no energy spent by the cell. |
| Active transport | Movement against a gradient, powered by ATP directly (primary) or by a gradient another pump built (secondary). |
| Sodium-potassium pump | Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase: 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in, one ATP per cycle. Electrogenic. |

## The membrane as a fluid mosaic

The plasma membrane is a sheet 5-10 nm thick, far below what a light microscope can resolve, yet it decides everything that enters or leaves a cell. By mass it is about half protein, 40% lipid and 10% carbohydrate, but the ratio follows the job: myelin, an insulator, is three-quarters lipid; a mitochondrial inner membrane is three-quarters protein.

**Phospholipids** are the sheet. Each has a charged phosphate head and two hydrocarbon tails, and in water (week 2) the only stable arrangement is a bilayer with heads out and tails in. Nothing but hydrophobic exclusion holds it together, so every molecule drifts sideways past its neighbours; the membrane is a two-dimensional fluid that flows, self-seals and can bud or fuse. Fluidity depends on three things: temperature (warmer is more fluid), the saturation of the tails (cis double bonds put kinks in the tails that stop them packing, so unsaturated membranes stay fluid in the cold) and **cholesterol**, whose rigid rings sit among the tails and buffer fluidity in both directions. Organisms adjust the first two: fish in cold water build membranes richer in unsaturated fatty acids.

**Proteins** are the machinery. **Integral** proteins span the bilayer with one or more stretches of about 20-25 nonpolar amino acids, usually an α helix, flanked by polar regions that sit in the water on either side; you can read a membrane protein's location straight from its sequence (week 3). They are the channels, carriers, pumps, receptors and enzymes. **Peripheral** proteins sit on a surface, attached to integral proteins or to lipid heads, and anchor the cytoskeleton or catalyse reactions at the interface.

**Carbohydrates** are the labels. Short sugar chains attached to proteins (glycoproteins) or lipids (glycolipids) face outward only, forming the **glycocalyx**. They identify the cell to its neighbours and to the immune system; blood groups are differences in these sugars. The two faces of the membrane are therefore different, and this asymmetry is set up in the ER and preserved by vesicle traffic.

## Selective permeability

The hydrophobic core is the gatekeeper. A substance crosses a bare bilayer in proportion to how well it dissolves in oil:

- **Small nonpolar molecules** (O₂, CO₂, N₂, steroid hormones, fat-soluble vitamins) dissolve in the core and pass freely.
- **Small polar molecules** (water, glycerol, urea) slip through slowly; water crosses a pure bilayer about a hundred times more slowly than oxygen.
- **Large polar molecules** (glucose, amino acids) hardly cross at all.
- **Ions** (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻, Ca²⁺) essentially cannot cross: each carries a full charge and a shell of bound water (week 1), and stripping that shell to enter the oil is far too costly.

Size matters much less than polarity. Na⁺ is smaller than glucose and crosses a billion times more slowly. Everything in the last three groups needs a protein, and that is where the cell gets control: by choosing which proteins to put in the membrane, it chooses what crosses.

The **cell wall** of plants, fungi and bacteria is a different kind of boundary: a porous mesh outside the membrane, freely permeable to water and small solutes, that provides shape and resists pressure. The wall holds the shape; the membrane chooses.

## Passive and active transport

A **concentration gradient** is stored energy. Molecules spread from high to low concentration on their own, and the cell can harvest that movement; pushing molecules the other way costs energy. The rule for classifying any transport event is to ask which way the substance moves relative to its own gradient.

**Passive transport** is down the gradient at no cost to the cell: simple diffusion through the bilayer, facilitated diffusion through a protein (next week), and osmosis. Rate depends on the steepness of the gradient, temperature, surface area, membrane thickness and the molecule's size and solubility.

**Active transport** is up the gradient and must be paid for. In **primary** active transport a pump hydrolyses ATP directly. The **sodium-potassium pump** is the model: three Na⁺ bind inside; ATP phosphorylates the pump; it changes shape, opens outward and releases the Na⁺ as its affinity drops; two K⁺ bind outside; the phosphate leaves; the pump returns to its first shape and releases the K⁺ inside. Each cycle costs one ATP and exports a net positive charge, which is why the pump is called **electrogenic** and why it contributes to the inside-negative **membrane potential**. A resting cell holds roughly 140 mM K⁺ inside against 5 mM outside, and 15 mM Na⁺ inside against 145 mM outside, and spends a large fraction of its ATP keeping it that way, because ions leak back through channels all the time.

In **secondary** active transport the cell spends a gradient instead of ATP. Na⁺ flowing back into a gut cell down the gradient the pump built passes through a **symporter** that drags glucose in with it, up glucose's own gradient. No ATP is hydrolysed by the symporter; the ATP was spent earlier by the pump. Block the pump and glucose uptake stops within minutes as the Na⁺ gradient drains. Ions feel both concentration and charge, so the force on them is the **electrochemical gradient**: K⁺ is pushed out by concentration and pulled in by the negative interior, and the two nearly cancel at rest.

## Worked example 1: a permeability table

| Substance | Polar or charged? | Relative permeability through pure bilayer |
|---|---|---|
| O₂ | nonpolar | 1 |
| H₂O | polar | 0.01 |
| Glucose | polar, large | 10⁻⁶ |
| Na⁺ | charged | 10⁻¹¹ |

*Question:* Where would ethanol (small, slightly polar) and a steroid (large, nonpolar) fall?

*Reasoning:* Ethanol is small and only mildly polar, so between O₂ and water, near the top. A steroid is nonpolar; despite its size it dissolves in the core and sits near O₂, which is why steroid hormones have receptors inside cells. Place molecules by polarity first, size second. The gap between the bare bilayer and a real cell is the work of proteins: a kidney cell moves water a hundred times faster than this table allows because of aquaporins.

## Worked example 2: reading a fluidity experiment

Mouse and human cells were fused and their surface proteins labelled in different colours. After 40 minutes at 37 °C, 93% of hybrids had fully mixed colours; at 15 °C only 15%; at 37 °C with ATP synthesis blocked, 91%.

*Reasoning:* The proteins moved, so the membrane is fluid (mosaic model, not a rigid sheet). Cooling slowed mixing, so movement depends on lipid fluidity, which falls as tails pack in the cold. Blocking ATP changed nothing, so the movement is passive diffusion within the plane, not active transport. Three columns, three conclusions; a good answer draws one from each.

## Worked example 3: pump arithmetic

*Question:* A pump runs 1,000 cycles per second. How much Na⁺ leaves, how much K⁺ enters, how much ATP is used, and what is the net charge moved?

*Reasoning:* 3,000 Na⁺ out, 2,000 K⁺ in, 1,000 ATP spent, net 1,000 positive charges exported. The net charge is the electrogenic contribution to the membrane potential.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Facilitated transport is active because a protein is involved."** Proteins provide a path, not energy. Only movement against a gradient is active.
- **"Small things cross membranes; big things don't."** Polarity and charge decide. Na⁺ is tiny and excluded; a steroid is large and passes.
- **"The sodium-potassium pump moves ions down their gradients."** Both ions move uphill; that is why it needs ATP.
- **"Secondary active transport doesn't need energy."** It needs the gradient, which cost ATP to build. Poison ATP synthesis and it stops.
- **"Membrane sugars are on both faces."** Only the outside. The asymmetry is set in the ER.
