Both topics are about the same idea: **a linear sequence of monomers carries information, and interactions between the monomers' side groups turn that sequence into a three-dimensional shape with a job.**

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Nucleotide | A pentose sugar, a phosphate group and a nitrogenous base. The monomer of DNA and RNA. |
| Deoxyribose / ribose | DNA's sugar lacks the –OH at the 2′ carbon that ribose has. That one group makes RNA more reactive and less stable. |
| Purine / pyrimidine | Two-ring bases (adenine, guanine) / one-ring bases (cytosine, thymine, uracil). A purine always pairs with a pyrimidine, so the helix has a constant width. |
| Phosphodiester bond | The covalent link between the 3′ –OH of one nucleotide's sugar and the 5′ phosphate of the next. Made by dehydration synthesis. |
| 5′ and 3′ ends | The end carrying a free phosphate on carbon 5 of the sugar / the end carrying a free hydroxyl on carbon 3. A strand is read and built 5′ → 3′. |
| Antiparallel | The two DNA strands run in opposite directions: one 5′ → 3′, the other 3′ → 5′. |
| Complementary base pairing | A pairs with T (two hydrogen bonds), G with C (three). RNA uses U in place of T. |
| mRNA, rRNA, tRNA | Messenger (the copy of a gene), ribosomal (the structural and catalytic core of the ribosome), transfer (brings the amino acid). |
| Amino acid | A central α carbon bonded to an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen and a variable R group. Twenty kinds. |
| R group | The side chain that differs between amino acids: nonpolar, polar, positively charged, negatively charged, or aromatic. |
| Peptide bond | The covalent link between the carboxyl of one amino acid and the amino group of the next; dehydration synthesis. |
| Primary structure | The sequence of amino acids, written from the N-terminus to the C-terminus. Encoded by DNA. |
| Secondary structure | Local folding of the backbone held by hydrogen bonds between backbone C=O and N–H groups: α helix and β pleated sheet. R groups are not involved. |
| Tertiary structure | The overall 3-D fold of one polypeptide, held by interactions between R groups: hydrophobic clustering, hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bridges. |
| Quaternary structure | The arrangement of two or more polypeptides in one protein (haemoglobin has four). |
| Denaturation | Loss of shape (and function) without breaking the primary sequence, caused by heat, pH or salt. Sometimes reversible. |
| Chaperone | A protein that helps another fold correctly. |

## Nucleic acids: a backbone with a readable edge

A DNA strand is a chain of nucleotides joined sugar-to-phosphate. The sugar-phosphate backbone is identical all the way along; the information is the order of the bases hanging off it. Because each phosphodiester bond links a 3′ carbon to a 5′ carbon, the chain has two chemically different ends, and every enzyme that reads or builds DNA respects that direction.

Two strands pair by hydrogen bonding between bases. A–T makes two hydrogen bonds; G–C makes three. The pairing rule has three consequences worth stating in an exam answer:

1. The strands are **complementary**: knowing one tells you the other.
2. The strands are **antiparallel**: for the bases to meet, one strand must run the opposite way.
3. A G–C-rich stretch is held more tightly than an A–T-rich one, because more hydrogen bonds must be broken to separate it.

RNA differs in three ways: ribose instead of deoxyribose, uracil instead of thymine, and it is usually single-stranded, which lets it fold back on itself (tRNA's cloverleaf) and act as a catalyst (rRNA makes the peptide bond).

## Proteins: sequence becomes shape becomes function

Twenty amino acids share the same backbone and differ only in their R groups. When a ribosome links them by peptide bonds, the result is a specific **primary** sequence. What happens next is chemistry, not instruction:

- Backbone atoms hydrogen-bond to one another and pull stretches of the chain into **α helices** and **β sheets** (secondary structure).
- R groups then sort themselves. In a watery cytoplasm, nonpolar R groups cluster inside, away from water; polar and charged R groups face outward; oppositely charged R groups form ionic bonds; two cysteines can form a covalent **disulfide bridge**. The result is the **tertiary** fold.
- Some proteins are assemblies of several folded chains (**quaternary** structure).

The chain of causation is the point: **a change in the sequence can change the shape, and a change in the shape changes the function.** Sickle-cell haemoglobin is the standard example. One of about 600 amino acids changes, glutamate (charged) to valine (nonpolar), at position 6 of the β chain. The new hydrophobic patch on the protein's surface sticks to the same patch on neighbouring molecules when oxygen is low; the molecules polymerise into fibres, and the cell is pulled into a sickle.

**Denaturation** is the same idea run backwards. Heat, extreme pH or high salt break the weak interactions that hold tertiary structure (not the peptide bonds), the protein unfolds, and its function stops. A fried egg is irreversibly denatured albumin. Many enzymes, however, refold once conditions return, which is why the exam asks you to distinguish "loses shape" from "loses sequence".

## Worked example 1: predicting where a mutation matters

*Question:* Two mutations each change one amino acid in a soluble enzyme. Mutation A swaps leucine for isoleucine (both nonpolar) on the protein's surface. Mutation B swaps leucine for lysine (positively charged) in the hydrophobic core. Predict which is more likely to destroy the enzyme's activity and justify.

*Reasoning:* Mutation B. A charged R group in the hydrophobic core cannot be shielded from water by its neighbours, so the core will not pack; the tertiary fold is disrupted and the active site, which depends on that fold, loses its shape. Mutation A exchanges one nonpolar group for a similar one in a position that interacts mostly with water, so the fold is barely perturbed. The general rule: **chemistry of the R group and location in the fold together decide the effect**, not the mere fact of a change.

## Worked example 2: reading a DNA sequence

*Question:* One strand of a DNA fragment reads 5′-ATGGCCTTA-3′. Write the complementary strand with its ends labelled, and state how many hydrogen bonds hold the fragment together.

*Reasoning:* Pair each base (A↔T, G↔C) and remember the partner runs the other way. Written under the original it is 3′-TACCGGAAT-5′; written on its own in the conventional 5′ → 3′ direction it is 5′-TAAGGCCAT-3′. Now count pairs by kind: A–T pairs at positions 1, 2, 7, 8 and 9 (five pairs × 2 bonds = 10) and G–C pairs at positions 3, 4, 5 and 6 (four pairs × 3 bonds = 12), so 22 hydrogen bonds hold the fragment together. The exam usually gives a shorter sequence; the method is the same: write the complement, flip the ends, count each kind of pair, multiply, add.

## Worked example 3: a denaturation graph

A student measures the activity of an enzyme at pH 2, 4, 6, 7, 8 and 10 and finds a sharp peak at pH 7 falling to near zero on both sides.

*Reasoning:* At the optimum, the ionic bonds and hydrogen bonds among R groups hold the active site in the right shape. Adding H⁺ (low pH) protonates negatively charged carboxylate R groups and removing it (high pH) deprotonates positively charged amino R groups; either way, ionic bonds that held the fold are lost and the active site deforms. The peptide bonds are untouched, so if the pH is restored the enzyme may refold. Note that a drop of one pH unit is a tenfold change in H⁺ (week 1).

## Common misconceptions

- **"Secondary structure is held by R-group interactions."** No: by hydrogen bonds between *backbone* C=O and N–H. R groups drive *tertiary* structure.
- **"Hydrogen bonds hold the DNA backbone together."** The backbone is covalent phosphodiester bonds. Hydrogen bonds join the bases across the two strands.
- **"A mutation that changes one amino acid always destroys the protein."** It depends on where and what: a conservative swap on the surface may do nothing; a charge in the core can ruin it.
- **"Denatured proteins have lost their primary structure."** Denaturation breaks weak interactions; the peptide bonds remain. That is why some proteins refold.
- **"DNA strands are parallel."** They are antiparallel; the 5′ end of one lies against the 3′ end of the other.
- **"RNA is just single-stranded DNA."** Different sugar (ribose, with a 2′ –OH), different base (uracil), and it folds into functional shapes and can catalyse reactions.
