## What you need to be able to do

The College Board phrases the learning objectives for this week as:

- **5.1** Explain how meiosis results in the transmission of chromosomes from one generation to the next, and describe the similarities and differences between mitosis and meiosis.
- **5.2** Explain how the process of meiosis generates genetic diversity, including the roles of crossing over, independent assortment and random fertilisation.

In plain language: you must be able to follow one chromosome through both divisions and say, at every stage, how many chromosomes and chromatids are in the cell and whether it is haploid or diploid; you must be able to say what meiosis I does that mitosis never does; and you must be able to explain, with arithmetic, why no two gametes from one parent are the same.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Diploid (2n) / haploid (n) | Two sets of chromosomes, one from each parent / one set. Human body cells 46, gametes 23. |
| Homologous chromosomes | The two members of a pair: same length, same centromere position, same genes in the same order, possibly different alleles. One came from each parent. |
| Sister chromatids | The two identical copies of a chromosome made in S phase, held together at the centromere by cohesin until anaphase II. |
| Synapsis | The gene-by-gene pairing of homologues in prophase I, zipped together by the synaptonemal complex. |
| Tetrad (bivalent) | A synapsed pair of homologues: four chromatids. |
| Crossing over | The exchange of segments between non-sister chromatids of a tetrad. |
| Chiasma (plural chiasmata) | The visible point of exchange. It holds the pair together until anaphase I; at least one per pair is needed for proper separation. |
| Recombinant chromatid | A chromatid carrying a combination of alleles neither parent chromosome had. |
| Independent assortment | Each tetrad orients at random on the metaphase I plate, independently of every other tetrad. |
| Reductional / equational division | Meiosis I halves the chromosome number (2n → n); meiosis II keeps it at n and separates chromatids. |
| Nondisjunction | Failure of homologues (meiosis I) or sister chromatids (meiosis II) to separate; produces gametes with n + 1 or n − 1 chromosomes. |
| Red Queen hypothesis | Species coevolve with their enemies, so a population must keep generating new combinations just to keep its place. |

## One replication, two divisions

Meiosis starts exactly as mitosis does. In interphase the cell grows (G1), replicates every chromosome (S) and prepares to divide (G2). After S phase each chromosome is two sister chromatids joined at the centromere.

The difference is what happens next. Mitosis divides once and separates sister chromatids, giving two identical diploid cells. Meiosis divides twice. The first division separates **homologous chromosomes**; the second separates **sister chromatids**. The result is four haploid cells, none of them identical.

**Prophase I** is long and is where almost everything unique to meiosis happens. Each chromosome finds its homologue and the two pair along their length, gene for gene (**synapsis**), held by a protein lattice called the synaptonemal complex. Inside the complex, at recombination nodules, enzymes cut both strands of a chromatid from each homologue and rejoin them crosswise. That is **crossing over**, and when the complex disassembles the pair stays attached at the exchange points, the **chiasmata**. The chiasmata are what hold the tetrad together through metaphase I; a pair with no chiasma risks going to the same pole.

**Metaphase I.** Tetrads, not individual chromosomes, line up on the plate, one homologue facing each pole. The orientation of each tetrad is random and has no effect on its neighbours. This is **independent assortment**.

**Anaphase I.** The chiasmata are released and the homologues move apart. Sister chromatids stay attached at their centromeres. This is the moment the chromosome number is halved: each pole receives one chromosome of each pair, still doubled.

**Telophase I and cytokinesis.** Two haploid cells, each chromosome still made of two chromatids. A short interkinesis follows with no S phase.

**Meiosis II** looks like mitosis in a haploid cell: chromosomes line up individually at the metaphase II plate, centromeres split at anaphase II and sister chromatids go to opposite poles. Four haploid cells result. They are not identical, because crossing over in prophase I already made the sister chromatids different, and independent assortment already gave the two meiosis I products different chromosome mixtures.

## Meiosis against mitosis

| Feature | Mitosis | Meiosis I | Meiosis II |
|---|---|---|---|
| DNA replication before it | Yes (S phase) | Yes (S phase) | No |
| Homologues pair (synapsis) | No | Yes | No |
| Crossing over | No | Yes | No |
| What lines up at the plate | Individual chromosomes | Tetrads | Individual chromosomes |
| What separates at anaphase | Sister chromatids | Homologues | Sister chromatids |
| Ploidy change | 2n → 2n | 2n → n | n → n |
| Products | 2 identical cells | 2 different cells | 4 different cells in total |

The useful slogan: **homologues apart in I, sisters apart in II.** Every chromosome-counting question in this unit turns on it.

## Where the variation comes from

Mutation is the only source of new alleles. Sexual reproduction does something different: it shuffles existing alleles into new combinations, three ways.

1. **Crossing over** (prophase I) builds chromosomes that carry a mixture of maternal and paternal alleles along their length. Genes that sit close together on one chromosome tend to stay together; genes far apart are separated more often. That gradient is what week 17 turns into a map.
2. **Independent assortment** (metaphase I) decides which member of each pair a gamete receives. With n pairs there are 2ⁿ combinations. For humans 2²³ is about 8.4 million, and that is before a single crossover is counted.
3. **Random fertilisation** multiplies the parents' gamete combinations: 2²³ × 2²³ is roughly 70 trillion possible zygote chromosome combinations from one couple.

Why bother? Asexual reproduction is faster: every individual reproduces. OpenStax frames the answer with the **Red Queen hypothesis**: predators, prey, parasites and hosts coevolve, so a population must keep changing simply to keep its place. A parasite adapted to the common genotype will devastate a clone; a sexual population keeps presenting new combinations. Variation is what selection acts on, which is where Unit 7 begins.

## Three life cycles

All sexual life cycles alternate between haploid and diploid, and all contain one meiosis and one fertilisation; they differ in which stage builds a body.

- **Diploid-dominant** (almost all animals): the body is diploid and meiosis makes the gametes directly.
- **Haploid-dominant** (most fungi, some algae): the body is haploid; a brief diploid zygote undergoes meiosis at once to make four haploid spores.
- **Alternation of generations** (plants, some algae): a haploid gametophyte makes gametes by *mitosis*; the diploid sporophyte makes spores by *meiosis*. In mosses the gametophyte is the visible plant; in seed plants it is a few cells inside the flower.

## Worked example 1: counting through a division

A cell with 2n = 8 enters meiosis. Fill in the table before you read the answers.

| Stage | Chromosomes per cell | Chromatids per cell | Ploidy |
|---|---|---|---|
| G1 | 8 | 8 | 2n |
| After S phase | 8 | 16 | 2n |
| Metaphase I | 8 (as 4 tetrads) | 16 | 2n |
| After meiosis I | 4 | 8 | n |
| Metaphase II | 4 | 8 | n |
| After meiosis II | 4 | 4 | n |

Rules: count chromosomes by centromeres; replication doubles chromatids but not chromosomes; meiosis I halves chromosomes without changing chromatids per chromosome; meiosis II halves chromatids per chromosome without changing the chromosome count. The same table for mitosis ends at 8 chromosomes, 8 chromatids, 2n.

## Worked example 2: the Sordaria crossover count (AP Investigation 7)

The fungus *Sordaria fimicola* packages the four products of one meiosis, each copied once by mitosis, as eight spores in a row inside an ascus. Cross a black-spore wild type with a tan mutant and the hybrid asci show one of two things. If no crossover happened between the spore-colour gene and the centromere, the alleles separate at meiosis I and the ascus reads 4 black : 4 tan. If a crossover did happen, each homologue now carries one of each allele, the alleles separate only at meiosis II, and the ascus reads 2:2:2:2 or 2:4:2.

A class counts 128 asci of the 4:4 type, 52 of 2:2:2:2 and 40 of 2:4:2.

- Crossover asci = 52 + 40 = 92 of 220, so 41.8% of asci show a crossover.
- Only two of the four chromatids in a tetrad take part in any one crossover, so half the spores in a crossover ascus are still parental. Map distance = 41.8 ÷ 2 = **20.9 map units** between the gene and the centromere.

On the exam, be ready to classify a drawn ascus, to explain *why* the alternating patterns mean second-division segregation, and to justify the division by two.

## Worked example 3: nondisjunction

A pair of homologues fails to separate in anaphase I: one meiosis I cell receives both, the other neither, and meiosis II divides each faithfully, so two gametes are n + 1 and two are n − 1. If instead sister chromatids fail to separate in anaphase II in one cell, that cell gives one n + 1 and one n − 1 gamete and the other cell gives two normal ones. Count what separates at each stage and the answer follows.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Homologous chromosomes are identical."** They carry the same genes, not necessarily the same alleles. Sister chromatids are the identical ones.
- **"Sister chromatids separate in meiosis I."** They separate in meiosis II. Meiosis I separates homologues.
- **"DNA replicates between the two divisions."** There is no S phase in interkinesis. One replication serves both divisions.
- **"Crossing over happens between sister chromatids."** Swapping identical segments changes nothing. Crossing over is between non-sister chromatids of a tetrad.
- **"Variation among gametes is produced in meiosis II."** Both crossing over and independent assortment have acted by the end of metaphase I. Meiosis II only distributes the results.
- **"2ⁿ uses the diploid number."** The exponent is the number of pairs, n. For 2n = 8, the answer is 2⁴ = 16, not 2⁸.
- **"Meiosis produces gametes in every organism."** In plants and fungi meiosis makes spores; gametes are made later by mitosis.
