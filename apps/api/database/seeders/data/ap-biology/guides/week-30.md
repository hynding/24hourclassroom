## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Density-dependent factor | A limit whose per-capita effect grows with density: competition, predation, parasitism, disease, waste. |
| Density-independent factor | A cause of mortality that takes the same proportion at any density: weather, fire, flood, eruption. |
| K-selected | Few, large, well-cared-for offspring; late maturity; long life; stable habitats near K. |
| r-selected | Many small offspring; no care; early maturity; short life; disturbed habitats. |
| Dispersion | Uniform (territories, allelopathy), random (wind-blown seeds), clumped (patchy resources, herds). |
| Mark-recapture | N = (marked first × caught second) ÷ (marked in second). |
| Community | All the populations of different species living and interacting in an area. |
| Niche | A species' use of resources and conditions; fundamental (possible) versus realised (actual, with competitors). |
| Competitive exclusion | Two species cannot share one niche indefinitely; one wins. |
| Resource partitioning | Dividing a resource by place, time or type so competitors coexist. |
| Aposematic coloration | Warning colours on a toxic species. |
| Batesian / Mullerian mimicry | A harmless copier of a dangerous model / several dangerous species sharing one pattern. |
| Mutualism, commensalism, parasitism | +/+, +/0, +/−. |
| Foundation species | Builds the habitat: kelp, corals, trees. |
| Keystone species | Effect on the community far out of proportion to its abundance; Pisaster, wolves. |
| Trophic cascade | A predator's effect passing down through consumers to producers. |
| Succession | Primary (no soil: lava, glacier) and secondary (soil remains: fire, farmland) community change. |

## What holds a population at K

The logistic curve bends because, as a population grows, each individual finds the environment harder. The factors responsible are **density-dependent**: their effect per individual rises with density. Competition for food, water, light, space and mates; predators that concentrate on abundant prey; parasites and diseases that spread faster through crowds; waste that accumulates. In the intestinal roundworm *Ascaris*, eggs per female fall steeply as worms per host rise and then level off above 30–50 worms: fecundity itself depends on density. The lynx and the snowshoe hare show predation as a density-dependent factor acting with a lag: hare numbers swing between about 10,000 and 150,000 on a ten-year cycle and lynx numbers follow one or two years behind, because it takes time to turn hares into lynx kittens and time for lynx to starve when hares are gone. Delayed negative feedback oscillates rather than settling, which is why real populations cycle around K instead of sitting on it.

**Density-independent** factors are different in kind. A cold snap, a flood, a wildfire or a volcanic eruption removes the same proportion of a population whether it is crowded or sparse, so it cannot regulate a population toward K; it resets N. What happens afterwards is density-dependent again: a dense population that lost 60% has more survivors, and with density reduced its per-capita growth is high, so it rebounds faster than a sparse population that lost the same share.

Species differ in how they live with these pressures. **K-selected** species (elephants, primates, oaks) live near carrying capacity in stable habitats, mature late, live long, and put a lot into each of a few offspring; an oak waits twenty years before its first acorns. **r-selected** species (dandelions, jellyfish, most insects) exploit disturbed or unpredictable habitats with many cheap offspring and no care. The dichotomy has been replaced by life-table models in research, but it remains the right way to read a trade-off question: what does the environment reward, rapid growth from low density or competitive ability at high density?

Populations also have **dispersion**: uniform when territories or allelopathy keep individuals apart, random when seeds land wherever the wind drops them, clumped (the commonest) when resources are patchy or animals are social. To count them, ecologists use quadrats for sessile organisms and **mark-recapture** for mobile ones.

## The community: who interacts with whom

A **community** is all the species in an area and their interactions, and each species occupies a **niche**: the set of resources and conditions it uses. Two species that use the same limiting resource in the same way cannot coexist indefinitely; the **competitive exclusion principle** was demonstrated with *Paramecium aurelia* and *P. caudatum*, which each thrive alone on bacteria but, when cultured together, *P. aurelia* drives *P. caudatum* to extinction within weeks. Coexisting competitors have **partitioned** the resource by place, time or type, so each occupies a **realised niche** narrower than its **fundamental niche**.

**Predation** and **herbivory** are +/− interactions that select for defences: mechanical (thorns, shells), chemical (foxglove toxins), camouflage (stick insects), behavioural (playing dead, grouping). Toxic species advertise with **aposematic coloration** that predators learn to avoid; harmless species cash in by **Batesian mimicry** (a robber fly that looks like a bumblebee, which works only while mimics are rarer than models), and several toxic species share one pattern in **Mullerian mimicry** (*Heliconius* butterflies), so every predator lesson protects them all.

**Symbioses** are long-term, close interactions classified by the sign of each partner's outcome. **Mutualism** (+/+): termites and the cellulose-digesting protozoa in their guts; lichens; *Rhizobium* and legumes; corals and their zooxanthellae. **Commensalism** (+/0): a bird nesting in a tree. **Parasitism** (+/−): a tapeworm that lives for years in an intestine, or *Plasmodium* using a mosquito to reach new hosts; selection favours parasites that keep their host alive long enough to spread.

Some species matter more than their numbers suggest. **Foundation species** build the habitat: kelp, reef corals, forest trees. **Keystone species** hold the structure by their effect: when the sea star *Pisaster ochraceus* is removed from a rocky shore, its main prey, mussels, spread until they cover the rock and competitively exclude most other species, and richness falls by about half. When wolves returned to Yellowstone in 1995, elk fell and changed where they grazed, willows and cottonwoods along streams regrew, beavers and songbirds returned and banks stabilised: a **trophic cascade** from the top predator down to the plants and the river.

Communities also change through time. **Primary succession** begins on new land with no soil, such as fresh lava on Hawaii, with lichens and mosses as **pioneer species** that weather rock and build soil over centuries. **Secondary succession** follows a disturbance that leaves soil, such as fire or abandoned farmland: annuals and grasses, then shrubs and pines, then an oak-hickory **climax community** after about 150 years.

## Worked example 1: a mark-recapture estimate

Sixty turtles are netted, tagged and released. Two weeks later 90 are netted and 15 carry tags.

*Logic:* tagged turtles are 15/90 = 1/6 of the second sample, so the 60 tagged turtles should be 1/6 of the lake's population.

*Formula:* N = (60 × 90) ÷ 15 = **360**.

*Assumptions to state on the exam:* tags are not lost; tagged animals mix back in; tagged and untagged animals are equally catchable; no births, deaths or migration in between. If tagged turtles learn to avoid the net, the second sample has too few tags and N is overestimated.

## Worked example 2: reading the Pisaster removal data

| Year | Species, control | Species, removal | Mussel cover, removal (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 15 | 15 | 30 |
| 1 | 15 | 12 | 55 |
| 3 | 15 | 8 | 85 |
| 5 | 15 | 8 | 90 |

*Claim:* *Pisaster* is a keystone species. *Evidence:* richness fell from 15 to 8 only in the removal plot; the control held 15 throughout, so the shore itself was stable. *Mechanism:* mussel cover rose from 30% to 90% as richness fell; freed from predation, the dominant competitor took the limiting resource, space. *Prediction the model makes:* remove the mussels instead and richness stays high. *Trap:* the plateau at 8 is a new equilibrium, not a recovery.

## Worked example 3: reading a lynx-hare graph

Two curves, hares peaking at 150,000 and lynx at a few thousand, with the lynx peak one to two years after each hare peak. *What kind of factor is predation here?* Density-dependent: lynx build up on abundant hares, so predation per hare rises with hare density. *Why the lag?* Breeding and starvation take time. *Why cycle instead of settling?* Delayed negative feedback overshoots. *Would hares cycle without lynx?* Probably, more weakly: they strip their winter browse when abundant, and plant recovery has its own delay. On the exam, say which curve leads and why, and name the feedback.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Predators are bad for the prey population."** Predation is density-dependent regulation; removing a predator often leads to overshoot, starvation and crash (the reindeer of week 29).
- **"A keystone species is the most abundant species."** The most abundant species is usually the dominant competitor; the keystone is what keeps it in check.
- **"Density-independent factors regulate populations."** They reset N without regard to density; only density-dependent factors can hold a population near K.
- **"Mimicry means two species look alike by chance."** Batesian mimics are selected to resemble a model that predators avoid; the resemblance is adaptive.
- **"Competitive exclusion means similar species never coexist."** They coexist by partitioning; exclusion is what happens when they do not.
- **"Commensalism is common and easy to spot."** It is hard to prove that one partner is truly unaffected; many cases are weak mutualisms or parasitisms.
