## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Fossil record | Remains in dated rock layers showing that lineages have changed form over time; incomplete because fossilisation is rare. |
| Radiometric dating | Using the fixed half-life of a radioactive isotope to date a rock layer and the fossils in it. |
| Biogeography | The geographic distribution of species, which records where their ancestors lived (Proteaceae across the former Gondwana). |
| Endemic | Found in one place and nowhere else; the product of isolation. |
| Homologous structures | Same construction and embryonic origin, inherited from a common ancestor, whatever the current function. |
| Analogous structures | Same function, different construction and origin; produced by convergent evolution. |
| Vestigial structures | Reduced remnants of parts that worked in an ancestor. |
| Common ancestry | Descent of a group from one ancestral lineage, inferred from features shared by all members and absent outside. |
| Horizontal gene transfer | Movement of genes between cells that are not parent and offspring (transformation, transduction, conjugation). |
| Antibiotic resistance | A heritable ability to survive an antibiotic, selected by the drug from pre-existing variation. |
| Molecular clock | Dating a divergence from the number of sequence differences, assuming a constant rate calibrated against fossils. |
| Percent identity | Matching positions ÷ positions compared × 100, for two aligned sequences. |
| Three domains | Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya, recognised by Woese from ribosomal RNA sequences. |

## Several independent witnesses

The strength of the case for evolution is that unrelated kinds of evidence, gathered by different methods, tell the same story.

**Fossils.** Rock layers can be dated by **radiometric decay** (carbon-14 for the last 50,000 years, potassium-40 for billions), and the fossils in successive layers show lineages changing form: horses, whales, humans. Transitional fossils (whale ancestors with hind limbs, feathered dinosaurs) are the intermediates that descent with modification predicts. The record is patchy because fossilisation needs hard parts and quick burial, so absence of a fossil is weak evidence.

**Biogeography.** Species are distributed by history, not just habitat. The plant family Proteaceae lives in Australia, southern Africa and South America because it spread across Gondwana before the supercontinent broke up about 200 million years ago and the continents carried it apart. Isolated islands and continents are full of **endemic** species that resemble the nearest mainland's, because a few founders arrived, gene flow stopped, and drift and selection did the rest.

**Anatomy.** **Homologous structures** share construction and development because they were inherited: the forelimbs of a human, dog, bird and whale have one upper bone, two forearm bones, wrist bones and digits, whatever they now do. **Analogous structures** share only a job: a butterfly's wing and a bird's wing both fly, but one has no bones and develops from different tissue; they are products of convergent evolution and say nothing about relatedness. **Vestigial structures**, such as the pelvic bones inside a whale or the wings of a flightless cormorant, are leftovers with no reason to exist except inheritance.

**Embryology.** All vertebrate embryos develop pharyngeal slits and a tail; fish keep the slits as gills, land vertebrates remodel them. Shared early stages reflect a shared developmental programme.

**Molecules.** DNA and the genetic code are universal, so any gene can be compared across all life. Mutations accumulate with time, so the more recently two species shared an ancestor, the fewer differences their sequences show, and the ranking of relatives from DNA matches the ranking from anatomy and fossils. **Gene duplication** supplies a mechanism for new functions: one copy keeps the old job while the other is free to change, and shared duplicates in related species are a signature of descent.

How to tell homology from analogy: ignore the function; look at the parts, the embryonic origin and the genes that build them.

## One ancestor for all life, one for all eukaryotes

The CED asks for two levels of common ancestry, each argued from features shared by every member of the group and absent outside it.

*All life* shares DNA as the hereditary molecule, the same genetic code, ribosomes that translate it, ATP as the energy currency and core metabolism such as glycolysis. None of these had to be the way it is; chemists have built other base pairs and the code could map codons to amino acids in countless ways. When something arbitrary is the same everywhere, it was inherited once. Carl Woese used the slowest-changing universal gene, ribosomal RNA, to build the tree of all life and found three domains: **Bacteria**, **Archaea** and **Eukarya**.

*All eukaryotes* (protists, fungi, plants, animals) share membrane-bound organelles including mitochondria, a nucleus with linear chromosomes wrapped on histones, and genes interrupted by introns. Prokaryotes have none of these, so the suite marks a single eukaryotic ancestor. (Where the mitochondria came from is week 27.)

Two cautions for free-response answers. Shared features tell you about the *joint* ancestor of the whole group, not which two members are closest; ranking relatives needs features shared by some and not others (week 26). And modern species are branch tips, not ancestors: humans and chimpanzees are sister lineages that split about seven million years ago; neither descends from the other.

## Evolution is happening now

**Antibiotic resistance.** Random mutation, or a plasmid acquired from another cell, gives a few bacteria an allele that inactivates a drug or pumps it out. The antibiotic kills the sensitive majority; the resistant few reproduce; within days most of the population is resistant. The drug *selects*; it does not *create*. The Lederbergs proved it in 1952 by replica plating: the resistant colonies on penicillin plates mapped back to the same spots on a master plate that had never touched the drug. Bacteria evolve resistance faster than any other organism because their populations are enormous, their generations are minutes long, and **horizontal gene transfer** (transformation, transduction, conjugation) moves resistance genes between cells and even between species. MRSA appeared within a few years of methicillin's introduction. The lessons for practice follow from the mechanism: full courses at full dose, fewer unnecessary prescriptions, and rotation of drugs so that no single resistance allele is favoured continuously.

**Pesticides and herbicides** follow the same logic, and farmers who alternate chemicals with different targets keep both working longer. **Pathogens** such as influenza change their surface proteins every season, which is why vaccines are reformulated, and HIV evolves drug resistance inside a single patient. And the Grants' **finches** show measured, heritable, generation-to-generation change in a wild vertebrate population. Evolution is not only a theory about the past; it is a process you can watch.

**Molecular clocks.** Because mutations accumulate at a roughly steady rate, especially neutral ones that selection ignores, the number of differences between two sequences is proportional to the time since they diverged. Calibrate the rate with a divergence that fossils have dated, then apply it to pairs with no fossils. Different genes tick at different rates (rRNA slowly, viral genes very fast), rates wander between lineages, and the result is an estimate with error bars, not a date.

## Worked example 1: a cytochrome c table and a clock

| Species | Amino-acid differences from human (of 104) | Fossil-dated divergence (million years) |
|---|---|---|
| Chimpanzee | 0 | 7 |
| Rhesus monkey | 1 | 25 |
| Dog | 11 | 95 |
| Chicken | 13 | 310 |
| Tuna | 21 | 450 |
| Fruit fly | 29 | — |

*Relatedness.* Differences rise with divergence time, so the ranking of relatives is chimp, monkey, dog, chicken, tuna, fly. This is the molecular prediction of descent with modification, checked against an independent (fossil) timescale.

*Why this protein.* It is in every aerobic eukaryote doing the same essential job, so it can be aligned across all of them, and strong selection on its function keeps it changing slowly enough that a billion years leaves a readable signal.

*Clock.* Rate from the chicken pair = 13 / 310 = 0.042 differences per million years, about one every 24 million years. Fruit fly: 29 / 0.042 ≈ 690 million years. Check with tuna: 21 / 0.042 ≈ 500 against a fossil estimate of 450. The clock is right to within 10–20%, which is as good as a single calibration gets. The dog pair would give a faster rate (11 / 95 = 0.12); real clocks use several calibration points and report a range.

## Worked example 2: percent identity

A 300-base region differs at 12 positions between two species: identity = (300 − 12) / 300 × 100 = 96%. Between a third species and the first, the same region differs at 60 positions: 80%. The second species is the closer relative of the first. Always compare the same gene over the same length; identity between different genes or different regions means nothing.

## Worked example 3: arguing from a resistance experiment

A culture grown from one cell for 30 generations in drug-free broth is plated on plain agar (1,000 colonies) and on ampicillin agar (2 colonies). The resistant colony is grown 100 generations without drug and replated: 950 of 1,000 colonies are resistant.

*The two colonies.* Thirty generations from one cell is about a billion cells; a mutation with a frequency of one in a hundred million will be present in a handful of them. The ampicillin plate did not make them; it found them.

*The 950.* Resistance persisted for a hundred generations with no drug present, so it is heritable, carried in DNA. The 5% loss suggests a small fitness cost, which is why resistance declines (slowly) when a drug is withdrawn and why rotation works.

*The argument.* Claim: resistance evolved by natural selection on pre-existing variation. Evidence: resistant cells existed before exposure; the drug removed the sensitive ones; the trait was inherited. Reasoning: those are the three conditions from week 23.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Similar structures always mean common ancestry."** Analogous structures do not. Check construction and development, not function.
- **"Humans evolved from chimpanzees."** Sister lineages with a common ancestor. Living species are not ancestors of other living species.
- **"The fossil record has gaps, so evolution is doubtful."** Gaps are expected because fossilisation is rare; the fossils that exist appear in the predicted order and at the predicted ages.
- **"Antibiotics cause bacteria to become resistant."** They select among variants that already exist. Replica plating settled this.
- **"A molecular clock gives an exact date."** It gives an estimate whose accuracy depends on calibration and on the gene; rates vary.
- **"Evolution explains the origin of life."** It explains change once replicators with heritable variation exist. The origin of those replicators is week 27.
- **"Features shared by all mammals show which two mammals are closest."** Features shared by everyone in a group tell you about the group's ancestor; ranking relatives needs features shared by a subset.
