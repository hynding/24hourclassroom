## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Homeostasis | Keeping internal conditions within a working range by constant adjustment: a dynamic equilibrium. |
| Set point | The target value a control centre compares the variable against. Can be reset, as in fever. |
| Receptor, control centre, effector | The sensor that detects change, the comparator (often the hypothalamus or a gland), and the muscle or gland that produces the response. |
| Negative feedback | The response opposes the stimulus and returns the variable toward the set point. Stabilising; the main mechanism of homeostasis. |
| Positive feedback | The response reinforces the stimulus and drives a one-way event to completion. Ends when the end state removes the trigger. |
| Cell cycle | The ordered sequence of growth, DNA replication and division that produces two daughter cells. |
| Interphase | G₁ (growth), S (DNA synthesis), G₂ (preparation). About 90% of the cycle. |
| Sister chromatids | The two identical copies of a chromosome made in S phase, joined by cohesin at the centromere. |
| Kinetochore | Protein structure on each chromatid's centromere that captures spindle microtubules. |
| Mitotic spindle | Microtubules organised from the two centrosomes; kinetochore microtubules move chromosomes, polar microtubules push the poles apart. |
| Cytokinesis | Division of the cytoplasm: a cleavage furrow of actin in animals, a cell plate from Golgi vesicles in plants. |

## Feedback: the response acts on the signal (4.4)

Week 12 followed a signal from receptor to response. This week adds one arrow: the response changes the stimulus that started it. If the response **reduces** the stimulus, the loop is **negative** and the variable settles around a set point. If the response **increases** the stimulus, the loop is **positive** and the variable runs in one direction until something ends it.

**Blood glucose** is the standard negative loop, and it runs in both directions. After a meal, sensor cells in the pancreas detect high glucose and release insulin; liver, muscle and fat cells take glucose up (through transporters inserted into their membranes) and the liver stores it as glycogen; glucose falls, so insulin release falls. Between meals the same gland releases glucagon; the liver breaks glycogen down and releases glucose; glucose rises, so glucagon falls. Both hormones are negative feedback because in both cases the response removes the condition that triggered it. **Calcium** works the same way: low blood calcium triggers parathyroid hormone, which raises calcium by absorption from gut and kidney and release from bone, and the rise shuts the hormone off.

**Body temperature** is a pair of negative loops around one set point held in the hypothalamus. Too warm: vasodilation sends blood to the skin to radiate heat, and sweating removes about 586 calories per gram of water that evaporates. Too cold: vasoconstriction keeps blood in the core, shivering makes heat through muscle contraction, and arrector pili muscles raise fur or hair (goose bumps) to trap insulating air. Endotherms run these loops with internal heat; ectotherms adjust mostly by behaviour, basking or seeking shade. During infection, pyrogens raise the hypothalamic set point; the body then shivers at a normal temperature because 37 °C is now a deficit, and sweats when the set point returns. Fever is the loop working around a new target, not the loop failing.

**Positive feedback** produces events, not stability. In labour, the baby's head stretches the cervix, stretch receptors trigger oxytocin release, oxytocin makes the uterus contract, contraction stretches the cervix further, and more oxytocin follows until birth removes the stretch. In clotting, thrombin converts fibrinogen to fibrin and also activates more of the factors that produce thrombin, so a clot forms fast at the wound and the loop stops when the wound is sealed. In fruit, ethylene gas triggers ripening and ripening fruit makes more ethylene, in the same fruit and its neighbours, which is why one ripe apple in a bag ripens the rest.

## The cell cycle: copy, then distribute (4.5)

A eukaryotic genome is several linear DNA molecules, each a chromosome. Human body cells carry 46, two sets of 23 (**diploid**, 2n); gametes carry one set (**haploid**, n). Stretched out, one cell's DNA is about two metres long, 2 nm wide, inside a nucleus 10 μm across. It fits because DNA wraps around cores of eight histones to form **nucleosomes** (about sevenfold shortening), nucleosomes coil into a 30 nm fibre (about fiftyfold), and the fibre folds further. Histones bind DNA because their lysine- and arginine-rich R groups are positively charged and the phosphate backbone is negative. Before division the folding goes to its extreme, producing the compact, countable chromosomes you see in a squash.

**Interphase** is most of the cycle and most of the work. In **G₁** the cell grows and stockpiles nucleotides, histones and energy. In **S** every chromosome is replicated; the two copies, **sister chromatids**, stay joined by **cohesin**, most tightly at the **centromere**, and the centrosome is also duplicated. In **G₂** the cell replenishes energy, makes the proteins that will move chromosomes, and begins dismantling the interphase cytoskeleton to supply tubulin for the spindle. In rapidly dividing cultured human cells the cycle takes about 24 hours: G₁ 9 h, S 10 h, G₂ 4.5 h, M 0.5 h. Cells that stop cycling enter **G₀**; some return when conditions improve, but mature nerve and cardiac muscle cells stay there for life.

**Mitosis** (karyokinesis) distributes the copies.

1. **Prophase.** Chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes of two chromatids each. The nucleolus disappears, the nuclear envelope begins to break into vesicles, membranous organelles fragment, and the two centrosomes move apart as microtubules grow between them.
2. **Prometaphase.** The envelope finishes fragmenting. A **kinetochore** assembles on each chromatid's centromere and captures microtubules from a pole; the two kinetochores of a chromosome face opposite poles. Polar microtubules overlap at the middle and push the poles apart.
3. **Metaphase.** Every chromosome sits on the **metaphase plate**, each chromatid attached to a different pole, cohesin still holding them. Chromosomes are at maximum condensation.
4. **Anaphase.** Cohesin is degraded. Sister chromatids separate at the centromere and each, now called a chromosome, is pulled toward its pole; polar microtubules slide past each other and the cell elongates.
5. **Telophase.** Chromosomes reach the poles and decondense, the spindle depolymerises, nuclear envelopes re-form around each set and nucleoli reappear.

**Cytokinesis** then splits the cytoplasm. Animal cells form a contractile ring of actin just inside the membrane at the old metaphase plate; it tightens into a cleavage furrow that pinches the cell in two. Plant cells cannot pinch a cellulose wall, so Golgi vesicles carrying wall materials gather at the plate, fuse into a **cell plate**, and the plate grows outward until it joins the old wall.

Why the daughters are identical: S phase makes an exact copy of every chromosome, cohesin keeps the copies together until the spindle has attached each to opposite poles, and anaphase sends one copy of every chromosome to each pole. A diploid parent gives two diploid daughters with the same genome. That is how organisms grow, repair and, in many cases, reproduce asexually.

## Worked example 1: reading a glucose-tolerance table

| Time (min) | Healthy glucose (mg/dL) | Healthy insulin (μU/mL) | Patient glucose | Patient insulin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 85 | 6 | 160 | 1 |
| 30 | 140 | 60 | 240 | 1 |
| 120 | 90 | 15 | 320 | 1 |
| 180 | 82 | 7 | 310 | 1 |

*Question:* Where is the patient's loop broken?

*Reasoning:* In the healthy subject the stimulus (glucose 85 → 140) produces the signal (insulin 6 → 60), the response lowers glucose, and the falling glucose lowers insulin: every arrow of a negative loop is visible. In the patient the stimulus is present and growing but the signal never appears, so the break is at the sensor-gland, not at the effectors. Prediction: injecting insulin should restore the correction, because the effector cells still have receptors. Locate a break by asking which component's output is missing.

## Worked example 2: converting a count to time

A root-tip field of 600 cells contains 540 in interphase, 30 in prophase, 12 in metaphase, 8 in anaphase and 10 in telophase; the cycle takes 24 h.

Cells are unsynchronised, so a snapshot samples time: fraction of cells in a stage = fraction of the cycle spent there. Mitosis: 60/600 = 0.10, so 0.10 × 24 = **2.4 h**. Anaphase: 8/600 × 24 = 0.32 h, about 19 minutes. Interphase: 21.6 h. The fraction in mitosis, 10%, is the **mitotic index**; week 14 compares indices between treated and control tissue with a chi-square test. The reasoning fails if the cells are synchronised or if one stage is easier to spot than another.

## Worked example 3: chromosome and chromatid bookkeeping

| Stage | Chromosomes | Chromatids | DNA content |
|---|---|---|---|
| G₁ | 46 | 46 | 2c |
| G₂ | 46 | 92 | 4c |
| Metaphase | 46 | 92 | 4c |
| Each daughter after anaphase | 46 | 46 | 2c |

Count centromeres to count chromosomes. S phase doubles chromatids and DNA but not chromosomes; anaphase doubles chromosomes (each chromatid becomes one) without changing DNA; cytokinesis halves everything per cell. A drawing that shows metaphase chromosomes as single chromatids, or daughters with 23 chromosomes, has confused mitosis with meiosis.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Negative feedback is harmful and positive feedback is helpful."** The words describe direction. Negative loops keep you alive every second; positive loops are rare and run brief, one-way events.
- **"Glucagon is positive feedback because it raises glucose."** It raises a variable that was too low, which removes the stimulus. Opposing the change is what makes it negative.
- **"Fever means homeostasis has failed."** The set point was moved; the loop is working around the new target.
- **"Interphase is the resting stage."** It is growth, replication and preparation, and it takes nine-tenths of the cycle.
- **"Mitosis halves the chromosome number."** That is meiosis. Mitosis keeps it.
- **"Cytokinesis is part of mitosis."** Mitosis is nuclear division; cytokinesis is cytoplasmic division and follows it.
