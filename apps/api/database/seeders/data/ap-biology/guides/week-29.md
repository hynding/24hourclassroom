## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Biogeochemical cycle | The movement of an element between organisms and the abiotic reservoirs (air, water, soil, rock). |
| Reservoir and flux | Where an element is stored, and the rate at which it moves from one store to another. |
| Residence time | The average time a molecule spends in a reservoir: 1.5 weeks for water in the air, thousands of years in the ocean. |
| Transpiration | Water vapour released by plants; a major flux from land to atmosphere. |
| Nitrogen fixation | N₂ to ammonium by prokaryotes (Rhizobium, Azotobacter, cyanobacteria). |
| Ammonification | Nitrogen in dead matter and waste converted to ammonium by bacteria and fungi. |
| Nitrification | Ammonium to nitrite to nitrate by Nitrosomonas and relatives. |
| Denitrification | Nitrate to N₂ gas by Pseudomonas and Clostridium; closes the cycle. |
| Eutrophication | Nutrient enrichment of water, algal bloom, decomposition, oxygen depletion, dead zone. |
| Population size (N) and density | How many individuals; how many per unit area or volume. |
| dN/dt | The rate of change of population size: births minus deaths. |
| rmax | The maximum per-capita growth rate under ideal conditions (intrinsic rate of increase, biotic potential). |
| Exponential growth | dN/dt = rmax N; J-shaped; unlimited resources. |
| Carrying capacity (K) | The largest population an environment can sustain. |
| Logistic growth | dN/dt = rmax N (K − N)/K; S-shaped; growth slows as N approaches K. |
| Intraspecific competition | Competition within a species; the biological reason growth slows near K. |
| Life table and survivorship curve | A cohort's deaths by age; Type I (die old), Type II (constant), Type III (die young). |

## Matter cycles

Energy leaves an ecosystem as heat and must be replaced from outside. Atoms do not leave: the same carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus are used again and again, and the pattern of their reuse is a **biogeochemical cycle**. For each cycle, think reservoirs and fluxes.

**Water.** Sunlight drives **evaporation** from oceans and lakes and **sublimation** from ice; plants add vapour by **transpiration**. Vapour **condenses** and falls as **precipitation**, then **infiltrates** to groundwater, **runs off** into streams, or evaporates again. Only 2.5% of Earth's water is fresh, and of that 69% is frozen and 31% is underground; the lakes and rivers organisms drink from hold 0.3% of the fresh water. **Residence times** range from about a week in organisms and the atmosphere to 4,000 years in the ocean and 10,000 in glaciers and deep groundwater.

**Carbon** has a fast loop and a slow one. The fast loop is biology: **photosynthesis** fixes atmospheric CO₂ into organic carbon, and **cellular respiration** by producers, consumers and decomposers returns it. The slow loop is geology: CO₂ dissolves in seawater as carbonic acid and then bicarbonate (over 90% of ocean carbon), organisms build calcium carbonate shells, shells become sediment and then **limestone**, the largest carbon reservoir on Earth. Buried organic matter becomes **fossil fuel** over millions of years. Burning it, together with deforestation, moves carbon from the slow reservoir into the air far faster than any process returns it, which is why atmospheric CO₂ has climbed from about 280 ppm before 1750 to over 400 ppm.

**Nitrogen** is 78% of the air, but as N₂, whose triple bond nothing eukaryotic can break. **Nitrogen fixation** by prokaryotes, free-living (Azotobacter, cyanobacteria) or symbiotic in legume root nodules (Rhizobium), converts N₂ to ammonium. **Ammonification** by decomposers turns the nitrogen in dead matter and waste back into ammonium; **nitrification** oxidises ammonium to nitrite and nitrate, which plants absorb; **denitrification** reduces nitrate to N₂ and returns it to the air. Humans now fix as much nitrogen industrially as nature does, and the runoff of that fertiliser, with sewage, drives **eutrophication**: algae bloom, die, and are decomposed by bacteria that strip the water of oxygen. The Gulf of Mexico dead zone fed by the Mississippi exceeds 8,000 square miles. Fossil-fuel burning adds nitrogen oxides that fall as acid rain and nitrous oxide, a greenhouse gas.

**Phosphorus** is the odd one out: no atmospheric phase. Phosphate enters ecosystems by the **weathering of rock**, moves from soil to plants to consumers to decomposers, washes to the sea, settles in sediment, and comes back to land only by **geological uplift** over tens of thousands of years. Because its supply is small and slow, phosphorus is usually the **limiting nutrient** in fresh water, which is why phosphate in detergent and fertiliser has such an outsized effect on lakes.

## Populations grow by births minus deaths

A **population** is all the individuals of one species in an area that can interbreed, described by **size** (N) and **density**. Its change over time is **dN/dt = B − D**, births minus deaths (add immigration and subtract emigration for an open population). Divide births and deaths by N and you have per-capita rates; their difference is **r**, the per-capita growth rate, and its ceiling under ideal conditions is **rmax**, the species' intrinsic rate of increase.

**Exponential growth** is what happens when nothing limits r: **dN/dt = rmax N**. Because growth is proportional to N, the curve is J-shaped; 1,000 bacteria dividing every hour become 1,000 × 2²⁴, more than 16 billion, in a day. Real populations do this briefly: bacteria in fresh medium, a species newly arrived on an island, humans since 1800.

**Logistic growth** adds the environment. Resources are finite, so there is a **carrying capacity K**, and the growth rate is scaled by the fraction of K still unused: **dN/dt = rmax N (K − N)/K**. When N is small the factor is nearly 1 and growth is almost exponential; as N approaches K the factor falls to 0 and growth stops; if N overshoots, the factor is negative and the population declines. The biological content of the factor is **intraspecific competition**: more individuals per unit of food means lower birth rates and higher death rates. The curve is S-shaped, and its steepest point, where dN/dt is largest, is at **N = K/2**. Yeast in a flask trace the S almost perfectly; harbor seals in the wild oscillate above and below K because the real K shifts with seasons and other species.

**Life tables and survivorship curves** describe deaths by age. A **Type I** curve (humans, Dall sheep) is flat until old age; **Type II** (many birds) falls at a constant rate; **Type III** (trees, most fish, marine invertebrates) drops steeply in the first interval because most offspring die young. Mortality rate for an interval is deaths in the interval divided by survivors at its start, usually per 1,000.

## Worked example 1: exponential and logistic calculations

A population of 400 water fleas has rmax = 0.35 per day.

*With unlimited food:* dN/dt = 0.35 × 400 = **140 per day**.

*In a pond with K = 1,000:* dN/dt = 0.35 × 400 × (1,000 − 400)/1,000 = 0.35 × 400 × 0.6 = **84 per day**. The (K − N)/K factor of 0.6 says that 60% of the carrying capacity is still unused.

*If N were 1,200:* (1,000 − 1,200)/1,000 = −0.2, so dN/dt = 0.35 × 1,200 × (−0.2) = **−84 per day**: the population is above K and shrinking. Always check the sign and the units (individuals per unit time, not a new N).

## Worked example 2: reading a growth table

| Time (h) | Yeast (thousands/mL) | Increment |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 10 | — |
| 2 | 20 | 10 |
| 4 | 40 | 20 |
| 6 | 80 | 40 |
| 8 | 150 | 70 |
| 10 | 250 | 100 |
| 12 | 340 | 90 |
| 14 | 390 | 50 |
| 16 | 400 | 10 |
| 18 | 400 | 0 |

*What model fits?* Doubling through hour 6, then shrinking increments, then a plateau at 400: logistic, with K ≈ 400. *Where is growth fastest?* The largest increment, hours 8–10 (50 thousand/mL per hour), when N ≈ 200 ≈ K/2. *Why did it stop?* A closed flask: sugar consumed, ethanol accumulating, births falling to meet deaths. The question to ask of any table is "what are the increments doing?"

## Worked example 3: an Investigation 10 energy budget

Caterpillars eat 2.00 g (dry) of Brassica leaf, gain 0.20 g, and produce 0.80 g of frass. Stored: 0.20 ÷ 2.00 = **10%**. Egested: 0.80 g, **40%**. The remaining 1.00 g, **50%**, was assimilated and respired, leaving as CO₂, water vapour and heat, which is why it cannot be weighed. To express this as energy, multiply each dry mass by its energy content in kcal/g (from calorimetry or reference values). The 10% here is a measurement, not a law: a different animal or diet would give a different number.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Plants get nitrogen from the air."** They take it from soil as nitrate or ammonium; only bacteria use N₂.
- **"Denitrification helps plants."** It removes usable nitrogen from the soil and returns N₂ to the air.
- **"Phosphorus cycles through the atmosphere like carbon."** It has no gaseous phase; rock is its only new source.
- **"Respiration is as bad for CO₂ as burning coal."** Respiration returns recently fixed carbon; burning coal moves carbon out of a reservoir that had been locked up for millions of years.
- **"The per-capita rate stays at rmax until the population hits K."** In the logistic model it falls continuously; rmax is reached only when N is near zero.
- **"Growth is fastest at the start."** The per-capita rate is highest at the start; the total growth rate dN/dt peaks at K/2.
- **"K is a fixed property of a species."** It belongs to an environment and a species together, and it changes with weather, competitors and the damage a population does to its own food supply.
