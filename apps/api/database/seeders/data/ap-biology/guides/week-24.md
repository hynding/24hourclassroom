## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Gene pool | All the alleles of all genes in a population. |
| Allele frequency | The fraction of all copies of a gene that are a particular allele; computed by counting alleles, not individuals. |
| Microevolution | A change in allele frequencies within a population across generations. |
| Genetic drift | Random change in allele frequency due to chance sampling of which individuals reproduce; strongest in small populations. |
| Bottleneck effect | Drift caused by a sudden random collapse in numbers; variation is lost and stays lost. |
| Founder effect | Drift caused by a small, unrepresentative group starting a new population. |
| Fixation | An allele reaches frequency 1.0; the alternative is gone until mutation or migration returns it. |
| Gene flow | Movement of alleles between populations by migrating individuals or gametes; makes populations more alike. |
| Nonrandom mating | Assortative mating or inbreeding; raises homozygosity without changing allele frequencies. |
| Inbreeding depression | Lower fitness because harmful recessive alleles are exposed in homozygotes. |
| Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium | The state in which allele and genotype frequencies do not change; requires five conditions. |
| Null hypothesis | The claim that observed counts match the no-evolution expectation; a chi-square test can reject it. |
| Chi-square (χ²) | Σ (observed − expected)² / expected; compared with a critical value at the right degrees of freedom. |
| Heterozygote advantage | Heterozygotes are fitter than either homozygote, so both alleles are kept. |
| Cline | A gradual geographic gradient in a trait or allele frequency. |

## Evolution as a change in allele frequency

The **modern synthesis** of the 1940s joined Darwin's mechanism to Mendel's genetics and gave evolution a measurable definition: a change in the frequencies of alleles in a population's **gene pool** across generations. To compute an allele frequency, count alleles. In 500 pea plants with 245 YY, 210 Yy and 45 yy there are 1,000 alleles; Y = (2 × 245 + 210) / 1,000 = 0.7 and y = (210 + 2 × 45) / 1,000 = 0.3. Heterozygotes count once, homozygotes twice.

Five things can change the numbers.

1. **Natural selection** (week 23): genotypes differ in reproductive success, so the favoured allele rises. The only force that produces adaptation.
2. **Genetic drift**: chance decides which individuals happen to reproduce, so frequencies wander. In a population of ten rabbits with p = q = 0.5, if by chance only five breed (2 BB, 3 Bb), the next generation has p = 0.7; if by chance only the two BB rabbits breed after that, b is gone. No rabbit was fitter; the sample was small. Drift is **sampling error**, and the smaller the sample the larger the error: losing one individual from ten removes a tenth of the gene pool, from a hundred only a hundredth. Drift ends in **fixation**, and a lost allele cannot be regenerated by mating. Two special cases: a **bottleneck**, when a disaster leaves a few random survivors (northern elephant seals, hunted to about twenty, still show almost no variation after recovering to over 100,000), and the **founder effect**, when a few colonists start a new population (several Dutch founders of the Afrikaner population carried the alleles for Huntington's disease and Fanconi anaemia, which remain unusually common there).
3. **Gene flow**: alleles arrive by migration of individuals or gametes (pollen, seeds, dispersing male lions). It adds variation to the receiving population and makes populations more alike; cutting it off is what allows them to diverge.
4. **Mutation**: the only source of new alleles, but so rare (about one per gene per hundred thousand gametes) that on its own it moves frequencies very slowly. Its job is to supply the variation the other forces act on.
5. **Nonrandom mating**: **assortative mating** (choosing similar mates) and **inbreeding** (mating with relatives). These raise the share of homozygotes and lower heterozygotes but leave p and q unchanged; they alter **genotype** frequencies, not allele frequencies. Inbreeding exposes harmful recessive alleles, which is **inbreeding depression**, and why breeding programs import unrelated animals.

Which forces add variation? Mutation and gene flow. Which remove it? Drift (including bottlenecks and founders), and stabilizing or directional selection. Which keep it? Disruptive, frequency-dependent and heterozygote-advantage selection. Geographic variation can be gradual, a **cline** (larger bodies toward the poles, later flowering with altitude), when gene flow smooths the gradient, or abrupt when gene flow is blocked.

## Hardy-Weinberg: the population that is not evolving

Hardy and Weinberg asked what sexual reproduction *by itself* does to allele frequencies. The answer is nothing. If a gamete carries allele A with probability p and allele a with probability q, random union of gametes gives genotypes in the proportions

**p² (AA) + 2pq (Aa) + q² (aa) = 1**, with **p + q = 1**.

It is a Punnett square for the whole gene pool, and the cross term is doubled because Aa and aA are both heterozygotes. Those proportions reproduce themselves generation after generation provided five conditions hold: a **very large population** (no drift), **no mutation**, **no gene flow**, **no selection**, and **random mating**. Each condition is the absence of one evolutionary force, so a population that meets all five is, by definition, not evolving.

No real population meets all five, and that is the point. Hardy-Weinberg is a **null hypothesis**: it tells you what the genotype counts would be if nothing were happening, so that when the counts differ you know something is. Two cautions. First, matching the prediction means you *cannot reject* the null; it does not prove the population never evolves, because a single generation of random mating restores p², 2pq, q² even after allele frequencies have shifted (the mosquito data in this week's quiz show exactly that). Second, a significant difference tells you *that* a force is acting, not *which*; a heterozygote deficit with unchanged p and q points to nonrandom mating, a change in p over years points to selection, drift or gene flow.

## Worked example 1: allele frequencies and expected counts

1,000 deer mice are genotyped: 400 BB, 400 Bb, 200 bb.

*Allele frequencies.* Copies of B = 2 × 400 + 400 = 1,200 out of 2,000 alleles, so p = 0.60 and q = 0.40.

*Expected counts under equilibrium.* BB = p² × 1,000 = 0.36 × 1,000 = 360. Bb = 2pq × 1,000 = 2 × 0.6 × 0.4 × 1,000 = 480. bb = q² × 1,000 = 0.16 × 1,000 = 160.

*Compare.* Observed has 80 fewer heterozygotes and 40 more of each homozygote. Allele frequencies are by construction the same in both columns, so whatever is acting changes genotype proportions only. That is nonrandom mating's fingerprint.

## Worked example 2: carriers from a recessive phenotype

Cystic fibrosis affects about 1 in 2,500 newborns of European ancestry. Under equilibrium the affected class is q², so q² = 0.0004 and q = 0.02; then p = 0.98 and carriers = 2pq = 2 × 0.98 × 0.02 = 0.039, about **1 in 25**. A hundred times as many people carry the allele as show the condition. This is why selection against a rare recessive is so slow: almost every copy sits in a carrier where selection cannot see it. The method depends on equilibrium; you can only take the square root of the recessive class if the population mates at random with respect to the gene.

## Worked example 3: a full chi-square test

Continue example 1. The AP formula sheet gives **χ² = Σ (o − e)² / e**.

| Genotype | Observed (o) | Expected (e) | (o − e)² / e |
|---|---|---|---|
| BB | 400 | 360 | 1,600 / 360 = 4.44 |
| Bb | 400 | 480 | 6,400 / 480 = 13.33 |
| bb | 200 | 160 | 1,600 / 160 = 10.00 |
| Sum | | | **27.8** |

Degrees of freedom = categories − 1 = 2. The AP table gives a critical value of 5.99 at p = 0.05 (3.84 for df = 1, 7.82 for df = 3). 27.8 is far above 5.99, so the probability that chance alone produced a gap this large is below 5%: **reject the null**. The population is not in Hardy-Weinberg proportions at this locus. The next sentence of a good answer names a candidate cause (inbreeding or assortative mating by coat colour) and says what further data would test it.

If instead the observed counts had been 365, 475, 160, χ² = 0.07 + 0.05 + 0 = 0.12, below 5.99: fail to reject. Not "the population is in equilibrium", but "we have no evidence against it".

## Common misconceptions

- **"Allele frequency = the fraction of individuals showing the trait."** Count alleles; homozygotes carry two.
- **"If a population fits Hardy-Weinberg it is not evolving and never has."** Random mating rebuilds the proportions every generation; a snapshot cannot see history or other loci.
- **"Drift only happens in tiny populations."** It happens in every finite population; it is *weaker* in large ones. Population size sets the strength.
- **"Inbreeding changes allele frequencies."** It changes genotype frequencies. Only selection on the exposed homozygotes changes p and q.
- **"A recessive allele is rare because it is recessive."** Dominance has nothing to do with frequency. A dominant allele can be rare (Huntington's) and a recessive one common.
- **"The rarer a recessive allele, the easier to eliminate."** The opposite: as q falls, the share of copies in carriers (2pq versus q²) rises and selection slows to a crawl.
- **"A large chi-square proves selection."** It proves a departure from expectation. Naming the force is a separate inference.
