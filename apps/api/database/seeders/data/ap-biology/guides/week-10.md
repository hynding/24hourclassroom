## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Calvin cycle | The stroma pathway that uses ATP and NADPH to reduce CO₂ to three-carbon sugar. |
| Carbon fixation | Attaching inorganic CO₂ to an organic molecule, here RuBP. |
| RuBisCO | The enzyme of fixation; abundant, slow, and able to bind O₂ by mistake. |
| RuBP | Ribulose bisphosphate, the five-carbon CO₂ acceptor. |
| 3-PGA | 3-phosphoglycerate, the three-carbon first stable product of fixation. |
| G3P | Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate, the three-carbon sugar the cycle exports; also a glycolysis intermediate. |
| Photorespiration | RuBisCO's side reaction with O₂, which wastes carbon and energy. |
| Glycolysis | The ten-step splitting of glucose into two pyruvate in the cytosol. |
| Energy-investment / payoff phase | The first five steps, which spend 2 ATP / the last five, which make 4 ATP and 2 NADH. |
| Phosphofructokinase (PFK) | The allosterically regulated enzyme of the first committed step of glycolysis. |
| Substrate-level phosphorylation | Direct transfer of a phosphate from an intermediate to ADP. |
| Pyruvate oxidation | Conversion of pyruvate to acetyl CoA with release of CO₂ and formation of NADH. |
| Acetyl CoA | The two-carbon acetyl group carried by coenzyme A; entry point to the citric acid cycle. |
| Citric acid cycle | The eight-step matrix cycle that oxidises acetyl groups to CO₂, loading NADH and FADH₂. |
| Oxaloacetate | The four-carbon acceptor that starts and ends the cycle. |
| Beta-oxidation | Chopping fatty acids into acetyl CoA units in the matrix. |
| Deamination | Removing an amino acid's amino group so the carbon skeleton can be respired. |

## The Calvin cycle: fix, reduce, regenerate

The Calvin cycle spends the ATP and NADPH the light reactions left in the stroma. It has three stages, and the exam tests the carbon arithmetic.

**Fixation.** RuBisCO joins one CO₂ to the five-carbon sugar **RuBP**. The six-carbon product is so unstable it splits at once into two molecules of three-carbon **3-PGA**. That is the only step where inorganic carbon becomes organic.

**Reduction.** Each 3-PGA is phosphorylated by ATP and then reduced by NADPH to **G3P**, a true sugar. This is where the energy and the reducing power from the light reactions are deposited into carbon.

**Regeneration.** For every three CO₂ fixed, six G3P are made, but only one leaves the cycle. The other five (fifteen carbons) are rearranged, at the cost of three more ATP, into three RuBP (fifteen carbons) so that the next three CO₂ have something to attach to. The books balance: three carbons in as CO₂, three out as one G3P.

The budget per G3P is therefore 9 ATP and 6 NADPH; per glucose, two G3P joined outside the cycle, 18 ATP and 12 NADPH. G3P is also the branch point for sucrose, starch, cellulose, fatty acids and amino acids.

RuBisCO has a flaw: its active site also accepts O₂, and the oxygenase reaction, **photorespiration**, consumes RuBP and releases CO₂ without making sugar. O₂ and CO₂ compete for the same site, week 8's competitive inhibition in the wild. On a hot day with stomata shut, CO₂ inside the leaf falls, O₂ rises, and photorespiration climbs; the C4 and CAM strategies concentrate CO₂ around RuBisCO in response.

"Light-independent" is misleading: the reactions use no photons, but they consume ATP and NADPH that last only seconds after the light goes off. In the dark, 3-PGA piles up and RuBP drains; remove CO₂ in the light and the opposite happens (worked example 3).

## Respiration: the overview and the plan

C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + energy. Respiration is the controlled oxidation of glucose: the electrons photosynthesis loaded onto carbon are removed in small steps, and much of the energy is captured rather than released as heat. There are four stages: **glycolysis** in the cytosol; **pyruvate oxidation** and the **citric acid cycle** in the mitochondrial matrix; and **oxidative phosphorylation** in the inner membrane (next week). By the end of this week's three stages, every carbon of glucose has left as CO₂, four ATP have been made directly, and ten NADH and two FADH₂ hold the electrons.

## Glycolysis

Ten steps, all in the cytosol, no oxygen, no organelle, no CO₂. The first five are the **energy-investment phase**: hexokinase phosphorylates glucose (trapping it in the cell), an isomerase rearranges it, **phosphofructokinase** adds a second phosphate, and the six-carbon doubly phosphorylated sugar is split into two three-carbon G3P. Two ATP have been spent to make the sugar unstable. The last five are the **energy-payoff phase**: each G3P is oxidised, loading one NAD⁺ to NADH, and phosphorylated, then gives up two phosphates to ADP by **substrate-level phosphorylation**, ending as pyruvate. Per glucose: 2 pyruvate, 2 NADH, 4 ATP made, 2 invested, net 2.

Phosphofructokinase is the valve. It catalyses the first committed step and is allosteric: ATP and citrate inhibit it, AMP and ADP activate it. A cell with plenty of ATP slows glucose breakdown; a working cell speeds it up. This is week 8's feedback inhibition, with the pathway's ultimate product as the signal.

Glycolysis is the same in bacteria, yeast, plants and animals and needs no oxygen. The CED treats that universality as evidence of common ancestry: one origin in an ancestor that lived before the atmosphere held oxygen.

## Pyruvate oxidation and the citric acid cycle

Pyruvate is transported into the matrix, and three things happen to each one: a carboxyl group leaves as **CO₂** (the first glucose carbon fully oxidised), the two-carbon remainder is oxidised with NAD⁺ → **NADH**, and the acetyl group is attached to coenzyme A to make **acetyl CoA**. Two of each per glucose.

Acetyl CoA hands its two carbons to four-carbon oxaloacetate, making six-carbon citrate, and the **citric acid cycle** then strips them back down. Per turn: 2 CO₂ released, 3 NADH and 1 FADH₂ loaded, 1 ATP (or GTP) made by substrate-level phosphorylation, and oxaloacetate regenerated to go again. Two turns per glucose. All the enzymes are in the matrix except succinate dehydrogenase, which sits in the inner membrane and doubles as part of the electron transport chain. ATP and NADH slow the cycle's dehydrogenases, ADP speeds them, and pyruvate dehydrogenase upstream is inhibited by acetyl CoA and NADH, so the pathway runs at the rate demand sets.

The cycle is where the carbon leaves, not where most ATP is made: four ATP by the end of this week against roughly thirty by the end of next. The energy is in the twelve reduced carriers.

The pathways are porous. Glycogen enters as glucose 6-phosphate; fructose and galactose enter as glycolytic intermediates; glycerol from fat enters as G3P; fatty acids are chopped by **beta-oxidation** into acetyl CoA, with NADH and FADH₂ made at each cut, which is why fat yields about 9 kcal/g; amino acids are **deaminated** (the nitrogen leaves as urea) and their skeletons enter as pyruvate, acetyl CoA or cycle intermediates. Intermediates are also drawn out to build amino acids, nucleotides and lipids.

## Worked example 1: a Calvin-cycle budget

*Question:* How many turns, ATP and NADPH does one glucose cost?

*Reasoning:* Six carbons need six CO₂, so six turns. Each turn fixes one CO₂, uses 2 ATP and 2 NADPH in reduction and 1 ATP in regeneration: 3 ATP and 2 NADPH per turn. Six turns: 18 ATP and 12 NADPH. Check by G3P: three turns give one G3P at 9 ATP and 6 NADPH; two G3P make a glucose; double it. Respiration recovers only about 30 ATP from that glucose; the second law guarantees the round trip is not free.

## Worked example 2: carbon and electron accounting

*Question:* Where are the six carbons, and where is the energy, after glycolysis, pyruvate oxidation and the citric acid cycle?

*Reasoning:* Glycolysis: 6 carbons → 2 × 3 (no CO₂); 2 NADH; net 2 ATP. Pyruvate oxidation: 2 CO₂; 2 NADH. Cycle, two turns: 4 CO₂; 6 NADH; 2 FADH₂; 2 ATP. Totals: 6 CO₂ (all the carbon), 10 NADH, 2 FADH₂, 4 ATP. Labelled glucose first appears in CO₂ at pyruvate oxidation, never in glycolysis; the O₂ you breathe becomes water, never CO₂.

## Worked example 3: reading a tracer and intermediate table

| Condition | RuBP | 3-PGA |
|---|---|---|
| Light on, CO₂ on | 1.0 | 1.0 |
| 30 s after light off | 0.2 | 2.4 |
| 30 s after CO₂ off | 2.6 | 0.3 |

*Reasoning:* For each intermediate, ask what makes it and what uses it. 3-PGA is made by fixation (needs CO₂ and RuBP, not ATP) and used by reduction (needs ATP and NADPH). RuBP is made by regeneration (needs ATP) and used by fixation (needs CO₂). Light off: reduction and regeneration stop, fixation runs on for a while, so 3-PGA rises and RuBP falls. CO₂ off: fixation stops, reduction and regeneration continue until 3-PGA is gone, so RuBP rises and 3-PGA falls. The careful conclusion is not "the Calvin cycle needs light" but "the Calvin cycle needs the products of the light reactions", because fixation itself visibly continued in the dark.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The Calvin cycle happens at night."** It runs by day, on fresh ATP and NADPH. Light-independent describes the chemistry, not the schedule.
- **"The Calvin cycle makes glucose."** It makes G3P. Glucose is assembled from two G3P outside the cycle.
- **"O₂ comes from CO₂."** It comes from water. CO₂'s carbon and oxygen go into sugar and water.
- **"Glycolysis needs oxygen."** It uses none and releases no CO₂; it is the one stage every organism shares.
- **"Most ATP is made in the citric acid cycle."** Two ATP per glucose are. The cycle's job is to load carriers.
- **"CO₂ is released in the electron transport chain."** All six CO₂ leave in the matrix stages, before the chain.
- **"Carbon from glucose ends up in ATP."** Glucose contributes energy, not atoms; ATP is made from ADP and phosphate.
