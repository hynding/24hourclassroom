## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Facilitated diffusion | Passive movement through a membrane protein down a gradient; no ATP. |
| Channel protein | A hydrated pore; the substance passes without binding. Tens of millions per second. Some are gated. |
| Carrier protein | Binds the substance, changes shape, releases it on the other side. Specific; saturates; thousands to a million per second. |
| Aquaporin | A channel for water. |
| Osmosis | Diffusion of free water across a selectively permeable membrane, toward the side with more solute. |
| Hypotonic, isotonic, hypertonic | A solution with fewer, equal, or more solutes than the cell; water enters, stays, or leaves. |
| Turgor, plasmolysis | A plant cell pressing on its wall in hypotonic solution; its protoplast pulling away from the wall in hypertonic solution. |
| Water potential (Ψ) | The tendency of water to move; Ψ = Ψs + Ψp in bars. Pure water at atmospheric pressure is 0. Water moves toward lower Ψ. |
| Solute potential (Ψs) | −iCRT; always zero or negative. |
| Pressure potential (Ψp) | Physical pressure; positive in a turgid cell, zero in an open beaker. |
| Osmoregulation | Keeping water and solute balance; contractile vacuoles, gills, kidneys. |
| Endocytosis | Taking material in by pinching off a vesicle: phagocytosis, pinocytosis, receptor-mediated. |
| Exocytosis | Releasing a vesicle's contents by fusing it with the plasma membrane. |

## Channels and carriers

Last week's table said polar molecules and ions barely cross a bare bilayer. Real cells move them quickly, and the difference is **facilitated diffusion**: a protein offers a hydrophilic path, the substance moves down its gradient, and the cell spends nothing. A **channel** is a pore lined with polar groups that replace the water of hydration an ion would otherwise have to shed; ions and water pass without binding, at tens of millions per second. Many channels are **gated**, opening in response to a voltage change, a bound ligand or stretch, which is how nerves fire. **Aquaporins** are channels for water, abundant wherever water must move fast: kidney tubules, red blood cells, roots. A **carrier** binds its substrate, changes shape and lets go on the other side. It is specific (a glucose carrier ignores galactose, which differs by one hydroxyl orientation) and it **saturates**: each carrier handles one molecule at a time, so once every carrier is busy the rate stops rising no matter how steep the gradient. A plateau on a rate-versus-concentration graph is the fingerprint of a carrier; a straight line is simple diffusion.

## Osmosis and tonicity

Water is the one substance every cell must manage. **Osmosis** is the diffusion of free water across a selectively permeable membrane, from where free water is more concentrated (fewer solutes) to where it is less (more solutes). Dissolved particles tie water up in hydration shells (week 1), so a solution has less free water than pure water, and the more particles, the less. What matters is the number of particles, not their size: one sucrose molecule and one Na⁺ ion count the same, and NaCl counts twice because it dissociates.

**Tonicity** describes what a solution does to a cell's volume. A **hypotonic** solution has fewer solutes than the cell, so water enters; **isotonic**, no net change; **hypertonic**, water leaves. The consequence depends on whether the cell has a wall. A red blood cell in pure water swells and bursts (**lysis**); in strong salt it shrivels (**crenation**); in 0.9% NaCl, which is isotonic, it is unchanged, which is why that is the concentration of intravenous saline. A plant cell in pure water takes up water until its wall pushes back and it is **turgid**, the healthy state that holds a plant upright; in a hypertonic solution the protoplast shrinks away from the wall (**plasmolysis**) and the plant wilts.

## Water potential: the quantity that predicts direction

When numbers are given, use **water potential**, Ψ, measured in bars (or megapascals), which combines everything that affects water's tendency to move:

**Ψ = Ψs + Ψp**

**Solute potential** Ψs is always zero or negative; dissolving anything lowers it. It is calculated from **Ψs = −iCRT**, where *i* is the ionisation constant (1 for sucrose or glucose, 2 for NaCl), *C* is the molar concentration, *R* is the pressure constant 0.0831 L·bar/(mol·K), and *T* is the temperature in kelvin (°C + 273). **Pressure potential** Ψp is physical pressure: zero in an open beaker, positive inside a turgid cell pressing on its wall. Pure water in an open container has Ψ = 0, the highest value a solution can have. **Water moves from higher Ψ to lower Ψ.**

Two warnings. First, add the components for each compartment *before* comparing: a plant cell with Ψs = −10 and Ψp = +6 is at −4, and it will gain water from a beaker at −2.5 even though its Ψs is far below the beaker's. Second, in an open beaker the solution's Ψ is just its Ψs, so a tissue at equilibrium with it has that water potential.

## Osmoregulation

Organisms spend energy to live in water that does not match them. A freshwater *Paramecium* is constantly flooded and pumps water out through a **contractile vacuole**, using ATP. Freshwater fish are saltier than their water, so water enters and salt leaks out; they excrete large volumes of dilute urine and actively absorb salt through their gills. Marine bony fish are the reverse: they lose water, so they drink sea water, excrete little concentrated urine and pump salt out through their gills. Either way a fish spends about 5% of its metabolic energy on water balance. In you, osmoreceptors sense the solute concentration of blood and, through a hormone (ADH), tell the kidney to insert aquaporins and reclaim water, so the urine becomes concentrated. Water is never pumped; organisms move solutes and let water follow.

## Bulk transport

Molecules too large for any protein, or needed in bulk, travel in vesicles. **Endocytosis** takes material in: **phagocytosis** engulfs a particle or a whole cell into a vesicle that then fuses with a lysosome; **pinocytosis** takes in a droplet of fluid with whatever is dissolved in it; **receptor-mediated endocytosis** concentrates a specific substance on surface receptors in a **clathrin**-coated pit before pinching it in. The last is how cells take up cholesterol as LDL; people with defective LDL receptors have dangerously high blood cholesterol. **Exocytosis** does the reverse: a vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane and releases hormone, enzyme, antibody or neurotransmitter outside, and the vesicle membrane joins the cell surface. All of it costs ATP, for the cytoskeleton that moves vesicles and the proteins that bend and fuse membranes, so bulk transport is active regardless of any gradient. The two directions balance: a secretory cell takes back by endocytosis the membrane it adds by exocytosis.

## Worked example 1: a solute-potential calculation

*Question:* What is Ψs of 0.3 M NaCl at 25 °C?

*Reasoning:* Ψs = −iCRT = −(2)(0.3)(0.0831)(298) = −14.9 bar. The *i* of 2 is the step most often missed; 0.3 M sucrose would be −7.4 bar. Convert temperature to kelvin every time: 25 °C is 298 K, not 25.

| 0.4 | −2 |

*Question:* What is the water potential of the potato tissue at 22 °C?

*Reasoning:* Describe first: mass change falls with concentration and crosses zero between 0.2 and 0.4 M, by interpolation at about 0.36 M. That solution is isotonic to the tissue, so the tissue's Ψ equals the solution's Ψ, which in an open beaker is Ψs alone: −(1)(0.36)(0.0831)(295) ≈ −8.8 bar. The cores in water gained mass because 0 bar is higher than −8.8 bar.

## Worked example 3: channel or carrier?

Glucose uptake by red blood cells rises with external concentration, then flattens at a maximum. Ethanol uptake rises in a straight line with no limit.

*Reasoning:* Glucose shows saturation, so it is entering through a finite number of carriers; ethanol shows none, so it is diffusing through the bilayer. If a glucose analogue of similar shape were added, glucose uptake would fall (competition for binding sites) and ethanol uptake would not.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Water moves toward the higher solute concentration because solutes pull it."** Solutes do not pull; they lower free water, and free water diffuses down its own gradient.
- **"Compare Ψs values to decide direction."** Compare total Ψ. Pressure counts.
- **"Plant cells don't burst because their membrane is less permeable."** Their membrane is highly permeable; the wall builds the pressure that stops net entry.
- **"Endocytosis is passive because nothing moves against a gradient."** It costs ATP to reshape membrane; bulk transport is always active.
- **"Hypertonic means more water."** Hypertonic means more solute, so water leaves the cell.
