## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Electron transport chain | Four complexes (I to IV) and two mobile carriers in the inner membrane that pass electrons toward oxygen, pumping protons. |
| Ubiquinone (Q) | A lipid-soluble carrier moving electron pairs from complexes I and II to complex III. |
| Cytochrome c | A small protein carrying single electrons from complex III to complex IV. |
| Final electron acceptor | Oxygen, reduced to water at complex IV. |
| Proton-motive force | The energy of the H⁺ gradient (concentration plus charge) across the inner membrane. |
| Chemiosmosis | ATP synthesis driven by protons flowing down their gradient through ATP synthase. |
| ATP synthase | A rotary membrane enzyme that couples proton flow to the phosphorylation of ADP. |
| Oxidative phosphorylation | The chain plus chemiosmosis; about 90% of respiration's ATP. |
| Uncoupler | A molecule (DNP, thermogenin) that lets protons leak across the membrane, converting the gradient to heat. |
| Fermentation | Regenerating NAD⁺ by passing electrons from NADH to an organic acceptor, with no chain and no extra ATP. |
| Lactic acid fermentation | Pyruvate reduced directly to lactate; muscle, red blood cells, some bacteria. |
| Alcohol fermentation | Pyruvate decarboxylated to acetaldehyde, which is reduced to ethanol; yeast. |
| Anaerobic respiration | A chain with an inorganic acceptor other than O₂ (sulfate, nitrate, CO₂); some prokaryotes. |
| Facultative / obligate anaerobe | Can ferment or respire as oxygen allows / is poisoned by oxygen. |
| Respirometer | A sealed chamber with a CO₂ absorbent that measures oxygen uptake as a volume change. |

## The electron transport chain

Last week ended with ten NADH and two FADH₂ per glucose holding the energy. The **electron transport chain** that cashes them in is four protein complexes embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane, numbered I to IV, with two small mobile carriers between them. NADH hands its electron pair to **complex I**. FADH₂ hands its pair to **complex II**, which is the same enzyme as the citric acid cycle's succinate dehydrogenase. From either entry, **ubiquinone**, a lipid-soluble molecule that moves within the bilayer, carries the electrons to **complex III**; **cytochrome c**, a small protein on the membrane surface, carries them one at a time to **complex IV**; and complex IV passes them to **oxygen**, which with four protons becomes two molecules of water. That is the only step of respiration that uses O₂ and the only place the water in the overall equation is made.

The electrons fall in energy at each transfer, because each carrier is a stronger electron acceptor than the one before, and oxygen, the most electronegative, is the strongest. Complexes I, III and IV use the energy of the fall to pump protons from the matrix into the intermembrane space. Complex II pumps none, which is why FADH₂'s electrons, entering there, drive two pumping sites instead of three and yield about 1.5 ATP against NADH's 2.5.

## Chemiosmosis and ATP synthase

Pumping makes the intermembrane space acidic and positively charged relative to the matrix. The inner membrane's lipid bilayer is nearly impermeable to H⁺ (Unit 2: ions do not cross the nonpolar core), so the gradient holds. Its stored energy, concentration plus charge, is the **proton-motive force**.

The one route back is **ATP synthase**, a rotary motor. Protons flowing down their gradient through its membrane portion turn a rotor; the rotation changes the shape of catalytic sites in the matrix portion, forcing ADP and Pi together. This is facilitated diffusion coupled to an endergonic synthesis, and it is called **chemiosmosis**. The whole arrangement, chain plus synthase, is **oxidative phosphorylation**. It makes about 26 to 28 ATP per glucose, roughly 90% of the total; substrate-level phosphorylation in glycolysis and the cycle contributes the other 4.

Two kinds of experiment prove the mechanism. A **poison** such as cyanide blocks complex IV: electrons stop, pumping stops, the gradient collapses, ATP stops, oxygen consumption stops, and because NADH is no longer reoxidised the cycle stops too. An **uncoupler** such as DNP does the opposite: it carries protons across the lipid, so electrons keep flowing (oxygen consumption rises) but the gradient leaks away as heat and ATP falls. Brown fat in newborn mammals uses a natural uncoupler protein to make heat on purpose.

The yield per glucose is often quoted as 36 to 38 and more realistically as 30 to 32. It varies because protons are also spent importing pyruvate and phosphate, the two cytosolic NADH may enter the chain as FADH₂ depending on the shuttle, intermediates are drawn off for biosynthesis, and membranes leak a little. About 34% of glucose's free energy ends up in ATP; the rest is heat, the second law's fee.

## Life without oxygen

Take oxygen away and the chain backs up, the gradient falls, and every NAD⁺-dependent step in the matrix stops. Glycolysis can continue, but only if its NADH is reoxidised, and that is the entire purpose of **fermentation**: pass the electrons from NADH to an organic molecule so NAD⁺ is regenerated and glycolysis keeps making its 2 ATP. The fermentation steps make no ATP themselves; their products are waste.

**Lactic acid fermentation** reduces pyruvate directly to lactate, keeping all three carbons and releasing no gas. Muscle does it when effort outruns oxygen delivery; red blood cells, which have no mitochondria, do it always. **Alcohol fermentation**, in yeast, first removes CO₂ from pyruvate (making acetaldehyde) and then reduces the acetaldehyde to ethanol. The CO₂ raises bread; the ethanol is wine. Yeast is a **facultative anaerobe**, respiring when oxygen is present and fermenting when not. **Obligate anaerobes** such as Clostridium are poisoned by oxygen. Some prokaryotes perform **anaerobic respiration**, a genuine chain with an inorganic acceptor other than O₂ (sulfate, nitrate or CO₂), yielding more than fermentation but less than oxygen allows.

## Regulation

Respiration is tuned to demand at its committed steps. GLUT4, the glucose transporter of muscle and fat, is inserted into the membrane in response to insulin. **Phosphofructokinase** is inhibited by ATP and by citrate and activated by AMP and ADP, so glycolysis slows when the cell is rich and the cycle is backed up. **Pyruvate dehydrogenase** is inhibited by its products acetyl CoA and NADH. In the cycle, isocitrate dehydrogenase and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase slow with ATP and NADH and speed with ADP. The chain itself runs faster when ADP is high. All of it is week 8's allosteric regulation.

## The synthesis: photosynthesis and respiration side by side

The two processes are chemical opposites. Photosynthesis takes CO₂ and water, uses light to move electrons from water onto carbon, and stores the energy in sugar while releasing O₂. Respiration takes sugar and O₂, moves the electrons from carbon back onto oxygen, and releases the energy as ATP and heat while returning CO₂ and water. **Matter cycles** between them; **energy flows** through once, entering as light and leaving as heat. Plants do both, in every cell, all the time; at night they are net consumers of O₂.

The two chemiosmotic systems are the same machine in mirrored rooms. Both have an electron transport chain that pumps protons across a membrane and an ATP synthase that lets them back. In the chloroplast, protons are pumped into the thylakoid lumen and ATP is made in the stroma; electrons come from water and end on NADP⁺; the energy is light. In the mitochondrion, protons are pumped into the intermembrane space and ATP is made in the matrix; electrons come from NADH and end on O₂; the energy is food. Both organelles descend from free-living bacteria, which is why both carry their own DNA and run chemiosmosis on an internal membrane. Glycolysis is older than either, universal and anaerobic, the signature of a common ancestor that lived before oxygen.

## Worked example 1: a respirometer rate (AP Investigation 6)

Germinating peas at 25 °C moved the droplet 0.85 mL in 20 minutes; a vial of glass beads moved 0.03 mL.

*Reasoning:* KOH in the vial absorbed the CO₂ produced, so the volume change is oxygen consumed. Subtract the non-living control: 0.85 − 0.03 = 0.82 mL. Rate = Δy/Δx = 0.82 ÷ 20 = 0.041 mL/min. At 10 °C the same peas gave (0.38 − 0.02) ÷ 20 = 0.018 mL/min, so warming by 15 °C more than doubled the rate, as expected for enzyme-catalysed steps below their optimum. Do not extrapolate that doubling to 60 °C; the enzymes denature long before.

## Worked example 2: an ATP yield

*Question:* Using 2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 per FADH₂, calculate the ATP per glucose.

*Reasoning:* Substrate-level: 2 (glycolysis) + 2 (cycle) = 4. Carriers: 10 NADH × 2.5 = 25; 2 FADH₂ × 1.5 = 3. Total 32. If the two glycolytic NADH enter as FADH₂ (the glycerol-phosphate shuttle), subtract 2: 30. If asked why the textbook says 36 to 38, say older values assumed 3 and 2 per carrier. If asked for fermentation, the answer is 2.

## Worked example 3: predicting a poison's effect

*Question:* Oligomycin blocks the proton channel of ATP synthase. Predict the effect on ATP synthesis, the gradient, electron transport and oxygen consumption.

*Reasoning:* ATP synthesis stops at once. Protons can no longer return, so the gradient builds until the pumps cannot work against it; then electron transport stops, and because electrons are no longer reaching oxygen, oxygen consumption stops. Compare DNP: ATP stops but the gradient collapses, so electron transport and oxygen consumption *rise*. Opposite oxygen readings, same ATP result: a classic data item.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Oxygen is needed to make CO₂."** All CO₂ leaves in the matrix before the chain. Oxygen becomes water.
- **"Most ATP comes from the citric acid cycle."** Two per glucose do. The cycle loads carriers; the chain makes the ATP.
- **"Fermentation makes ATP."** It makes NAD⁺. Glycolysis makes the ATP, and only 2 of it.
- **"Anaerobic respiration and fermentation are the same thing."** Anaerobic respiration uses a chain with an inorganic acceptor; fermentation uses neither.
- **"Protons diffuse freely through the membrane."** Then there would be no gradient and no ATP.
- **"Plants photosynthesise instead of respiring."** Plants respire in every cell, day and night.
- **"The chloroplast and the mitochondrion pump protons the same way."** The mechanism is the same; the direction and compartments are mirrored.
