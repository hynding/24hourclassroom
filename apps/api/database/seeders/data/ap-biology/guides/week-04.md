## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Prokaryote | A cell with no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles; DNA sits in a nucleoid region. About 0.1-5 μm. |
| Eukaryote | A cell with a membrane-bound nucleus and organelles. About 10-100 μm. |
| Nucleus | Double-membrane envelope with pores; holds chromatin and the nucleolus, where ribosome subunits are assembled. |
| Ribosome | Two subunits of rRNA and protein that translate mRNA. Free in the cytosol or bound to the rough ER. |
| Rough ER | Ribosome-studded sheets where secreted and membrane proteins are folded and glycosylated. |
| Smooth ER | Ribosome-free ER that makes lipids and steroids, detoxifies poisons and stores calcium. |
| Golgi apparatus | Stacked sacs: receive at the cis face, modify and tag, ship from the trans face. |
| Lysosome | Single-membrane sac of acid-optimal hydrolytic enzymes; the cell's recycling centre. |
| Mitochondrion | Double-membrane organelle; inner membrane folded into cristae around the matrix; site of ATP synthesis; own DNA and ribosomes. |
| Chloroplast | Double envelope around thylakoids (stacked into grana) and stroma; site of photosynthesis; own DNA and ribosomes. |
| Central vacuole | Large plant-cell compartment that stores water and solutes and produces turgor. |
| Surface area to volume ratio | SA ÷ V. Falls as a cell grows because SA scales with the square and V with the cube of size. |
| Endomembrane system | Nuclear envelope, ER, Golgi, lysosomes, vesicles and plasma membrane, linked by vesicle traffic. Not mitochondria or chloroplasts. |
| Endosymbiosis | One cell living inside another to mutual benefit; the origin of mitochondria and chloroplasts. |

## The organelles as a division of labour

Every cell has four things: a plasma membrane, cytoplasm, DNA and ribosomes. A prokaryote stops there, with its DNA in an open nucleoid region and a peptidoglycan wall outside its membrane. A eukaryote adds a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles, which the exam treats as a system of specialised parts, not a list.

The **nucleus** is bounded by two membranes pierced by pores; inside, DNA is wound into chromatin and the **nucleolus** assembles ribosome subunits, which leave through the pores and join in the cytosol as **ribosomes** to translate mRNA. Where a ribosome sits tells you where its product goes: free ribosomes make cytosolic proteins; ribosomes docked on the **rough ER** feed their chain into the ER lumen, where it folds and gains its first sugars, bound for membranes, lysosomes or export. The **smooth ER** has no ribosomes and does chemistry: lipid and steroid synthesis, detoxification in liver cells, calcium storage in muscle.

Vesicles bud from the ER and fuse with the **Golgi** at its cis face; cargo is modified (mostly by editing sugar chains) and tagged for its destination as it moves through the stack, then leaves in vesicles from the trans face. Some become **lysosomes**, acid-filled sacs of hydrolases that digest worn-out organelles and engulfed microbes; others fuse with the plasma membrane and release their contents outside, the secretory pathway. A pancreatic cell that exports enzymes is packed with rough ER and Golgi; a liver cell that detoxifies is rich in smooth ER. A cell's organelle inventory is its job description.

The **mitochondrion** has two membranes; the inner is folded into **cristae**, which carry the electron transport chain and ATP synthase, around the **matrix**, where the Krebs cycle runs and the organelle's own DNA and ribosomes sit. The **chloroplast** has a double envelope around flattened sacs, the **thylakoids**, stacked into **grana**; the light reactions run in the thylakoid membranes and the Calvin cycle in the surrounding fluid, the **stroma**.

Plant cells also have a cellulose **cell wall** and a large **central vacuole** whose pressure against the wall holds the plant up; animal cells have centrosomes and lysosomes, which plants lack. The **cytoskeleton** runs through everything: actin microfilaments (7 nm) for movement, intermediate filaments (8-10 nm) for tension, microtubules (25 nm) for vesicle tracks, the spindle and cilia.

## Why cells are small

Everything a cell needs enters across its surface; everything it does happens throughout its volume. For a cube of side *s*, surface area is 6*s*² and volume *s*³, so SA:V = 6/*s*. For a sphere, SA:V = 3/*r*. Either way the ratio falls as the cell grows. A 1 mm cube has a ratio of 6; a 2 mm cube, 3; a 3 mm cube, 2. Double the size and each unit of interior has half the surface serving it.

Diffusion makes it worse: in a given time it covers a fixed distance whatever the size of the block, so the centre of a large cell is out of reach. Cells answer in three ways: stay small and divide; change shape (flat, long, or with projections such as microvilli and root hairs, which add surface without much volume); and fold membranes inside, which is what cristae and thylakoids are. Topics 2.2 and 2.9 are one idea at two scales.

## Compartmentalization: why organelles pay for themselves

A membrane-bound compartment lets a cell run incompatible chemistries at once: the lysosome keeps its hydrolases at pH 5 while the cytosol stays at 7.2, so digestion is contained and a leak is harmless because the enzymes barely work at neutral pH. It concentrates enzymes with their substrates, holds gradients across internal membranes that mitochondria and chloroplasts spend to make ATP, and multiplies membrane surface. The cost is building membranes and managing traffic, which is why the endomembrane system is a system: ER, Golgi, lysosomes, vesicles, nuclear envelope and plasma membrane constantly exchange membrane. Mitochondria and chloroplasts are not members; they trade no vesicles with the rest, and the reason is their history.

## Endosymbiosis: organelles with a past

The **endosymbiotic theory** holds that an ancestral host cell engulfed an aerobic bacterium, both benefited, and over time the partners became one organism; the bacterium is now the mitochondrion. Later, in one lineage, a cyanobacterium was taken in the same way and became the chloroplast. Four observations make the case; be able to recite them and say why each matters:

1. **Two membranes.** The inner one resembles a bacterial membrane (the cristae even look like the surface of alpha-proteobacteria); the outer is the host's engulfing membrane.
2. **Their own circular DNA**, not wound on histones, like a bacterial chromosome.
3. **Their own ribosomes and tRNAs**, bacterial in type and blocked by the same antibiotics.
4. **Division by binary fission**, on their own schedule. New mitochondria come only from old ones.

Mitochondria are in essentially every eukaryotic lineage while chloroplasts are in only some, so the mitochondrial event came first; eukaryote-like fossils appear about 2.1 billion years ago. The nucleus and endomembrane system arose by inward folding of the host's own membrane. Some algae later engulfed an alga that already had chloroplasts (**secondary endosymbiosis**), giving plastids with three or four membranes and sometimes a remnant algal nucleus.

## Worked example 1: the SA:V table

| Side (μm) | SA (μm²) | V (μm³) | SA:V |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6 | 1 | 6 |
| 2 | 24 | 8 | 3 |
| 4 | 96 | 64 | 1.5 |

*Question:* A cell needs at least 2 μm² of membrane per μm³ of cytoplasm to supply itself. What is the largest cube it can be?

*Reasoning:* SA:V = 6/*s* ≥ 2, so *s* ≤ 3 μm. Beyond that the cell must divide, change shape or fold its membranes. Quadrupling the side cuts the ratio to a quarter.

## Worked example 2: reading the agar-cube data (Investigation 4)

Agar cubes containing an indicator are placed in sodium hydroxide; the indicator changes colour where hydroxide has diffused in.

| Side (cm) | SA:V | Depth reached (cm) | Volume reached (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6 | 0.4 | 99 |
| 2 | 3 | 0.4 | 78 |
| 3 | 2 | 0.4 | 59 |

*Question:* What do the data show about diffusion as a supply mechanism?

*Reasoning:* Describe: depth is the same in every cube, so diffusion ran at the same rate, but the fraction of the interior reached fell from 99% to 59%. Explain: a fixed depth is most of a small cube and little of a large one. Apply: a cell relying on diffusion must be small enough that its centre is within reach.

## Worked example 3: building an argument from evidence

*Claim to evaluate:* "Lysosomes, like mitochondria, were once free-living bacteria."

*Reasoning:* Apply the four tests. Lysosomes have one membrane, no DNA, no ribosomes, and are made by budding from the Golgi rather than by dividing. They fail every test that mitochondria pass, so the claim is not supported. A membrane and enzymes are not evidence of ancestry, because a cell-built compartment has those too; evidence must discriminate between the hypotheses.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Plant cells have chloroplasts instead of mitochondria."** They have both. Chloroplasts make sugar in the light; mitochondria release its energy in every cell at every hour.
- **"The nucleus makes proteins."** It makes mRNA and ribosome subunits; translation happens in the cytosol or on the ER.
- **"Larger cells are more efficient because they have more membrane."** More total membrane, less per unit of interior; the ratio is what matters.
- **"Mitochondria are part of the endomembrane system."** They exchange no vesicles with it and have a separate origin.
- **"Any organelle with a membrane could have been a bacterium."** Only the two with their own DNA, ribosomes and fission.
