## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Genetic diversity | The number and frequency of different alleles in a population; the raw material selection can act on. |
| Heterozygosity | The proportion of loci at which an individual carries two different alleles; 2pq under Hardy-Weinberg. |
| Monoculture | A crop of one genotype; a population with no variation. |
| Genetic rescue | Restoring variation to a depleted population by introducing unrelated individuals (managed gene flow). |
| Stromatolite | A layered rock built by microbial mats; the oldest fossils of life, about 3.5 billion years old. |
| Oxygenation | The rise of atmospheric oxygen from cyanobacterial photosynthesis, beginning about 2.3 billion years ago. |
| Abiotic synthesis | Formation of organic molecules without life, as in the Miller-Urey experiment. |
| Protocell | A membrane-bound vesicle enclosing self-replicating molecules; the first unit of selection. |
| RNA world | The hypothesis that RNA, which both stores information and catalyses reactions, preceded DNA and protein. |
| Ribozyme | A catalytic RNA; the ribosome's peptide-bond-forming centre is one. |
| Endosymbiosis | One cell living inside another; the origin of mitochondria (from an alpha-proteobacterium) and chloroplasts (from a cyanobacterium). |
| LUCA | The last universal common ancestor of all living things; already a cell with DNA, the code and ribosomes. |

## Why variation decides who survives

Natural selection chooses among phenotypes that exist. A population rich in alleles is likely to contain, somewhere, individuals that resist a new pathogen, tolerate a hotter summer or escape a new predator; selection favours them and the population adapts. A population with few alleles may contain no such individuals. Its members respond alike, so a stress that harms one harms all, and the population declines or disappears. That is the whole of CED 7.11, and the exam tests it with data.

The gauge is **heterozygosity**: the proportion of loci at which an average individual carries two different alleles. In a large, randomly mating population it equals 2pq at each locus; in a small one it falls every generation as drift removes alleles. Three facts follow from week 24. A bottleneck or founder event cuts variation at once, and recovering numbers does not recover alleles: cheetahs, descended from a severe ancient bottleneck, are so uniform that skin grafts between unrelated animals are accepted, and northern elephant seals, hunted to about twenty, remain almost without variation after rebounding to over a hundred thousand. Lost alleles come back only by mutation, which is far too slow, or by **gene flow**, which is why conservation programs move animals between isolated populations and why seed banks store the wild relatives of crops.

Humans build the extreme case on purpose. A **monoculture**, a field of one genotype propagated by cuttings, has heterozygosity of zero. One potato clone met one blight in 1840s Ireland; the Cavendish banana, a single clone grown worldwide, faces a fungus today. Numbers are no protection: a million identical plants offer selection one genotype, and if it is susceptible, all are.

Two distinctions matter in answers. Population size and genetic diversity are different things; a large population can be uniform. And phenotypic variation is not the same as heritable variation; a population whose differences are all environmental cannot evolve, however varied it looks.

## Worked example 1: heterozygosity and survival

| Population | Founders | Heterozygosity | Survival after exposure to a fungus (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 4 | 0.12 | 15 |
| B | 12 | 0.31 | 42 |
| C | 40 | 0.58 | 71 |
| Wild source | — | 0.64 | 78 |

*Pattern.* Survival rises with heterozygosity, and heterozygosity rises with the number of founders. The wild population shows what the captive ones lost.

*Mechanism.* Four founders carry at most eight alleles per locus, an unrepresentative sample (founder effect), and a small population loses more each generation (drift). With few alleles, population A had almost no resistant variants for selection to favour.

*Loss.* Population A has lost (0.64 − 0.12) / 0.64 = 81% of the source's heterozygosity. *Rescue.* Introduce unrelated animals (gene flow) to add alleles, then keep the population large so drift is weak. Breeding A up from its own animals would raise numbers without restoring a single lost allele.

## The timeline of life

| Event | Billion years ago |
|---|---|
| Earth forms | 4.6 |
| Possible chemical traces of life in rocks | 3.8 |
| Oldest stromatolites (fossil microbial mats) | 3.5 |
| Oxygen begins to accumulate (cyanobacteria) | 2.3 |
| Oldest eukaryotic fossils | 2.1 |
| Primary endosymbiosis of chloroplasts | 1.5 to 1.0 |
| Oldest multicellular animals | 0.6 |

Life was prokaryotic for 3.5 − 2.1 = 1.4 billion years before the first eukaryote. In that time cyanobacteria evolved water-splitting photosynthesis and released oxygen, which first reacted with dissolved iron (leaving banded iron formations) and then built up in the air from about 2.3 billion years ago. Oxygen was toxic to most existing life; it drove the evolution of **aerobic respiration**, and later the ozone layer that made land habitable. The order is itself an argument: an aerobic bacterium had to exist before one could become a mitochondrion, and eukaryotes appear in the record just after oxygen rises.

## How life might have begun

Natural selection does not explain the origin of life; it begins once there are replicators with heritable variation. The question before that is chemistry, and the hypotheses are tested step by step.

1. **Abiotic synthesis of monomers.** Oparin and Haldane proposed that a reducing early atmosphere, energised by lightning and ultraviolet light, would make organic molecules that accumulated in the oceans. In 1953 Miller and Urey tested it: water vapour, methane, ammonia and hydrogen circulated past sparks for a week yielded amino acids and other organics. It showed that building blocks form without life. It did **not** make polymers, cells or anything alive, and the exact early atmosphere is still debated; mixtures based on volcanic gases also work, and amino acids and nucleotide bases turn up in meteorites. Deep-sea **hydrothermal vents**, with heat, hydrogen, sulfides, metal catalysts and chemical gradients across mineral pores, are the leading alternative setting.
2. **Polymers.** Monomers link on clay and mineral surfaces that concentrate and orient them.
3. **Self-replicating molecules.** Here the **RNA world** enters. RNA stores a sequence *and* folds into catalytic shapes (**ribozymes**), so an RNA molecule could in principle copy itself; DNA stores but cannot catalyse, protein catalyses but cannot be copied from a template. Modern cells keep the evidence: the ribosome's peptide-bond-forming centre is RNA, DNA replication starts from RNA primers, ribonucleotides are the precursors of deoxyribonucleotides, and ATP and NAD are RNA-based cofactors. DNA, more stable, later took over storage; proteins, better catalysts, took over most chemistry. From this step on, natural selection applies: copies that replicate faster or more accurately become more common.
4. **Protocells.** Amphipathic lipids in water form bilayer vesicles spontaneously. A vesicle around a replicator keeps its products with it, so a better replicator benefits its own lineage: the first unit on which selection could act.

The **last universal common ancestor** was far along this road, already a cell with DNA, the genetic code, ribosomes and ATP, which is why those features are universal (week 25). It was not the first life, only the last ancestor every surviving lineage shares. Early prokaryotes swapped genes so freely by horizontal transfer that the base of the tree looks more like a web than a branch.

## The eukaryotic cell: a merger

Lynn Margulis argued in the 1960s, against strong resistance, that mitochondria and chloroplasts descend from engulfed bacteria. The evidence, now accepted: each has **two membranes** (the inner one the bacterium's own, the outer from the host's vacuole), its **own circular chromosome**, **bacterial-type ribosomes** inhibited by antibiotics that spare cytosolic ribosomes, **division by binary fission** on its own schedule, bacterial size and shape, and gene sequences closest to **alpha-proteobacteria** (mitochondria) or **cyanobacteria** (chloroplasts). Most of the endosymbiont's genes moved to the host nucleus over time, which is why the organelles cannot live alone today. Chloroplasts came later, into a eukaryote that already had mitochondria; some algae then acquired photosynthesis by engulfing those algae (**secondary endosymbiosis**, recognisable by plastids with three or four membranes). Whether the host had a nucleus before or after it took in the mitochondrial ancestor is still open; both hypotheses agree the host was related to archaea, so the eukaryotic cell joins two domains.

## Worked example 2: the RNA-first argument

*Claim.* RNA preceded DNA and protein as the hereditary molecule.
*Evidence.* RNA catalyses peptide-bond formation in every ribosome; self-splicing RNAs exist; RNA primers start DNA synthesis; cells make deoxyribonucleotides from ribonucleotides.
*Reasoning.* Selection needs a molecule that is copied and varies; only RNA can serve as both template and catalyst, so only RNA could have replicated itself before proteins existed. The relics in modern cells are what we would expect if RNA once did everything and was later displaced where DNA or protein does the job better.

## Synthesis: the five weeks as one argument

Variation arises by mutation and recombination, at random with respect to need (week 23). Selection sorts it by reproductive success, drift samples it, gene flow spreads it, and Hardy-Weinberg tells you when nothing is happening (week 24). Run long enough, the process leaves fossils, homologies, shared genes and a molecular clock, and it is still running in every hospital (week 25). Cut gene flow, and selection and drift split one species into two; shared derived traits let us read the splits as a tree (week 26). Remove the variation, and a population cannot respond; trace the tree to its root, and it reaches a replicator that chemistry, not selection, had to make (week 27).

## Common misconceptions

- **"A big population is a safe population."** Size protects against drift going forward; it does not restore what a bottleneck took. Diversity is what the pathogen tests.
- **"Miller-Urey made life."** It made amino acids. Monomers, not cells.
- **"Oxygen was always there."** The atmosphere was anoxic for two billion years; cyanobacteria made the oxygen and it was a poison before it was a resource.
- **"Mitochondria are built by the cell."** They divide from existing mitochondria and carry a bacterial genome; the cell cannot make one from scratch.
- **"Evolution explains the origin of life."** It explains what happens once replicators exist.
- **"DNA came first because it is the genetic material now."** DNA cannot catalyse its own copying; RNA can, and the ribosome is the witness.
