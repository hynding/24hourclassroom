## What you need to be able to do

The College Board phrases this week's learning objectives as:

- **7.1** Describe the causes of natural selection, and explain how natural selection affects populations.
- **7.2** Describe the importance of phenotypic variation in a population, and explain how a population's phenotypic variation and the environment together determine which phenotypes are favoured.
- **7.3** Explain how humans can affect diversity within a population, and how the response to selection by humans is evidence for the mechanism of natural selection.

In plain language: given any population and any environment, you must be able to say whether the population *can* evolve (is there heritable variation?), *which way* it will evolve (which variants leave more offspring here?), and how to *read the result* from a table or histogram of a trait measured before and after selection. You must also be able to argue from a dog breed or a crop plant to the whole of evolution.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Natural selection | Individuals with heritable traits suited to the environment survive and reproduce more, so those traits become more common in the population across generations. |
| Adaptation | A heritable trait that raises survival and reproduction in the organism's present environment; also the process by which a population acquires such traits. |
| Fitness | An individual's genetic contribution to the next generation, measured by the number of surviving, reproducing offspring it leaves. |
| Relative fitness | Fitness expressed as a fraction of the most successful genotype's fitness. Evolution depends on relative, not absolute, fitness. |
| Selection pressure | Any environmental factor (predators, climate, food, mates, disease, a breeder) that makes some phenotypes reproduce more than others. |
| Heritability | The fraction of phenotypic variation due to genetic differences. Selection changes a population only in proportion to it. |
| Stabilizing selection | The average phenotype is favoured; the mean stays, the variation shrinks. |
| Directional selection | One extreme is favoured; the mean shifts toward it. |
| Disruptive selection | Both extremes are favoured over the intermediate; variation grows and the population may split. |
| Frequency-dependent selection | A phenotype's fitness depends on how common it is; when rare is favoured, several phenotypes persist. |
| Sexual selection | Selection by competition for mates; produces ornaments and weapons that may cost survival. |
| Sexual dimorphism | Consistent differences between males and females beyond the reproductive organs. |
| Convergent evolution | Similar traits evolving independently in unrelated lineages under similar pressures (bat and insect wings). |
| Artificial selection | Humans choose which individuals breed, so a chosen trait changes across generations. |
| Standard error | SE = s / √n; the uncertainty in a sample mean. Two means whose ±2 SE bars do not overlap are probably different. |

## The mechanism in three conditions

Darwin and Wallace reached the same idea independently, and presented it together in 1858. It needs only three facts about any population:

1. **Traits are heritable.** Offspring resemble parents because they inherit alleles.
2. **More offspring are produced than can survive.** Resources are limited, so individuals compete.
3. **Individuals vary**, and some variants compete better than others in the environment they are in.

Put the three together and the conclusion is forced: the variants that survive and reproduce best leave more offspring carrying their alleles, so the population's composition shifts each generation. Darwin called it **descent with modification**. Over many generations the population becomes **adapted** to its environment; when lineages split and adapt to different environments, they become different species (week 26).

Two things matter about the variation. First, it comes from **mutation**, which makes new alleles, and from **sexual reproduction**, which shuffles existing alleles into new combinations through crossing over, independent assortment and random fertilisation. Second, it is **random with respect to need**. A drought does not cause drought-resistance mutations; the variants are already there, and the drought only decides which of them leave descendants. The Lederbergs showed this directly in 1952 by finding antibiotic-resistant colonies on plates that had never seen the antibiotic.

A trait is favourable only relative to a particular environment. Large leaves are an adaptation in a moist climate because they capture more light; if the climate dries, the same leaves lose too much water and small leaves are selected instead. Natural selection has no foresight and no goal. Populations are not climbing toward complexity or perfection; they track whatever environment they are in with whatever variation they happen to have.

## Fitness is counted in offspring

**Fitness** is not strength, size or lifespan. It is an individual's contribution to the next generation's gene pool: how many surviving, reproducing offspring it leaves. A stag that lives six years and fathers nine fawns that reach adulthood is fitter than one that lives twenty years and fathers two. **Relative fitness** divides each genotype's success by the most successful genotype's: if GG lizards average 8 surviving offspring and gg average 6, the relative fitness of gg is 6/8 = 0.75. Selection acts on the 0.25 gap.

Because fitness is counted in offspring, selection can favour traits that *reduce* survival. A peacock's tail makes it slower and more visible, but peahens choose the males with the largest, brightest tails, so those males leave far more offspring. This is **sexual selection**, and it produces **sexual dimorphism** whenever one sex (usually males) varies greatly in mating success. Two explanations for why females prefer costly ornaments: the **good genes hypothesis** says the ornament signals disease resistance or metabolic efficiency that the offspring will inherit; the **handicap principle** says only a high-quality male can survive while paying for the ornament, so the cost makes the signal honest.

## The three modes of selection, read from data

The exam gives you a trait's distribution before and after selection and asks which mode is acting. Ask two questions: did the **mean** move, and did the **spread** change?

| Mode | Mean | Spread | Classic example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stabilizing | unchanged | narrows | Forest mice whose coats match the floor survive; very light and very dark are eaten |
| Directional | shifts toward one extreme | usually narrows | Finch beak depth after the 1977 Daphne Major drought |
| Disruptive (diversifying) | unchanged or bimodal | widens | Beach mice: light on sand, dark in grass, intermediates hidden nowhere |

Stabilizing selection is what a stable environment usually does; it removes variation. Directional selection follows an environmental change. Disruptive selection is the only mode that *increases* variation, and it is the seed of sympatric speciation (week 26).

Two further patterns keep variation in a population. In **negative frequency-dependent selection**, a phenotype's fitness falls as it becomes common. Male side-blotched lizards come in three throat colours: orange males hold big territories and beat blue males; blue males guard a single female and beat yellow "sneaker" males; yellow males slip into orange territories and beat orange. Whichever morph is common is the one its counter-strategy exploits, so the three cycle in frequency and none goes to fixation. **Heterozygote advantage** (the sickle-cell allele where malaria is common) is the other; it returns next week with Hardy-Weinberg.

Natural selection cannot build a perfect organism. It can only choose among variants that already exist; it cannot invent a trait. Alleles are inherited in linked blocks, so a good allele may travel with a bad one. Traits trade off: a mouse cannot be both best camouflaged and fastest. And drift and gene flow (week 24) can push a population away from what selection favours.

## Artificial selection: the mechanism on a human timescale

When a breeder decides which dogs, pigeons or wheat plants reproduce, the chosen trait changes across generations: that is **artificial selection**, and Darwin opened *On the Origin of Species* with it. The mechanism is identical to natural selection (heritable variation, non-random reproduction, change in allele frequency); only the **agent** differs. A breeder chooses by a trait with a goal in mind; an environment "chooses" by survival and reproduction with no goal at all.

Why is it evidence? Because it shows that selection on existing variation really does transform a population, within a few dozen generations, into something that looks like a different kind of organism (compare a wolf with a chihuahua). If a breeder can do that in centuries, an environment can do far more in millions of years. It also shows that the **response to selection** depends on two things: how strongly you select (how different the chosen parents are from the population mean) and how **heritable** the trait is. Strong selection on a trait whose variation is mostly environmental produces no response, which is the point of AP Investigation 1.

Humans also reduce diversity without meaning to. A crop bred from a few founders, or a breed inbred for a show standard, carries far less variation than its wild relative, and so has less to offer selection when a new disease arrives (week 27).

## Worked example 1: the Daphne Major drought with standard error

| Group | n | Mean beak depth (mm) | SD (mm) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1976 population | 400 | 9.4 | 1.0 |
| 1978 drought survivors | 100 | 9.9 | 1.0 |
| 1978 offspring | 200 | 9.7 | 1.0 |

*Mode.* The mean moved up by 0.5 mm after small soft seeds disappeared: directional selection for deeper beaks.

*Is the difference real?* The AP formula sheet gives SE = s / √n. For 1976, SE = 1.0 / √400 = 0.05 mm; for the survivors, SE = 1.0 / √100 = 0.10 mm. Build ±2 SE intervals: 9.3–9.5 mm and 9.7–10.1 mm. They do not overlap, so the difference is unlikely to be sampling chance. Note that the survivors' bar is wider only because the sample is smaller; the SD, the measure of real variation, is the same.

*Is it evolution?* The survivors are the same birds, selected; the offspring generation is where evolution shows. Its mean (9.7) is above the 1976 mean, so the response was inherited. It is below the survivors' mean because heritability is less than 1 and because the survivors mated with birds of all beak sizes.

## Worked example 2: relative fitness

Three genotypes of a moth leave, on average, 10, 8 and 4 surviving offspring. Relative fitnesses: 10/10 = 1.0, 8/10 = 0.8, 4/10 = 0.4. Selection is strongest against the third genotype, but the second is also being selected against, slowly. If the environment changes so that the third genotype leaves 12, the fitnesses are recomputed from the new maximum: 10/12 = 0.83, 8/12 = 0.67, 1.0. Fitness is always relative to the current population in the current environment.

## Worked example 3: an artificial-selection response

A class of Fast Plants has a mean of 8 trichomes per stem (range 0–25). Students let only the hairiest 10% (mean 20) breed. The offspring have a mean of 14.

*Reading it.* The selected parents were 12 trichomes above the population mean; the offspring are 6 above. The response is half the selection, which says about half the variation in hairiness is heritable. Had the offspring mean stayed at 8, the variation would have been environmental and selection could not act on it. An unselected control line grown alongside is needed to show the change was not caused by the growing conditions.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Individuals evolve."** An adult finch's beak does not deepen in a drought. The population's composition changes because some individuals die and others reproduce.
- **"The environment causes the mutations that are needed."** Variation exists before the environment changes; selection sorts it. Antibiotics did not create resistance genes.
- **"Fittest means strongest or longest-lived."** Fittest means leaving the most surviving offspring. A trait that shortens life can be favoured if it raises mating success.
- **"Natural selection produces perfect organisms."** It chooses among what exists, under trade-offs, with drift and gene flow interfering.
- **"Evolution has a direction toward complexity."** It has no goal; lineages become simpler as often as more complex, and a trait favoured today may be disfavoured tomorrow.
- **"Artificial selection is a different mechanism."** Same mechanism, different agent. That is why it counts as evidence.
- **"Non-overlapping means are always different, and equal SDs mean equal intervals."** The interval depends on the SE, which depends on n; compute it.
