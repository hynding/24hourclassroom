## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Species, genetic, ecosystem diversity | Number and evenness of species; variation within species; variety of ecosystems in a region. |
| Simpson's diversity index | 1 − Σ (n/N)²: the probability that two random individuals belong to different species. |
| Species richness / evenness | How many species / how equally individuals are shared among them. |
| Resistance | Ability to stay at equilibrium through a disturbance. |
| Resilience | Speed of return to equilibrium after a disturbance. |
| Endemic species | Found in one place only; the most extinction-prone. |
| Biodiversity hotspot | At least 1,500 endemic plants and 70% habitat lost; 34 of them hold 42% of vertebrates on 2.3% of land. |
| Ecosystem services | Pollination, water purification, soil formation, pest control, carbon storage. |
| Invasive species | Introduced where it did not evolve, spreading at natives' expense. |
| Habitat loss | The leading cause of extinction: clearing, draining, damming, fragmenting. |
| Overharvesting | Taking more than a population can replace; the cod collapse. |
| Greenhouse effect | CO₂, methane, water vapour, nitrous oxide and ozone trap infrared radiation. |
| Phenological mismatch | Partners in a timed interaction respond to warming at different rates. |
| El Niño | Weakened trade winds, warm eastern Pacific, suppressed upwelling, collapsed productivity. |
| Mass extinction | Five in the fossil record; the K-Pg impact 66 million years ago is the best documented. |
| Adaptive radiation | Rapid speciation into niches emptied by disruption. |

## Measuring diversity

Biodiversity has several layers. **Species diversity** is the most used, but **genetic diversity** within species is what lets populations adapt, and **ecosystem diversity** is the variety of communities a region holds. About 1.5 million eukaryotic species are described out of an estimated 8.7 million; at 17,000–20,000 new species a year, the catalogue would take 500 years to complete.

To compare communities you need a number that captures both **richness** (how many species) and **evenness** (how equally individuals are shared). The AP formula sheet gives **Simpson's diversity index = 1 − Σ (n/N)²**, where n is the count of one species and N the total. Σ (n/N)² is the chance that two individuals drawn at random are the same species; one minus it is the chance they differ. A monoculture scores 0; many equally common species score close to 1. Because each share is squared, a dominant species drags the index down hard: 90% in one species gives at most 0.19 whatever else is present, while five species at 20% each give 0.80.

Diversity matters because of **resistance** (staying put through a disturbance) and **resilience** (returning quickly afterwards). A community in which several species perform each role has replacements when one is lost; a monoculture does not. The same logic applies within species: Ireland grew one potato variety, blight found no resistance, over a million people died. Diversity also delivers **ecosystem services**: honey-bee pollination is worth about $1.6 billion a year to US farming, wetlands purify water, predators and parasites suppress most crop pests. And it is unevenly distributed: richness peaks in the tropics (older ecosystems, more energy, more niches, stable climate), and 34 **hotspots** covering 2.3% of the land hold 42% of terrestrial vertebrates and half of all plants, most of them **endemic**, found nowhere else.

## Disruptions

The CED groups disruptions into invasive species, human activities, geological and meteorological events, and climate change. Each changes the environment faster than populations can adapt; the species that persist are those whose existing variation happens to suit the new conditions, and the vacated niches invite **adaptive radiation** afterwards.

**Invasive species** arrive without the predators, parasites and competitors that limited them at home, often reproduce fast, and meet natives with no evolved defences. **Kudzu**, planted for soil conservation in 1876, grows a foot a day and covers 7 million acres. The **brown tree snake**, carried to Guam in aircraft about 1950, eliminated three bird species and several reptiles from an island whose fauna had never met a snake. **Nile perch**, introduced to Lake Victoria in the 1960s, drove about 200 of its 500 endemic cichlid species extinct and, by removing algae-grazers, helped fill the lake with blooms and anoxic water. **Asian carp** are 95% of the fish biomass in parts of the Illinois River. Diseases travel too: the **chytrid fungus**, spread with African clawed frogs used in laboratories, is a main cause of the global amphibian decline; the fungus of **white-nose syndrome**, from Europe, has killed millions of North American bats.

**Human activities** head the list of causes of extinction. **Habitat loss** is the largest: Sumatra and Borneo have lost about half their forest to timber and palm oil; 2.4% of humid tropical forest went in 2000–2005; 91% of US rivers have been altered. **Overharvesting** collapsed the Atlantic cod fishery within a decade of factory trawlers arriving in the 1980s, a textbook tragedy of the commons; whales, sharks and bushmeat primates are similarly exposed. **Pollution** acts two ways you already know: persistent toxins biomagnify up food chains, and nutrient runoff drives eutrophication and dead zones.

**Climate change** is the human disruption with the longest reach. Greenhouse gases let sunlight in and trap the infrared the warmed surface radiates. Antarctic ice cores show CO₂ cycling between 180 and 300 ppm over 400,000 years, each rise taking tens of thousands of years and temperature tracking it; since 1750 CO₂ has climbed from about 280 to over 400 ppm, faster and higher than anything in the record. Effects are measurable: Glacier National Park had about 150 glaciers in 1850 and 24 larger than 25 acres by 2010; ice sheets lose 150–250 km³ a year; sea level rises about 3 mm a year; 385 British plant species flower 4.5 days earlier than 40 years ago; European birds have shifted their ranges 91 km north. Timing shifts create **phenological mismatch** between flowers and pollinators or chicks and insect peaks; mountaintop and polar species run out of habitat to move into; grizzly bears now meet polar bears.

**Geological and meteorological events** disrupt without any human help. **Volcanic eruptions** bury habitat and cool the climate with ash and sulfate; the 1783 Icelandic eruptions produced a record-cold European winter. The **K-Pg meteor impact** 66 million years ago removed about 75% of species, including non-avian dinosaurs; a worldwide iridium layer and the Chicxulub crater are the evidence, and mammals radiated into the emptied niches. **Continental drift** separates lineages and makes new climates over millions of years. **El Niño**, every few years, weakens the Pacific trade winds, lets warm water block the nutrient upwelling off Peru, and collapses phytoplankton, anchovies, seabirds and sea lions while shifting rain and drought around the globe. Even fire is a disruption some communities depend on: savannas, chaparral and prairies are maintained by it, and suppressing it is itself a disturbance.

## Worked example 1: comparing two meadows with Simpson's index

| Species | Managed plot | Unmanaged plot |
|---|---|---|
| Grasshopper | 80 | 25 |
| Beetle | 10 | 20 |
| Butterfly | 5 | 20 |
| Ant | 5 | 20 |
| Spider | 0 | 15 |
| N | 100 | 100 |

*Managed:* shares 0.80, 0.10, 0.05, 0.05, 0; squares 0.64, 0.01, 0.0025, 0.0025, 0; sum 0.655; index **0.35**.

*Unmanaged:* shares 0.25, 0.20, 0.20, 0.20, 0.15; squares 0.0625, 0.04, 0.04, 0.04, 0.0225; sum 0.205; index **0.80**.

Both plots have 100 individuals and nearly the same richness, but the managed plot's grasshopper alone contributes 0.64 to the sum. *Prediction:* a grasshopper disease removes 80% of the managed community and 25% of the unmanaged one; the unmanaged plot is more resistant and more resilient. *Mechanism of the difference:* fertiliser favoured a few dominant grasses and mowing removed flowers, so the insects that depend on diverse plants declined.

## Worked example 2: reading the CO₂ record

Two data sets on one axis: ice-core CO₂ oscillating between 180 and 300 ppm with a period of about 100,000 years, and direct measurements climbing from 315 ppm in 1958 to over 400 ppm now. *Is the modern rise the next natural cycle?* No: natural rises of 100 ppm took about 50,000 years and never passed 300 ppm; this one took under 300 years and has passed it. *What does the ice record say about CO₂ and temperature?* They track each other through every cycle, evidence of a link. *What identifies the cause?* A change unprecedented in rate and level coinciding with fossil-fuel combustion, which moves carbon from a reservoir buried for millions of years into the air. Read both time scales before answering.

## Worked example 3: tracing an invasion through a food web

Nile perch, a large predator, enters Lake Victoria; algae-grazing cichlids are eaten; algae bloom; the bloom dies and bacteria decomposing it strip oxygen from deep water; more fish die. *Classify:* invasive species. *Why it exploded:* no predator or competitor for a big piscivore, naive and abundant prey, so growth ran near rmax. *Confound:* agricultural runoff rose over the same decades and also feeds blooms; to separate them, compare bays with runoff but few perch against the reverse, or check the timing of blooms against the perch explosion. *Effect on the fish diversity index:* richness fell by 200 species and the perch dominates biomass, so Σ (n/N)² rises and the index falls.

## Common misconceptions

- **"More species always means more diverse."** Richness is one input; eight species with one at 90% score below five species at 20% each. Calculate.
- **"Simpson's index is the sum of the squares."** The index is one minus the sum. A sum of 0.655 is an index of 0.345.
- **"The ocean is acidic."** Surface pH has fallen from 8.2 to 8.1; that is still basic, but it is a 26% rise in hydrogen ion concentration, enough to affect calcification.
- **"CO₂ has been high before, so this is natural."** The level is above the 400,000-year range and the rate is a hundred times faster.
- **"El Niño is just warm water."** It is a collapse of upwelling, hence of nutrients, hence of productivity at the base of an entire food web.
- **"Invasive species are stronger organisms."** They are released from their enemies; at home the same species is held at K like any other.
- **"Mass extinction is the end of the story."** The fossil record pairs each one with adaptive radiation into the emptied niches.
