## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Metabolism | All of a cell's reactions: anabolic (build, use energy) and catabolic (break down, release it). |
| First law of thermodynamics | Energy is neither created nor destroyed, only transferred or transformed. |
| Second law of thermodynamics | Every transfer loses some energy as heat; the entropy (disorder) of the universe increases. |
| Free energy (G) | The energy in a system available to do work. ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. |
| Exergonic / endergonic | ΔG negative, energy released, spontaneous / ΔG positive, energy required, non-spontaneous. |
| ATP | Adenosine triphosphate: adenine, ribose and three phosphates. Hydrolysis to ADP + Pi has ΔG ≈ −7.3 kcal/mol (about −14 in a cell). |
| Energy coupling | Driving an endergonic reaction with an exergonic one, usually by phosphorylation from ATP. |
| Phosphorylation | Transfer of a phosphate group onto a molecule, making it more reactive or changing its shape. |
| Substrate-level phosphorylation | An enzyme moves a phosphate from an intermediate directly onto ADP. |
| Oxidative phosphorylation | ATP synthase makes ATP from a proton gradient built by an electron transport chain. |
| Oxidation / reduction | Loss of electrons / gain of electrons; always paired (redox). |
| NAD⁺, FAD, NADP⁺ | Electron-carrier coenzymes; reduced forms are NADH, FADH₂, NADPH. |
| Photoautotroph | An organism that makes organic molecules from CO₂ using light. |
| Thylakoid, granum, stroma, lumen | Membrane sac; stack of sacs; fluid around them; space inside them. |
| Pigment | A molecule that absorbs particular wavelengths; chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, carotenoids. |
| Photosystem | Pigments and proteins in the thylakoid membrane around a reaction-centre chlorophyll a pair (P680, P700). |
| Chemiosmosis | ATP synthesis driven by protons flowing down their gradient through ATP synthase. |

## Energy, the two laws and free energy

Light becomes chemical energy in a leaf; chemical energy becomes movement, gradients and heat in an animal. The **first law** says none of this creates or destroys energy. The **second law** says every transformation leaks some energy as heat that cannot be put back to work, and the **entropy** of the universe rises. A cell can stay highly ordered because it is an **open system**: it takes in free energy (light or food) and exports heat and waste, raising the entropy outside by more than it lowers it within. Cut off the supply and the order decays; a cell at chemical equilibrium is a dead cell.

**Free energy** is the part of a system's energy available for work. For a reaction, ΔG = G(products) − G(reactants). If ΔG is negative the reaction is **exergonic**: it releases free energy and is spontaneous. Hydrolysis, the oxidation of glucose and diffusion down a gradient are exergonic. If ΔG is positive the reaction is **endergonic** and needs an input: dehydration synthesis, photosynthesis, pumping against a gradient. Spontaneous does not mean fast, and an enzyme never changes ΔG. In ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, a reaction that releases heat and increases disorder is doubly favoured.

## ATP and coupling

**ATP** is a nucleotide, adenine plus ribose with a tail of three phosphates whose crowded negative charges repel. Hydrolysis of the terminal phosphate (ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pi) relieves the strain and yields more stable products, releasing about 7.3 kcal/mol under standard conditions and roughly twice that in a cell. The energy is not "in the bond": breaking any bond costs energy; what matters is that the products are much more stable than the reactants.

Cells **couple** ATP hydrolysis to endergonic reactions rather than letting the energy out as heat. The usual route is **phosphorylation**: an enzyme transfers ATP's terminal phosphate onto a reactant, making a more reactive intermediate, or onto a protein, which changes shape. In the sodium-potassium pump, one ATP phosphorylates the pump, it changes conformation, three Na⁺ leave and two K⁺ enter, both uphill, and the phosphate is released to reset the cycle. The summed ΔG of the whole process is negative.

ATP is a currency, spent and remade constantly; the reserves are glycogen and fat. It is regenerated by **substrate-level phosphorylation** (a phosphate handed from an intermediate straight to ADP) and **oxidative phosphorylation** (ATP synthase driven by a proton gradient; the chloroplast's version is photophosphorylation). Because glycolysis, ATP and chemiosmosis are shared by nearly all living things, they are evidence of common ancestry.

## Redox and the carriers

Catabolism is controlled oxidation. **Oxidation** is loss of electrons and **reduction** is gain; they always happen together. Electrons usually travel with hydrogen atoms, so a molecule that loses hydrogen is being oxidised. Fuels such as glucose are rich in C–H bonds, that is, in electrons at high potential energy; fat, with more C–H bonds, stores more per gram. Dehydrogenases strip those electrons onto carriers: NAD⁺ becomes NADH, FAD becomes FADH₂, and in photosynthesis NADP⁺ becomes NADPH. The carriers deliver electrons to electron transport chains, which release the energy in steps small enough to be captured as a proton gradient rather than lost as heat.

## Photosynthesis: the overview

Photoautotrophs build organic molecules from CO₂ with light energy; heterotrophs eat them. The equation, 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂, hides two facts: the carbon in sugar comes from CO₂, and the oxygen gas comes from water, as ¹⁸O tracer experiments proved.

The chloroplast has three compartments that matter: the **thylakoid** membranes (stacked into **grana**), the **lumen** inside each thylakoid, and the **stroma** around them. The **light-dependent reactions** in the thylakoid membrane convert light into ATP and NADPH, releasing O₂. The **Calvin cycle** (next week) in the stroma uses that ATP and NADPH to reduce CO₂ to sugar, returning ADP and NADP⁺; remove CO₂ and the light reactions stall for lack of ADP and NADP⁺.

## Pigments and light

Photon energy rises as wavelength falls: violet (about 380 nm) carries more energy per photon than red (about 750 nm). A **pigment** absorbs only wavelengths that match its electronic structure. Chlorophyll a absorbs blue-violet and red and reflects green. Chlorophyll b and the carotenoids absorb other bands and pass the energy to chlorophyll a; carotenoids also dissipate excess energy as heat. An **absorption spectrum** is a purified pigment's absorbance at each wavelength; an **action spectrum** is a whole organism's rate of photosynthesis at each wavelength. The action spectrum is broader, because several pigments contribute, and its peaks roughly match chlorophyll's.

## The light-dependent reactions

The thylakoid membrane holds two **photosystems**, each an antenna of pigments funnelling energy to a reaction-centre pair of chlorophyll a: **P680** in photosystem II and **P700** in photosystem I. The numbers record the order of discovery; electrons start at photosystem II.

When P680 absorbs energy, an electron is boosted and handed to a primary acceptor. P680⁺, left behind, is strong enough to pull electrons from water: 2 H₂O → 4 e⁻ + 4 H⁺ + O₂. The protons stay in the lumen; the oxygen leaves as gas. The energised electron falls through plastoquinone, the cytochrome complex (which uses the energy to pump protons from the stroma into the lumen) and plastocyanin to photosystem I, where light at P700 boosts it again. It then passes to ferredoxin and NADP⁺ reductase, which reduces NADP⁺ to **NADPH**.

Meanwhile the lumen has become acidic relative to the stroma, from water splitting, cytochrome-complex pumping and the protons used when NADP⁺ is reduced. Protons flow back into the stroma through **ATP synthase**, whose rotation phosphorylates ADP. This is **chemiosmosis**, the mechanism a mitochondrion uses with the gradient turned the other way. ATP and NADPH are released into the stroma, where the Calvin cycle needs them.

## Worked example 1: a coupled-reaction budget

Making glutamine from glutamate and ammonia has ΔG = +3.4 kcal/mol. ATP hydrolysis has ΔG = −7.3 kcal/mol.

*Question:* Can the cell make glutamine, and how?

*Reasoning:* On its own, no: ΔG is positive. Coupled, yes: glutamine synthetase transfers a phosphate from ATP to glutamate, making an unstable glutamyl phosphate that then reacts with ammonia. Summed, ΔG = +3.4 + (−7.3) = −3.9 kcal/mol, so the overall process is exergonic. The enzyme organised the coupling; it did not change either ΔG.

## Worked example 2: absorption versus action spectrum

A purified chlorophyll a solution absorbs at 430 and 660 nm and almost nothing at 550 nm. A spinach leaf's rate of O₂ release is high at 430 and 660, and about a third as high at 550.

*Question:* Why is the leaf's rate at 550 nm not zero?

*Reasoning:* The leaf is not a chlorophyll a solution. Accessory pigments absorb toward the green-yellow region and pass the energy to chlorophyll a, so the action spectrum is broader than any single pigment's absorption. The matching main peaks show chlorophyll absorption drives photosynthesis; the mismatch in the middle is the evidence for accessory pigments.

## Worked example 3: the floating leaf disk ET50

In AP Investigation 5, ten disks in bicarbonate under a lamp floated as follows: 0, 0, 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 10 at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14 minutes.

*Question:* What are the ET50 and the rate index?

*Reasoning:* Half the disks (5) float at 8 minutes, so ET50 = 8 minutes. The rate index is 1/ET50 = 1/8 = 0.125 min⁻¹. The reciprocal makes a faster rate give a larger number. A green-light cup reaching 5 disks at 20 minutes has an index of 0.05 min⁻¹, 40% of the first; a cup that never reaches 5 is reported as below the others, not given an invented ET50.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Breaking ATP's high-energy bond releases energy."** Breaking a bond costs energy; the release comes from the more stable products.
- **"Plants create energy."** They transform light energy into chemical energy, losing some as heat.
- **"Spontaneous means fast."** It means ΔG is negative. Speed is activation energy's business.
- **"Oxygen from photosynthesis comes from CO₂."** It comes from water (¹⁸O experiments).
- **"Photosystem I comes first."** Electrons start at photosystem II. The numbers are historical.
- **"Green light works best because leaves are green."** Green is what leaves reject.
- **"The light reactions make sugar."** They make ATP, NADPH and O₂. Sugar is the Calvin cycle's product.
