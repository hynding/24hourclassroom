## What you need to be able to do

The College Board phrases the learning objectives for this week as:

- **8.1** Explain how the behavioural and physiological response of an organism is related to changes in internal or external environment, and explain how the behavioural responses of organisms affect their overall fitness and contribute to the success of the population.
- **8.2** Describe the strategies organisms use to acquire and use energy, and explain how changes in energy availability affect populations and ecosystems.

In plain language: given a description of what an animal or plant does, you must say whether the behaviour is innate or learned, what cue triggers it, and why it raised fitness. Given a choice-chamber data set, you must run a chi-square test and interpret it. Given an energy pyramid, you must calculate what fraction passed between levels, explain where the rest went, and say why the pyramid has only four or five steps.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Innate behaviour | Genetically programmed; performed correctly on first exposure without experience. Reliable but rigid. |
| Learned behaviour | Shaped by experience; flexible, but can be learned wrongly or too late. |
| Proximate / ultimate cause | The immediate mechanism (hormone, cue, circuit) versus the evolutionary reason the behaviour increased fitness. |
| Kinesis | Undirected movement whose rate changes with stimulus intensity. Woodlice move more in dry air and so accumulate in damp places. |
| Taxis | Directed movement toward (positive) or away from (negative) a stimulus: phototaxis, chemotaxis, geotaxis. |
| Fixed action pattern | A stereotyped sequence released by a sign stimulus and run to completion. The stickleback attacks anything with a red underside. |
| Habituation | Learned loss of response to a repeated, harmless stimulus. |
| Imprinting | Rapid learning in a critical period; ducklings follow the first large moving object. |
| Classical conditioning | A neutral stimulus paired with an unconditioned stimulus comes to produce the response on its own (Pavlov). |
| Operant conditioning | A behaviour is strengthened or weakened by its consequences (Skinner box). |
| Pheromone | A chemical signal between members of one species: mate attraction, alarm, trail marking. |
| Altruism | Behaviour that lowers the actor's fitness and raises another's. Explained by kin selection or reciprocity. |
| Inclusive fitness | One's own reproduction plus the reproduction of relatives, weighted by relatedness. |
| Gross primary productivity (GPP) | Rate at which producers capture energy by photosynthesis or chemosynthesis. |
| Net primary productivity (NPP) | GPP minus the producers' own respiration; the energy available to everyone else. |
| Trophic level | A feeding position: producer, primary consumer, secondary consumer, tertiary consumer. |
| Trophic level transfer efficiency | Production at one level divided by production at the level below, as a percentage. Usually 5–20%. |
| Net production efficiency | Net consumer productivity divided by assimilation; ectotherms about ten times endotherms. |
| Biomagnification | Rising concentration of a persistent toxin at each trophic level. |

## Behaviour is a phenotype with a cue and a pay-off

Organisms live in environments that change, and every response to change, whether a leaf turning toward light, a frog burrowing before winter or a bee dancing on a comb, is a trait that natural selection has shaped. The CED wants two questions asked of every behaviour. The **proximate** question: what triggers it and how does the body carry it out? The **ultimate** question: why did individuals who did this leave more offspring?

**Innate behaviours** are run by inherited circuitry and work the first time. The simplest are movements. A **kinesis** has no direction: a woodlouse in dry air simply walks faster and turns less, so it leaves dry places quickly and lingers in damp ones, and the population ends up where it is safest without any individual knowing where that is. A **taxis** is oriented: a moth flies toward light, a *Tetrahymena* swims up a chemical gradient. A **fixed action pattern** is a whole sequence released by a single **sign stimulus**: a male three-spined stickleback with a red breeding belly attacks any object with a red underside, including a crude wooden block, and ignores a realistic model without red. Plants respond too, without a nervous system: a shoot bends toward light because auxin accumulates on the shaded side (phototropism), and many plants time flowering by the length of the night, measured by the pigment phytochrome (photoperiodism). The pay-off of innate responses is reliability; the cost is that they cannot adjust if the cue stops predicting what it used to.

**Learned behaviours** trade that reliability for flexibility. **Habituation** switches off a response to a stimulus that proves harmless: prairie dogs stop alarm-calling at a familiar person's footsteps but still call at a hawk. **Imprinting** is learning locked to a critical period: a hatchling fixes on the first large moving thing as its parent, which is why conservationists raising whooping cranes wear full crane costumes. **Classical conditioning** (Pavlov's dogs salivating at a bell) pairs a neutral cue with a meaningful one; **operant conditioning** (Skinner's rats pressing a lever) adjusts a behaviour by its consequences. **Cognitive learning** manipulates information without trial and error: Kohler's chimpanzees stacked boxes to reach bananas as though they had pictured the solution first.

**Signals** are behaviours aimed at other organisms. A **pheromone** is a chemical released by one individual that alters the behaviour of another of the same species: moth sex attractants, ant trails, alarm scents. Birds and whales sing; crickets chirp; fireflies flash; a returning honeybee forager performs the **waggle dance**, whose angle encodes direction relative to the sun and whose duration encodes distance. For a signal to matter it has to be *received*: communication is a signal plus a response, and selection acts on both ends. Inside the receiver, the signal is handled exactly as in Unit 4: a ligand, a receptor, a transduction cascade, a response.

**Cooperation** raises fitness when being in a group makes each member more likely to survive and reproduce: pack hunting, mobbing a predator, schooling. **Altruism** is harder, because it lowers the actor's fitness by definition. **Kin selection** resolves most cases: helping relatives passes on shared genes, so sterile worker bees rearing a queen's offspring have high **inclusive fitness** although they never breed. **Reciprocal altruism** between non-relatives (monkeys grooming each other) needs repeated encounters and a way to punish cheats.

## AP Investigation 12: the choice chamber and the chi-square

The behaviour lab puts animals (usually pill bugs) into a two-chamber arena that differs in one condition, counts where they are at intervals, and tests the result. The **null hypothesis** is always "no preference": equal numbers on each side. The statistic is the one from the AP formula sheet, **χ² = Σ (o − e)² / e**, with degrees of freedom = number of chambers − 1. For two chambers df = 1 and the critical value at p = 0.05 is **3.84**; for three categories df = 2 and the critical value is 5.99.

## Energy flow: autotrophs capture, everyone else loses

An ecosystem is a community plus its abiotic environment, and energy enters it once. **Photoautotrophs** (plants, algae, cyanobacteria) capture light; **chemoautotrophs** (vent bacteria oxidising hydrogen sulfide) capture chemical energy from inorganic molecules. Either way, the autotrophs' **gross primary productivity** is the rate at which energy is fixed, and their **net primary productivity** is what remains after they have respired what they need to stay alive. Only NPP can be eaten.

From there energy climbs a **food chain** or, more realistically, spreads through a **food web**, from producers to **primary consumers** (herbivores) to **secondary** and **tertiary consumers**. At every step most of the energy is lost: respired as heat, never eaten, or eaten but egested. The **trophic level transfer efficiency** is typically 5–20%, which the **10% rule** rounds to a tenth. Ten thousand kilocalories of plant become about a thousand of herbivore, a hundred of carnivore, ten of top carnivore; after four to six transfers there is not enough to support another population, which is why food chains are short. Decomposers feed on dead material from every level and respire it; they recycle **matter**, never energy.

How much of the energy an animal assimilates becomes new tissue is its **net production efficiency**, and here physiology matters: an **ectotherm** caterpillar banks about 18% of what it assimilates, an **endotherm** squirrel about 1.6%, because the squirrel burns the rest keeping warm. Small endotherms are the extreme case: a shrew's surface-area-to-volume ratio sheds heat so fast that each gram of shrew respires many times faster than a gram of horse. That is why **metabolic rate per unit mass rises as body size falls**, a Unit 2 idea paying off in Unit 8.

Finally, what cannot be respired away accumulates. A persistent, fat-soluble toxin such as DDT or a PCB is passed intact from prey to predator, so its concentration rises with trophic level: **biomagnification**. Walleye in Saginaw Bay carried more than four times the PCB concentration of the phytoplankton, and fish-eating birds an order of magnitude more than the fish.

## Worked example 1: a choice-chamber chi-square

Forty pill bugs are released into a damp/dry chamber; after ten minutes, 31 are on the damp side and 9 on the dry side.

*Null hypothesis:* no preference, so expected = 20 and 20.

*Calculation:* damp: (31 − 20)² / 20 = 121 / 20 = 6.05. Dry: (9 − 20)² / 20 = 6.05. χ² = 12.1.

*Decision:* df = 1, critical value 3.84. 12.1 is larger, so reject the null hypothesis: a split this uneven would occur by chance less than 5% of the time. The pill bugs show a preference for moisture. Note that both chambers contribute a term even though the deviations are the same size, and that the expected values came from the hypothesis, not from the data.

## Worked example 2: reading the Silver Springs energy pyramid

| Trophic level | kcal/m²/yr |
|---|---|
| Producers (GPP) | 20,810 |
| Primary consumers | 3,368 |
| Secondary consumers | 383 |
| Tertiary consumers | 21 |
| Decomposers | 5,060 |

*What fraction passed from primary to secondary consumers?* 383 ÷ 3,368 = 0.114, about 11%. *From secondary to tertiary?* 21 ÷ 383 = 0.055, about 5.5%. Neither is exactly 10%; the rule is an estimate. *Why no quaternary consumer?* Another 5–10% of 21 kcal is 1–2 kcal/m²/yr, too little to feed a population. *Why do decomposers get more than the top two consumer levels combined?* They take dead material and waste from every level, including the enormous producer level.

## Worked example 3: the upside-down pyramid

In the English Channel, phytoplankton standing biomass is about 4 g/m² and zooplankton biomass about 21 g/m². Students often conclude the data are wrong. They are not: a biomass pyramid is a snapshot of what is present now, while an energy pyramid is a rate over a year. Phytoplankton divide and are eaten so quickly that their standing crop stays small even though their annual production is far larger than the zooplankton's. Energy pyramids are always upright; biomass and numbers pyramids can invert. Ask of every figure: is this a rate or a snapshot?

## Common misconceptions

- **"If the animals all end up in one chamber, they must have sensed where it was."** Not necessarily. A kinesis gets a population to the right place with no sense of direction at all.
- **"Learned behaviour has no genetic basis."** The capacity to learn, the critical period for imprinting and what can be learned are all inherited; only the content is acquired.
- **"Altruism evolves for the good of the species."** Selection acts on individuals. Altruism persists because it raises inclusive fitness (kin) or is repaid (reciprocity).
- **"Decomposers recycle energy."** They recycle matter. The energy in a dead leaf is respired to heat exactly as in a living consumer.
- **"Exactly 10% passes at each level."** Real efficiencies run 5–20%. Calculate from the data when numbers are given.
- **"A bigger animal has a higher metabolic rate per gram."** Total metabolic rate rises with size; rate per gram falls.
- **"A chi-square above the critical value supports the null hypothesis."** Above the critical value you reject the null.
