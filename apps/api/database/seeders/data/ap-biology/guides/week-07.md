## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Incipient plasmolysis | The point at which the membrane just leaves the wall; Ψp = 0, so Ψcell = Ψs(cell) = Ψsolution. |
| Zero crossing | The solute molarity at which tissue mass does not change; the isotonic point, used to find tissue Ψ. |
| Percentage change in mass | (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100; normalises samples of different size. |
| Marker | A molecule or structure that identifies an organelle in a cell fraction: acid hydrolases for lysosomes, cristae and oxygen use for mitochondria. |
| Topology | The sidedness of membranes: the lumen of the ER, Golgi and vesicles is equivalent to the outside of the cell. |
| Saturation kinetics | A rate that plateaus with concentration; the sign of a limited number of binding sites. |
| Homeoviscous adaptation | Adjusting membrane lipid composition to keep fluidity constant across temperatures. |
| Colligative | Depending on the number of dissolved particles, not their identity; osmotic effects are colligative. |

## The unit as one argument

Unit 2 can be told as a single story, and it is worth being able to tell it in two minutes.

A cell is a volume of chemistry that must be kept different from its surroundings. The boundary is a **phospholipid bilayer**, which self-assembles because water excludes hydrocarbon tails (Unit 1). That hydrophobic core is the gatekeeper: small nonpolar molecules dissolve through it, polar molecules struggle, ions cannot cross at all. Everything else enters or leaves through **proteins**, and by choosing its proteins the cell chooses its traffic. **Channels** and **carriers** let chosen substances move down their gradients at no cost; **pumps** spend ATP to move substances up their gradients and so build the differences, ionic and electrical, that make the cell alive. Secondary transporters spend those gradients to move still other things uphill. Water follows solutes by **osmosis**, and because water cannot be pumped the cell manages water by managing solutes and, in plants, by letting a wall turn water entry into pressure. **Vesicles** carry what is too large for any protein, at a cost in ATP.

Exchange happens at the surface and need arises throughout the volume, so a cell that grows runs out of surface: **SA:V** falls as size rises. Cells stay small, flatten, grow projections, or fold membranes inward, and the folding is what organelles are. **Compartments** add surface and keep incompatible chemistry apart: acid in the lysosome, oxidation in the peroxisome, gradients across the inner mitochondrial membrane. Two of those compartments, the mitochondrion and the chloroplast, carry double membranes, their own circular DNA and bacterial ribosomes, and divide on their own, because they were once **free-living bacteria**. The eukaryotic cell is a partnership with a history, and the history is readable in its membranes.

## Transport mechanisms side by side

| Mechanism | Protein? | ATP? | Direction | Saturates? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Simple diffusion | no | no | down gradient | no | O₂, CO₂, steroids |
| Facilitated diffusion, channel | yes | no | down gradient | at very high rates | K⁺ leak, aquaporin |
| Facilitated diffusion, carrier | yes | no | down gradient | yes | glucose into red cells |
| Osmosis | optional (aquaporin) | no | toward lower Ψ | no | water everywhere |
| Primary active transport | yes (pump) | yes, directly | up gradient | yes | Na⁺/K⁺ pump, proton pump |
| Secondary active transport | yes (cotransporter) | yes, indirectly | up gradient for one substance | yes | Na⁺-glucose symporter |
| Endocytosis / exocytosis | yes (clathrin, fusion proteins) | yes | independent of gradient | no | LDL uptake, hormone release |

Two experiments sort the rows. Poison ATP synthesis: the first four continue until equilibrium, the last three stop. Plot rate against concentration: simple diffusion is a straight line, everything through a protein bends toward a plateau.

## Investigation 4 end to end

**Procedure 1, agar cubes.** Cubes of indicator-loaded agar in NaOH model cells of different size. The colour front moves the same depth into each cube in a given time, so the fraction of volume reached falls as cube size rises. Shows that SA:V, not surface area, limits supply by diffusion.

**Procedure 2, dialysis bags.** Bags permeable to water but not sucrose, filled with sucrose and placed in water, gain mass in proportion to their molarity; filled with water and placed in sucrose, they lose it. A graph of percentage change in mass against molarity is a straight line, which can identify an unknown solution. Shows that water moves toward the lower Ψ and faster the steeper the gradient.

**Procedure 3, potato cores.** Cores in a series of sucrose concentrations gain or lose mass. Plot percentage change against molarity, draw the best-fit line and read the molarity at zero change; that solution is isotonic to the tissue. Convert with Ψs = −iCRT at the lab temperature; because the beaker is open, Ψp = 0 and the tissue's water potential equals that Ψs.

Use percentage change, not grams, so cores of different initial mass can be compared. Blot before weighing; soak long enough to approach equilibrium; keep core size the same so SA:V does not vary; cover beakers so evaporation does not concentrate the solutions.

## Worked example 1: incipient plasmolysis

Onion epidermis in NaCl at 25 °C: 2% of cells plasmolysed in 0.10 M, 52% in 0.20 M, 98% in 0.30 M.

*Reasoning:* The 50% point, about 0.20 M, is where the typical cell has just lost turgor, so Ψp = 0 and the cell's Ψ equals its own Ψs and equals the solution's. Ψsolution = −(2)(0.20)(0.0831)(298) = −9.9 bar; that is the typical cell's Ψs. If the experiment were repeated with sucrose (i = 1), about 0.40 M would be needed for the same effect, because each molarity of sucrose gives half the particles.

## Worked example 2: a two-tissue comparison

White potato cores show zero mass change at 0.33 M sucrose; sweet potato cores at 0.49 M, both at 20 °C (293 K).

*Reasoning:* Ψwhite = −(1)(0.33)(0.0831)(293) = −8.0 bar. Ψsweet = −(1)(0.49)(0.0831)(293) = −11.9 bar. The sweet potato's water potential is lower, which at equilibrium in an open beaker means its Ψs is lower, which means its cells hold more dissolved solute, as its name suggests. The wall has nothing to do with where the zero crossing falls; it only sets how much pressure builds in dilute solutions. Claims about "stronger walls" are tested by measuring sap sugar or by plasmolysis, not by this graph.

## Worked example 3: reading a fractionation table

Liver cells were broken and spun; pellets were tested for markers. Fraction 2 had circular DNA, consumed oxygen and showed cristae; fraction 3 had hydrolases active at pH 5; fraction 4 had membrane-bound ribosomes.

*Reasoning:* Fraction 2 is mitochondria (three agreeing markers, two of them endosymbiotic signatures). Fraction 3 is lysosomes, and the pH optimum of its enzymes is direct evidence that the cell keeps that compartment acidic. Fraction 4 is rough ER, with the cell's own ribosomes on its surface and no genome of its own, so the ER is not a former bacterium. Identify organelles by what they do; use more than one marker; notice which markers discriminate between hypotheses.

## Common misconceptions that cost the most points

- **Forgetting i.** NaCl is two particles; its Ψs is twice as negative as the same molarity of sucrose.
- **Using °C in −iCRT.** T must be in kelvin.
- **Comparing Ψs instead of total Ψ.** Add Ψp first. A turgid cell can gain water from a solution with a higher Ψs than its own.
- **A positive water potential for living tissue.** Tissue is always below zero in an open beaker.
- **Calling facilitated diffusion active.** A protein is not energy.
- **Calling endocytosis passive.** Vesicles cost ATP.
- **Describing data without numbers.** Direction plus two quoted values earns the point; "it increased" does not.
- **Treating a membrane as evidence of endosymbiosis.** Every organelle has one. DNA, ribosomes and fission are the evidence.
