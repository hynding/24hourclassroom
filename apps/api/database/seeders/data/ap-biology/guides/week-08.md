## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Activation energy (Eₐ) | The energy needed to push reactants into the unstable transition state. Every reaction has one, even an exergonic one. |
| Transition state | The strained, high-energy arrangement of atoms partway between reactants and products. |
| Catalyst | A substance that speeds a reaction by lowering Eₐ and is unchanged at the end. An enzyme is a biological catalyst. |
| Active site | The pocket in an enzyme where substrate binds; its R groups give it a specific shape and chemistry. |
| Substrate | The reactant an enzyme acts on. |
| Enzyme-substrate complex | The brief union of enzyme and substrate during catalysis. |
| Induced fit | Binding causes both enzyme and substrate to shift shape, tightening the fit and straining the substrate's bonds. |
| Cofactor | An inorganic ion (Mg²⁺, Zn²⁺, Fe²⁺) an enzyme needs to function. |
| Coenzyme | An organic helper molecule, often built from a vitamin (NAD⁺ from niacin, coenzyme A from pantothenic acid). |
| Saturation | Every active site continuously occupied; rate cannot rise with more substrate. |
| Denaturation | Loss of tertiary structure under heat, pH or salt; the active site deforms and activity is lost. |
| Competitive inhibitor | A molecule that resembles the substrate and blocks the active site; overcome by extra substrate. |
| Noncompetitive inhibitor | A molecule that binds elsewhere and changes the enzyme's shape; not overcome by extra substrate. |
| Allosteric site | A regulatory site away from the active site; a molecule binding there switches the enzyme's shape and activity. |
| Feedback inhibition | The end product of a pathway allosterically inhibits an enzyme early in the pathway. |
| Initial rate | The rate measured at the start of a run, before substrate depletion or product accumulation changes it. |

## What an enzyme does, and what it does not do

Even an exergonic reaction, one whose products hold less free energy than its reactants, cannot start until the reactants have been contorted into the transition state, and that costs energy: the **activation energy**. Sugar does not burn on the shelf because at room temperature only a tiny fraction of molecules carry enough energy to clear the barrier.

An **enzyme** lowers that barrier. It binds the substrate in its **active site**, holds it, and makes the transition state easier to reach, so a far larger share of collisions succeed. Four mechanisms, often together, do the work: the active site orients two substrates so the right atoms meet; its R groups create a local environment (an acidic pocket, a hydrophobic cleft) that favours the reaction; binding bends or stretches the substrate toward the transition state; and some enzymes briefly form a covalent bond with the substrate as an intermediate step.

What the enzyme does *not* do matters as much on the exam. It does not change ΔG, which is set by the reactants and products themselves: on an energy diagram it lowers the peak and leaves the start and finish where they were. It cannot make an endergonic reaction go on its own; that needs coupling to ATP, next week's topic. And it is not consumed: the product leaves and the same enzyme molecule goes again, thousands of times a second.

## Why an enzyme is specific

Sucrase hydrolyses sucrose and ignores lactose, although both are disaccharides joined by a glycosidic bond. The reason is the active site: a few residues, brought together from different parts of the chain by the fold, make a surface whose shape, polarity and charge complement sucrose and nothing else. This is Unit 1's structure-determines-function rule at its sharpest: sequence sets the fold, the fold sets the active site, the active site sets the substrate. A single substitution lining the site, or even one buried in the core far away, can change what the enzyme binds or whether it works at all.

The old **lock-and-key** picture of a rigid pocket has given way to **induced fit**: the enzyme shifts around the bound substrate, as a glove tightens around a hand, and the closing is part of catalysis.

Many enzymes need help. **Cofactors** are inorganic ions, often held in the active site; **coenzymes** are organic molecules, many derived from vitamins, that carry chemical groups or electrons. NAD⁺, FAD and coenzyme A are coenzymes, so a vitamin deficiency is at bottom an enzyme deficiency. Almost all enzymes are proteins, but not all: ribozymes, including the catalytic core of the ribosome, are RNA.

## The environment: substrate, enzyme, temperature, pH

**Substrate concentration.** With a fixed amount of enzyme, more substrate raises the rate at first, because more collisions find an empty active site. The curve then flattens: at **saturation** every site is always occupied, and only more enzyme would help.

**Enzyme concentration.** With substrate in excess, rate is proportional to enzyme: each added molecule is another working site.

**Temperature.** Warming speeds molecular motion, so collisions are more frequent and more energetic and the rate rises. Above an **optimum** the same motion starts to shake apart the hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds and hydrophobic clustering that hold the tertiary structure; the active site deforms, and the rate collapses. That collapse is **denaturation**. Where it begins depends on how tightly the fold is held, which is why a hot-spring bacterium's enzyme works at temperatures that would cook a human one. The curve's shape, a rise then a cliff, is those two opposite effects of heat added together.

**pH.** Changing H⁺ concentration changes which R groups carry a charge. Carboxyl groups lose their proton and go negative as pH rises; amino groups pick one up and go positive as pH falls. Because ionic and hydrogen bonds among those groups hold tertiary structure, and charged residues in the active site attract the substrate, shifting the pH reshapes the enzyme. Each enzyme has an optimum at the pH of its compartment: pepsin near pH 2 in the stomach, trypsin near pH 8 in the intestine, lysosomal hydrolases near 5. pH is logarithmic: two units from the optimum is a hundredfold change in H⁺.

## Inhibitors and regulation

A **competitive inhibitor** looks enough like the substrate to enter the active site and sit there. It and the substrate compete, so the outcome depends on their relative concentrations: flood the enzyme with substrate and the inhibitor is outcompeted. In data, the inhibited rate lags at low substrate but catches up at high substrate; the maximum is unchanged. Malonate inhibiting succinate dehydrogenase is the textbook case.

A **noncompetitive inhibitor** binds somewhere else, an **allosteric site**, and changes the enzyme's shape so that the active site works poorly or not at all. Extra substrate cannot rescue it, because the inhibitor is not in the substrate's way. In data, the inhibited rate plateaus well below the uninhibited maximum no matter how much substrate is added.

Allosteric binding is also how cells regulate. An **allosteric activator** stabilises the active shape; an **allosteric inhibitor** stabilises the inactive one. The commonest use is **feedback inhibition**: the final product of a pathway binds allosterically to an enzyme near its start and slows it. When product is plentiful, production stops; when it is used up, production resumes, and no gene has to be switched on or off. Isoleucine inhibiting the first enzyme of its own synthesis, and ATP inhibiting phosphofructokinase, are the examples the exam likes. Compartmentalisation, from Unit 2, is the other half of regulation: an organelle concentrates enzyme and substrate, holds its own pH, and separates reactions that would interfere.

## Worked example 1: reading an energy diagram

A diagram shows reactants at 40 kJ/mol, products at 15 kJ/mol, and a peak at 90 kJ/mol without enzyme or 55 kJ/mol with enzyme.

*Questions:* What is ΔG? What is Eₐ with and without the enzyme? Is the reaction exergonic?

*Reasoning:* ΔG = products − reactants = 15 − 40 = −25 kJ/mol, so the reaction is exergonic and spontaneous. It is the same with or without the enzyme. Eₐ uncatalysed = 90 − 40 = 50 kJ/mol; catalysed = 55 − 40 = 15 kJ/mol. The enzyme removed 35 kJ/mol from the barrier and nothing from ΔG. If a question asks what the enzyme changed, the only right answer is the peak.

## Worked example 2: a rate from a product-versus-time table

A peroxidase assay (AP Investigation 13) at 23 °C gave absorbance readings of 0.00, 0.12, 0.24, 0.36, 0.47, 0.56 and 0.63 at 0, 30, 60, 90, 120, 150 and 180 s.

*Question:* What is the initial rate, in absorbance units per minute?

*Reasoning:* The AP formula sheet gives rate = Δy/Δx. Choose the early, straight part of the curve: 0 to 90 s, where the readings rise by an even 0.12 per 30 s. Δy = 0.36 − 0.00 = 0.36; Δx = 90 s = 1.5 min. Rate = 0.36 ÷ 1.5 = 0.24 absorbance units per minute. Two traps: forgetting to convert seconds to minutes, and using the whole run. Between 150 and 180 s the rise is only 0.07 because substrate is running out, not because the enzyme is denaturing; adding fresh substrate would restore the rate.

## Worked example 3: competitive or noncompetitive?

| Substrate (mM) | No inhibitor | With X | With Y |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 25 | 10 | 12 |
| 4 | 60 | 38 | 30 |
| 16 | 88 | 80 | 44 |
| 64 | 96 | 95 | 48 |

*Reasoning:* Look at the bottom row, where substrate is overwhelming. X has caught up (95 against 96): it was outcompeted, so it is competitive. Y has stalled at 48, half the maximum: it has disabled a fraction of the enzyme regardless of substrate, so it is noncompetitive. The top row cannot distinguish them, because both inhibitors depress the rate at low substrate. On a free-response item, quote the numbers.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Enzymes supply energy to a reaction."** They lower the energy needed to start it. The energy released or absorbed, ΔG, is untouched.
- **"Enzymes are used up."** They are released unchanged and reused.
- **"A spontaneous reaction is a fast one."** Spontaneous means ΔG is negative; rusting is spontaneous and slow.
- **"Higher temperature always speeds enzymes up."** Only up to the optimum. Past it, the fold comes apart and the rate falls off a cliff.
- **"A falling rate during a run means the enzyme is denaturing."** Usually substrate is running out.
- **"Low pH breaks the enzyme's peptide bonds."** It changes R-group charges and breaks weak bonds. The primary structure survives, which is why mild denaturation can reverse.
- **"Noncompetitive inhibitors can be overcome with more substrate."** No; they are not in the substrate's way.
