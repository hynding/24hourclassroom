## What you need to be able to do

The College Board phrases the two learning objectives for this week as:

- **1.1** Explain how the properties of water that result from its polarity and hydrogen bonding affect its biological function.
- **1.2** Describe the composition of macromolecules required by living organisms, and explain how elements from the environment are used to build them.

In plain language: you must be able to start from one fact, *the oxygen in a water molecule pulls shared electrons toward itself*, and reason your way to why ice floats, why sweating cools you, why water climbs a tree, and why cells are mostly water. Then you must be able to say which elements living things take from their surroundings and what each is used to build.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Polar covalent bond | A shared pair of electrons pulled closer to the more electronegative atom, giving each end a partial charge (δ⁺ and δ⁻). |
| Electronegativity | How strongly an atom attracts shared electrons. Oxygen and nitrogen are high; hydrogen and carbon are lower. |
| Hydrogen bond | A weak attraction between a δ⁺ hydrogen on one molecule and a δ⁻ oxygen or nitrogen on another. Weak alone, strong in numbers. |
| Cohesion | Water molecules sticking to each other through hydrogen bonds. Produces surface tension. |
| Adhesion | Water molecules sticking to other charged or polar surfaces, such as the walls of a plant's xylem. |
| Capillary action | Water rising in a narrow tube because adhesion to the walls plus cohesion between molecules outpulls gravity. |
| Specific heat | The heat needed to raise 1 g of a substance by 1 °C. Water's is 1 cal/g·°C, about five times that of sand. |
| Heat of vaporization | The heat needed to turn 1 g of liquid into gas. For water, about 586 cal/g, because hydrogen bonds must be broken. |
| Hydrophilic / hydrophobic | "Water-loving" substances (polar, ionic) dissolve in water; "water-fearing" ones (nonpolar, such as oils) do not. |
| Hydration shell | The layer of water molecules that surrounds a dissolved ion, oxygen ends toward cations, hydrogen ends toward anions. |
| pH | −log₁₀ of the hydrogen ion concentration. Pure water is 10⁻⁷ M in H⁺, so pH 7. Each unit is a tenfold change. |
| Buffer | A substance that resists pH change by releasing or absorbing H⁺. Blood's carbonic acid–bicarbonate buffer holds pH near 7.4. |
| Valence electrons | The electrons in the outermost shell; they decide how many bonds an atom forms. |
| Octet rule | Atoms are most stable with eight electrons in the outer shell (two for the first shell). |
| Isotope | Atoms of one element with different numbers of neutrons. Carbon-14 is radioactive and decays with a half-life of about 5,730 years. |
| Functional group | A small cluster of atoms (hydroxyl, carboxyl, amino, phosphate, methyl, carbonyl, sulfhydryl) that gives a molecule its chemical behaviour. |

## The one idea everything hangs on

A water molecule is bent, with the oxygen at the apex. Oxygen is far more electronegative than hydrogen, so the two shared electron pairs spend more time near the oxygen. That leaves the oxygen δ⁻ and each hydrogen δ⁺. A δ⁺ hydrogen on one molecule is attracted to the δ⁻ oxygen on a neighbour: a **hydrogen bond**. Each water molecule can take part in up to four of them at once.

Every property on the exam follows from that:

1. **Cohesion and surface tension.** Surface molecules are pulled inward and sideways by their neighbours and have nobody above them, so the surface behaves like a stretched skin. A water strider stands on it; a drop beads on a leaf.
2. **Adhesion and capillary action.** Water also hydrogen-bonds to charged or polar surfaces. In a narrow glass tube (or a xylem vessel) adhesion to the walls pulls the edge of the column up, cohesion drags the rest along, and the column rises. Narrower tube, higher rise.
3. **High specific heat.** Heat added to water first goes into breaking hydrogen bonds rather than making molecules move faster, so the temperature climbs slowly. Oceans buffer coastal climates; your body temperature does not swing with every warm room.
4. **High heat of vaporization.** To leave the liquid, a molecule must break all its hydrogen bonds, which takes a lot of energy. The molecules that do escape are the most energetic ones, so the liquid left behind is cooler: that is evaporative cooling, and it is why sweating works.
5. **Ice floats.** As water freezes, hydrogen bonds lock the molecules into an open lattice that holds them *farther apart* than in the liquid. Solid water is less dense than liquid water, so ice forms on top of a pond and insulates the water beneath it.
6. **Water is the solvent of life.** Ions and polar molecules are surrounded by hydration shells and dissolve; nonpolar molecules are pushed together instead (the basis of membranes in Unit 2).

## The elements of life

Living things are built mostly from four elements: oxygen (about 65% of human body mass), carbon (18%), hydrogen (10%) and nitrogen (3%). Compare that with the atmosphere, which is 78% nitrogen and 21% oxygen with only a trace of carbon. Organisms are *not* a sample of their surroundings; they must take in specific elements and spend energy rearranging them.

What each element is for:

- **Carbon** forms four covalent bonds and makes chains, branches and rings. It is the backbone of every carbohydrate, lipid, protein and nucleic acid.
- **Nitrogen** is needed for amino groups in proteins and for the bases of DNA and RNA. The N≡N triple bond in air is so strong that most organisms cannot use atmospheric nitrogen directly (a theme that returns in the nitrogen cycle, Unit 8).
- **Phosphorus** is needed for the phosphate groups of nucleic acids, ATP and phospholipids.
- **Sulfur** appears in two amino acids and helps proteins fold.

Carbon's versatility comes with a cost: the same formula can be arranged in different ways. **Structural isomers** differ in how atoms are connected; **geometric (cis/trans) isomers** differ around a double bond that cannot rotate; **enantiomers** are mirror images. Enzymes usually recognise only one.

Functional groups decide behaviour. Hydroxyl (–OH), carbonyl (C=O), carboxyl (–COOH), amino (–NH₂), phosphate (–PO₄²⁻) and sulfhydryl (–SH) are polar and make a molecule hydrophilic; methyl (–CH₃) is nonpolar. A carboxyl group tends to give up H⁺ (acidic); an amino group tends to accept H⁺ (basic).

## Worked example 1: reading a surface-tension table

| Liquid | Surface tension at 20 °C (mN/m) | Can it hydrogen-bond? |
|---|---|---|
| Water | 72 | Yes, up to four per molecule |
| Ethanol | 22 | Yes, but only one –OH group |
| Hexane | 18 | No |

*Question:* Which claim does the table support best?

*Reasoning:* The liquids are ranked by how extensively they hydrogen-bond, and surface tension follows the same order. That is the pattern a cohesion explanation predicts. It does *not* prove that molar mass matters: hexane is heavier than ethanol yet has lower surface tension. On the exam, pick the explanation that matches the *pattern across all rows*, not one that fits a single row.

## Worked example 2: pH arithmetic

The AP equation sheet gives **pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]**.

- A solution with [H⁺] = 1 × 10⁻³ M has pH 3.
- Moving from pH 3 to pH 5 is a change of two units, so [H⁺] falls by a factor of 10 × 10 = 100.
- Blood at pH 7.4 is slightly basic; gastric fluid at pH 1–2 is strongly acidic. A drop of 0.2 pH units in blood is dangerous because it is a 58% rise in [H⁺], which is why the bicarbonate buffer matters.

If you are given [H⁺] and asked for pH, count the zeros after the decimal point. If you are given a pH change, think "tenfold per unit".

## Worked example 3: heat arithmetic

Specific heat of water is 1 cal/g·°C.

*How much heat raises 250 g of water by 4 °C?* Heat = mass × specific heat × ΔT = 250 × 1 × 4 = **1,000 cal**.

*Why does it matter biologically?* A 70 kg person is roughly 60% water, so tens of thousands of calories of metabolic heat only nudge body temperature. Sand, with a specific heat about one fifth of water's, would heat five times as fast.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Hydrogen bonds are the bonds inside a water molecule."** No. The O–H bonds *inside* a molecule are polar covalent. A hydrogen bond is the attraction *between* a hydrogen of one molecule and the oxygen of another.
- **"Ice sinks like other solids."** Most solids are denser than their liquids. Water is the exception because its hydrogen-bonded lattice is more open than the liquid.
- **"Water boils at a high temperature because it is heavy."** Water is a light molecule (18 g/mol). Its high boiling point and heat of vaporization come from hydrogen bonding, not mass.
- **"Nonpolar molecules repel water."** They do not repel it; water molecules simply prefer each other, so nonpolar molecules get excluded and cluster together.
- **"A pH change of 1 is a small change."** It is a tenfold change in [H⁺].
- **"Carbon is the most common element in the body."** By mass it is oxygen, because so much of the body is water.

## Where this goes next

The exclusion of nonpolar molecules by water is the reason phospholipids form bilayers (Unit 2, topic 2.3). Hydrogen bonds hold the two DNA strands together and shape proteins (weeks 2 and 3). Water's heat of vaporization returns in transpiration (Unit 8).
