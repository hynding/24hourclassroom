# Exam Prep Guide · Trigonometry

**Use it:** in week 33, before the practice final in week 34
**Covers:** all five units, weeks 1–32
**Standards:** HSG-SRT.C.6–8, HSG-SRT.D.9–11, HSF-TF.A.1–4, HSF-TF.B.5–7, HSF-TF.C.8–9, HSN-CN.A.3, HSN-CN.B.4–6, HSN-VM.A.1–3, HSN-VM.B.4–5

This guide explains how the course's cumulative final is put together and timed, shows which practice-final questions test which unit, collects the values, formulas and identities to know without looking anything up, and ends with a two-week review plan. Trigonometry is a regular course, not an AP course, so there is no outside exam to match: the practice final in week 34 has the same shape as the real final, and doing it under the real timing is the best rehearsal you can get.

## How the final is built

The final is cumulative: every unit counts, roughly in proportion to the weeks spent on it. It comes in four parts, taken in order. Calculators are collected or put away for the parts that don't allow them.

| Part | Questions | Format | Time | Calculator | Points |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 1–36 | multiple choice | about 70 minutes | not allowed | 36 (1 each) |
| B | 37–50 | multiple choice and numeric entry | about 35 minutes | scientific or graphing calculator | 14 (1 each) |
| C | 51–62 | select-all, true/false, fill-in-the-blank | about 20 minutes | not allowed | 16 (select-all 2 each) |
| D | 63–70 | free response | about 55 minutes | only where the question says "(Calculator.)" | 36 (four × 3, four × 6) |

That is about 3 hours of work for 102 points. Free response is a little over a third of the total, so a strong multiple-choice score cannot carry you on its own, and a strong Part D can rescue a shaky Part A.

**Calculator rules.** Part A and Part C are designed to be done by hand: every number in them comes from a special angle, a Pythagorean triple or a simple formula. In Part B and in the calculator questions of Part D, every question that needs one starts with "(Calculator.)". Use **degree mode** for any question whose angles are given in degrees (right triangles, bearings, oblique triangles, vectors, projectiles launched at 35°) and **radian mode** for everything else (sinusoidal models, equations on [0, 2π), complex numbers in radians). Check the mode at the start of every question, not once per part. A tide model evaluated in degree mode gives a confident, wrong answer with no warning.

**Answer formats.** Multiple choice has four options and one key. Select-all items have five options and two or three correct ones; you earn partial credit, so mark only the ones you can defend. Numeric items say how to round ("to the nearest hundredth"); round only at the end. Fill-in-the-blank items are matched exactly, so type the simplest form: "135" for an angle in degrees, "3x" for an argument, "parabola" for a conic.

Three question sets share a stimulus: questions 1–3 (a point on a terminal side), 8–10 (a sinusoid described only by a maximum and the next minimum) and 44–46 (two hikers walking on different bearings). In a set, a mistake in the first question tends to repeat in the others, so check the shared facts (r, the midline, the angle at the trailhead) before you start.

## Know these cold

### Unit 1

- Coterminal angles differ by 360° (or 2π). 1° = 60′, 1′ = 60″. Complementary angles sum to 90°, supplementary to 180°.
- 45°-45°-90°: sides 1 : 1 : √2. 30°-60°-90°: sides 1 : √3 : 2, with the short leg opposite 30° and the hypotenuse twice the short leg.
- SOH-CAH-TOA, and the reciprocals csc = 1/sin, sec = 1/cos, cot = 1/tan. Cofunctions: sin θ = cos(90° − θ), tan θ = cot(90° − θ), sec θ = csc(90° − θ).
- For a point (x, y) on the terminal side, r = √(x² + y²), sin θ = y/r, cos θ = x/r, tan θ = y/x. Signs by quadrant: all positive in I, sine (and cosecant) in II, tangent (and cotangent) in III, cosine (and secant) in IV.
- Reference angle: the acute angle to the x-axis. In degrees: 180° − θ in II, θ − 180° in III, 360° − θ in IV.
- π radians = 180°. Arc length s = rθ, sector area A = ½r²θ, linear speed v = rω, with θ and ω in radians. One revolution = 2π radians.
- Unit circle: the point at angle θ is (cos θ, sin θ). Values at 0, π/6, π/4, π/3, π/2 and their reflections into every quadrant, in both measures.
- Even/odd: cos(−θ) = cos θ; sin(−θ) = −sin θ; tan(−θ) = −tan θ. Pythagorean identities: sin²θ + cos²θ = 1, 1 + tan²θ = sec²θ, 1 + cot²θ = csc²θ.
- Bearings: N 52° E is measured from north toward east; the azimuth 052° is measured clockwise from north. The reverse of a bearing adds 180°.

### Unit 2

- For y = a sin(b(x − h)) + k or a cos(b(x − h)) + k: amplitude |a|, period 2π/|b|, phase shift h, midline y = k. Factor b out first: sin(2x − π/3) = sin(2(x − π/6)), a shift of π/6, not π/3.
- From a maximum M and minimum m: midline (M + m)/2, amplitude (M − m)/2. A maximum to the next minimum is half a period. Cosine starts at a maximum, −cosine at a minimum, sine at the midline going up.
- y = tan(bx) and y = cot(bx) have period π/|b|. Tangent's asymptotes are where cosine is 0; secant shares them. Cosecant's asymptotes are where sine is 0.
- Simple harmonic motion d = a sin ωt or a cos ωt: period 2π/ω, frequency ω/(2π) cycles per unit time.
- Ranges: sin⁻¹ in [−π/2, π/2], cos⁻¹ in [0, π], tan⁻¹ in (−π/2, π/2). The course's convention for the others: arccot in (0, π), arcsec in [0, π] without π/2, arccsc in [−π/2, π/2] without 0.
- sin(sin⁻¹ x) = x for −1 ≤ x ≤ 1, but sin⁻¹(sin x) = x only when x is already in [−π/2, π/2]. Compositions such as cos(tan⁻¹ x) come from a reference triangle: cos(sin⁻¹ x) = √(1 − x²), tan(sin⁻¹ x) = x/√(1 − x²).

### Unit 3

- Strategy for simplifying and verifying: rewrite in sines and cosines, use a Pythagorean substitution, factor, combine fractions, multiply by a conjugate. Work one side; never move terms across the equals sign in a proof. One counterexample shows an equation is not an identity.
- sin(α ± β) = sin α cos β ± cos α sin β. cos(α ± β) = cos α cos β ∓ sin α sin β (note the sign flip). tan(α ± β) = (tan α ± tan β)/(1 ∓ tan α tan β).
- sin 2x = 2 sin x cos x. cos 2x = cos²x − sin²x = 1 − 2 sin²x = 2 cos²x − 1. tan 2x = 2 tan x/(1 − tan²x).
- Power reducing: sin²x = (1 − cos 2x)/2, cos²x = (1 + cos 2x)/2. Half-angle: sin(u/2) = ±√((1 − cos u)/2), cos(u/2) = ±√((1 + cos u)/2), with the sign from the quadrant of u/2; tan(u/2) = (1 − cos u)/sin u = sin u/(1 + cos u).
- Product-to-sum: 2 sin A cos B = sin(A + B) + sin(A − B); 2 cos A cos B = cos(A − B) + cos(A + B); 2 sin A sin B = cos(A − B) − cos(A + B).
- a sin x + b cos x = R sin(x + φ) with R = √(a² + b²), cos φ = a/R, sin φ = b/R. The maximum value is R.
- Equations: isolate the function, find the reference angle, place it in the right quadrants, then add 2πn (πn for tangent). For sin(kx) = c on [0, 2π), solve for kx over [0, 2πk) and divide by k.

### Unit 4

- Law of Sines: a/sin A = b/sin B = c/sin C (for AAS, ASA and SSA). Law of Cosines: c² = a² + b² − 2ab cos C (for SAS and SSS).
- Ambiguous case (SSA with A acute): compute h = b sin A. a < h: no triangle; a = h: one right triangle; h < a < b: two triangles (B and 180° − B); a ≥ b: one triangle.
- Area = ½ab sin C; Heron's formula with s = (a + b + c)/2: Area = √(s(s − a)(s − b)(s − c)).
- Vector ⟨a, b⟩: magnitude √(a² + b²), direction angle from tan θ = b/a with the quadrant checked. v = |v|⟨cos θ, sin θ⟩. Unit vector v/|v|.
- Bearing to direction angle: θ = 90° − bearing (adding 360° if negative).
- u · v = u₁v₁ + u₂v₂ = |u||v| cos θ. Orthogonal means u · v = 0. proj_v u = (u · v/|v|²) v. Work W = F · d = |F||d| cos θ.
- On a ramp at angle θ, a weight w splits into w sin θ along the slope and w cos θ into it. Equilibrium: the forces sum to the zero vector.

### Unit 5

- x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ, r² = x² + y², tan θ = y/x (check the quadrant). (r, θ) = (r, θ + 2πn) = (−r, θ + π).
- r = 2a cos θ is the circle (x − a)² + y² = a²; r = 2a sin θ is x² + (y − a)² = a²; r = c sec θ is the line x = c.
- Roses r = a sin(nθ) or a cos(nθ): n petals for odd n, 2n for even n, petal length |a|. Limaçons r = a + b cos θ: inner loop if a/b < 1, cardioid if a/b = 1, dimpled if 1 < a/b < 2, convex if a/b ≥ 2.
- z = a + bi = r(cos θ + i sin θ) = r cis θ, with r = |z| = √(a² + b²). Multiply: multiply moduli, add arguments. Divide: divide moduli, subtract arguments.
- De Moivre: [r cis θ]ⁿ = rⁿ cis(nθ). The n nth roots of r cis θ are r^(1/n) cis((θ + 2πk)/n) for k = 0, …, n − 1: equally spaced, 2π/n apart, the vertices of a regular polygon.
- Parametric curves: eliminate t algebraically or with cos²t + sin²t = 1; the t-interval decides which part of the curve is drawn and in which direction.
- Projectiles: x = (v₀ cos θ)t, y = h + (v₀ sin θ)t − 16t² (feet) or − 4.9t² (metres). Maximum height at t = v₀ sin θ/32 (or /9.8); the ball lands at the positive root of y = 0.
- Polar conics r = ed/(1 ± e cos θ) or ed/(1 ± e sin θ): e < 1 ellipse, e = 1 parabola, e > 1 hyperbola. Get a 1 in the denominator before you read e.
- Euler: e^(iθ) = cos θ + i sin θ, so e^(iπ) + 1 = 0. Multiplying by e^(iθ) rotates a point counterclockwise by θ: (x, y) goes to (x cos θ − y sin θ, x sin θ + y cos θ).

## The procedures that cost the most points

These are the errors that cost the most points across a trigonometry course, and most of them have a distractor on the practice final built around them.

1. **The wrong quadrant after an inverse function.** A calculator's tan⁻¹ returns an angle in (−π/2, π/2) whatever the point is. For (−2, 2√3), for −3 − 3i and for the vector ⟨−4, 4⟩, sketch the point first and adjust: add π (180°) in Quadrants II and III, 2π (360°) in Quadrant IV if the answer must be positive.
2. **Signs of the ratios.** Find r (always positive), then read the sign of each ratio from x and y. In Quadrant III tangent is positive even though both coordinates are negative.
3. **Forgetting to factor b out of a phase shift**, and confusing a maximum-to-minimum gap (half a period) with a full period.
4. **Missing solutions of multiple-angle equations.** sin 3x = 1 has three solutions on [0, 2π), not one, because 3x runs through three periods. Always enlarge the interval for kx before solving.
5. **Dividing by a trigonometric function.** Dividing 2 sin x cos x = sin x by sin x loses x = 0 and x = π. Factor instead. Squaring both sides can add extraneous roots: check every one in the original equation.
6. **The cosine sum sign.** cos(α + β) has a minus in the middle. And cos(60° + 45°) is not cos 60° + cos 45°.
7. **Using the Law of Sines on an obtuse angle without thinking.** sin⁻¹ always gives an acute angle. If the angle you want could be obtuse, find the largest angle first with the Law of Cosines, or check whether 180° minus your answer also fits.
8. **Skipping the ambiguous-case test.** In SSA, compare a with h = b sin A before you compute anything; two triangles means two sets of answers.
9. **Bearings versus direction angles.** A bearing is measured clockwise from north; a direction angle is counterclockwise from east. Convert before writing components, and convert back before reporting a course.
10. **Degree mode for radian questions, or the reverse.** Check the mode on every calculator question.

## How free-response items are scored

Each short answer is worth 3 points, one per part. Each long answer is worth 6 points, usually 2 per part, and the scoring notes name exactly what earns each point. The habits that win those points:

- **Write the setup, then the number.** "c² = 4.2² + 5.6² − 2(4.2)(5.6) cos 75°, so c ≈ 6.07 km" earns the point; "6.07" alone may not, and it earns nothing if it is wrong.
- **Exact answers when the question says exact, or when there is no calculator.** Leave 23 + 10√2, 45π/4 and (2 + 5√3)/12 as they are, then add a decimal if the question asks for one.
- **Round only at the end,** to the place the question names.
- **Follow-through is real.** If part (A) is wrong but you use your answer correctly in part (B), part (B) can still earn its point. So never leave a later part blank because an earlier one went badly.
- **Give units and directions.** A ground speed is in mph; a course is a three-digit bearing such as 066.1°; a sector area is in square metres.
- **Show the interval reasoning for inequalities.** List the boundary points, then test one value in each interval or use the factor signs, and say whether the endpoints are included.
- **For identities, keep one side fixed** and write a chain of equal expressions down the page. Ending with "tan x = tan x" after working on both sides earns less than a clean one-sided proof.

## A two-week review plan

**Week 33 (this week): rebuild the toolkit.**

- Day 1: Units 1 and 2 summary sheets. Write the unit circle from memory twice, in degrees and radians, with all signs. Then redo the free-response items from the Unit 1 exam without looking at your first attempt.
- Day 2: Unit 3 summary sheet. Write every identity from the list above on a blank page, check it, and repeat until the page is perfect. Then solve ten equations on [0, 2π), including three with a multiple angle.
- Day 3: Unit 4 summary sheet. Solve one triangle of each type (AAS, SSA with two solutions, SAS, SSS) and one wind problem with vectors, start to finish.
- Day 4: Unit 5 summary sheet. Convert five points and three equations between polar and rectangular, find the cube roots of one complex number, and eliminate the parameter from two curves.
- Day 5: Calculator day. Work every "(Calculator.)" question from the five unit exams, switching modes deliberately, then run through the flashcards for the weeks you marked weak.

**Week 34: the practice final.**

- Day 1: Sit Parts A and B (questions 1–50) in one block, about 105 minutes, with the calculator only for Part B.
- Day 2: Sit Parts C and D (questions 51–70), about 75 minutes.
- Day 3: Score everything with the explanations and scoring notes. For each lost point write one line: the topic, the error, and what you will do differently. Sort the lines by unit.
- Day 4: Re-teach yourself the two topics that cost the most points, using their weekly guides and worked examples, then redo the missed questions from scratch.
- Day 5: Redo every free-response question you scored under 2 out of 3 or under 4 out of 6, then stop.

## Test-day reminders

- Bring a charged calculator you know well, with spare batteries, and two pencils.
- Before Part B, set the mode; before each question, check it again.
- In Parts A and C, mark any question that takes longer than two minutes and come back to it. There is no penalty for a wrong answer, so answer everything.
- In Part D, read every part of a question before starting: later parts often reuse an earlier result, and part (C) sometimes tells you what part (A) should have been.
