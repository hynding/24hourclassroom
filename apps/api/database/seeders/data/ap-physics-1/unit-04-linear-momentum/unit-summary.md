## The ideas that generate every question

1. **Momentum is mass times velocity, and it is a vector (4.1).** p = mv, in kg·m/s. In one dimension, a sign shows the direction. A system's momentum is the vector sum of its parts, so momenta can cancel while kinetic energies (scalars) never do.
2. **The centre of mass carries the system's momentum (4.1).** p_sys = M_total v_cm, so v_cm = Σ(mv)/Σm: a mass-weighted average, not a plain one.
3. **Momentum and kinetic energy answer different questions (4.1).** K = p²/(2m). Doubling the speed doubles p and quadruples K. For equal momenta, the lighter object has more kinetic energy.
4. **Net force is the rate of change of momentum (4.2).** F_net = Δp/Δt, which becomes F_net = ma for constant mass. The slope of a momentum–time graph is the net force.
5. **Impulse changes momentum (4.2).** J = F_avg Δt = area under the force–time graph = Δp. Area below the axis is negative impulse.
6. **Stopping time controls force, not impulse (4.2).** For a fixed Δp, F_avg ∝ 1/Δt. Airbags, mats, bent knees and crumple zones all lengthen Δt.
7. **Internal forces cannot change a system's momentum (4.3).** Third-law pairs give equal and opposite impulses. Only a net external impulse changes p_sys; when it is zero or negligible, momentum is conserved and v_cm is constant.
8. **Collisions are classified by kinetic energy (4.4).** Momentum is conserved in every collision of an isolated system. Elastic: K conserved too. Inelastic: K decreases. Perfectly inelastic: the objects stick, losing the most K that momentum allows.

## Equations and when to use them

| Relation | Use it when | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| p = mv; p_sys = Σmv | finding momentum of objects or systems | signs for direction |
| v_cm = p_sys/M_total | centre-of-mass motion | weight by mass |
| F_net = Δp/Δt | average force over an interval, slope of p–t | Δp, not p |
| J = F_avg Δt = area under F–t = Δp | kicks, bounces, crashes, force-sensor data | triangle areas use ½ |
| Σp_before = Σp_after | isolated system, or a brief collision | choose the system first |
| Σp_x and Σp_y conserved separately | 2D collisions and explosions | combine with √ and tan⁻¹ of components |
| ΣK_before = ΣK_after | elastic collisions only | never across a sticking collision |
| v_f = Σmv/Σm | perfectly inelastic | |
| v₁f = (m₁ − m₂)v/(m₁ + m₂), v₂f = 2m₁v/(m₁ + m₂) | elastic, head-on, target at rest | check with limiting cases |

## Representations to know

| Representation | What it shows | The check |
|---|---|---|
| Force–time graph | impulse as area; average force = area/duration | positive and negative areas add with signs |
| Momentum–time graph | net force as slope | horizontal line means zero net force |
| Momentum bar chart or before/after table | each object's p before and after | system totals equal when momentum is conserved |
| Energy bar chart or table | K, U_g, U_s and E_int at each state | total bar height constant; E_int grows in inelastic collisions |
| Component table (2D) | p_x and p_y for each object | each column sums to the same value before and after |

## Reasoning moves that earn AP points

- **Name the system and justify conservation.** "System: both carts. The collision forces are internal; friction's impulse during the brief collision is negligible, so the system's momentum is conserved."
- **Split two-stage problems at the collision.** Momentum across the collision; mechanical energy across the swing, slide or spring compression. Say why each law applies where it does.
- **Argue with functional dependence.** "Δp is fixed by the speeds, so F_avg = Δp/Δt; tripling Δt makes the average force one third as large."
- **Check limiting cases.** Equal masses in an elastic collision exchange velocities; a very heavy projectile sends a light target off at about 2v; a light object bouncing off a heavy one reverses with nearly the same speed. A sticking collision with zero total momentum loses all its kinetic energy.
- **Classify from data.** Confirm momentum, compute total K before and after (or compare approach and separation speeds), then name the collision type.
- **Linearise lab data.** Sticky collisions: graph v_f against v_i, slope m_A/(m_A + m_B). Impulse tests: graph Δv against J, slope 1/m. Explosions from rest: graph v_A/v_B against m_B/m_A, slope 1.
- **Account for the energy.** Kinetic energy that disappears becomes internal energy (and sound); kinetic energy that appears in an explosion came from stored elastic or chemical energy.

## Classic question types and how to attack them

**"Find the impulse / average force."** Read the area under the F–t graph (or compute Δp with signs), then divide by the duration for the average force. On rebounds, the magnitudes of the before and after momenta add.

**"Why does the cushion help?"** Same Δp, so same impulse; longer Δt; smaller average force. Never "the cushion absorbs momentum".

**"What is the recoil / final velocity?"** Write Σp before = Σp after with signs, including every object that moves together (student + skateboard).

**"Which system conserves momentum?"** Include every object that exerts a large force in the interaction. A falling ball conserves momentum only with Earth included.

**"Is the collision elastic?"** Compare total kinetic energy; momentum alone cannot decide.

**"Ballistic pendulum or block on a spring."** Work backward from the height or compression with energy, then use momentum for the collision.

**"Two dimensions."** Make a component table; never average speeds or add perpendicular momenta directly.

## Common mistakes

- Subtracting speeds instead of velocities on a rebound.
- Adding momentum magnitudes for objects moving in opposite directions.
- Saying an airbag reduces the impulse.
- Using the peak force as the average force of a triangular pulse.
- Assuming momentum conservation for an object acted on by an external force (the ball alone, the cart alone at a wall).
- Assuming equal speeds after an explosion instead of equal momentum magnitudes.
- Treating "inelastic" as "momentum not conserved".
- Using kinetic-energy conservation across a sticking collision.
- Forgetting to add the weight when asked for the normal force during a landing or bounce: F_N = F_net + mg.

## Self-test

1. A 0.50 kg ball hits a floor at 5.0 m/s and leaves at 3.0 m/s. Impulse from the floor?
2. A force–time graph is a triangle with peak 400 N and duration 0.020 s. Impulse and average force?
3. A 70 kg skater at rest throws a 5.0 kg ball forward at 7.0 m/s. Her recoil speed?
4. A 2.0 kg cart at 3.0 m/s sticks to a 4.0 kg cart at rest. Final speed and fraction of K lost?
5. A 1.0 kg cart at 2.0 m/s hits a 1.0 kg cart at rest elastically. Final velocities?
6. A system's p–t graph is horizontal for 3 s. What is the net external force?

**Answers.** 1. 0.50 × (3.0 + 5.0) = 4.0 N·s upward. 2. ½ × 400 × 0.020 = 4.0 N·s; F_avg = 200 N. 3. 35/70 = 0.50 m/s backward. 4. v = 6.0/6.0 = 1.0 m/s; K goes from 9.0 J to 3.0 J, so two thirds is lost. 5. The first cart stops; the second moves at 2.0 m/s. 6. Zero.
