# Exam Prep Guide · AP Physics 1: Algebra-Based

**Use it:** in week 33, before the practice final in week 34
**Covers:** all eight units, every one of which the AP Physics 1 exam tests
**Framework:** AP Physics 1: Algebra-Based Course and Exam Description (effective fall 2024, with the 2026-27 clarifications); exam format from AP Central, May 2027 exam

This guide explains the May 2027 AP Physics 1 exam, how the practice final rehearses it and how free responses are scored, then collects the representations, reasoning moves and unit-by-unit facts to know cold, the errors that cost the most points, and a two-week review plan. If you are taking physics as a regular course, the whole practice final is your final exam.

## The AP exam in May 2027

The exam is on **Wednesday, May 5, 2027**, in the afternoon session. It is **hybrid digital**: you read every question in the Bluebook app and select your multiple-choice answers there, but you **write your free responses by hand** in a paper booklet.

| Section | Questions | Time | Share of score | How you answer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I: Multiple choice | 42, four options each | 85 minutes | 50% | in Bluebook |
| II: Free response | 4 | 95 minutes | 50% | read in Bluebook, written in a paper booklet |

- **Calculators are allowed throughout**, in both sections (an approved scientific or graphing calculator; a graphing calculator is also built into Bluebook).
- The **equation sheet and table of information** are provided: g = 9.8 m/s², G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg², the density of water 1000 kg/m³, atmospheric pressure 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa, and the equations for every unit. You do not need to memorise formulas, but you do need to know which one applies, what every symbol means and when it stops being valid.
- Section I allows about 2 minutes per multiple-choice question. There is no penalty for a wrong answer, so never leave one blank.
- Because Section II is answered on paper, you will **draw** free-body diagrams, energy bar charts and graphs by hand. Practise drawing them neatly and labelling every arrow and axis; a grader can only score what is on the page.

### What the revised course tests, and what it does not

The course was revised for fall 2024. It **added** fluids (Unit 8), centre of mass, rotational dynamics and rolling, angular momentum and orbiting satellites. It **removed** electric charge, circuits, mechanical waves and sound. Skip those chapters in old review books and older released questions. Everything is algebra-based: slopes and areas of graphs replace calculus.

## How the practice final maps onto it

The practice final has 70 questions. **Questions 1–47 are a full mock AP exam** in the order and timing above.

- **Questions 1–42: Section I.** Forty-two four-option multiple-choice questions in roughly the AP unit proportions: 5 on Unit 1, 9 on Unit 2, 8 on Unit 3, 5 on Unit 4, 5 on Unit 5, 3 on Unit 6, 2 on Unit 7 and 5 on Unit 8. Three sets of three questions share one situation, like the shared-stimulus questions on the real exam: questions 10–12 (a cart pulled by a hanging mass), questions 18–20 (a skateboarder on a ramp, with an energy bar chart) and questions 38–40 (a block weighed in air and in water). Several questions ask you to read a described graph: velocity–time (question 2), force–position (question 22), force–time (question 24) and position–time for an oscillator (question 37).
- **Question 43: Mathematical Routines** (10 points). A box sliding down a rough ramp: derive the acceleration, calculate, test limiting cases, reason about a change in mass, and check with energy.
- **Questions 44 and 45: Translation Between Representations** (12 points). A student on a scale in an elevator: a free-body diagram, an equation derived from it, calculations from velocity data, then a graph of the scale reading and an argument that all the representations agree. The app caps one question at 10 points, so parts A–C (8 points) are question 44 and part D (4 points) is question 45, with the same situation word for word. Treat them as one question.
- **Question 46: Experimental Design and Analysis** (10 points). Finding a spring constant from oscillation periods: design the procedure, linearise the data, compute a slope from a table and interpret it.
- **Question 47: Qualitative/Quantitative Translation** (8 points). A disk and a hoop rolling down a ramp: a paragraph argument first, then a derivation that has to agree with it.

Sit questions 1–47 in one block with the AP timings (85 minutes, a short break, then 95 minutes). Write the free responses on paper, as you will in May, even though the app has a text box: then type or photograph them in. Do not convert your total into a 1–5 score (cut points are not published in advance); note the topic of every lost point instead.

**Questions 48–70** are not AP format: select-all, true/false, numeric, fill-in-the-blank and short-answer items across all eight units, including a three-question set on one cart collision data table. Do them on another day, in about 45 minutes. Between them and Section I, every one of the course's topics is tested at least once.

## What each unit contributes

| Unit | Multiple-choice weighting | What dominates the questions |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Kinematics | 10–15% | displacement vs distance, motion graphs (slopes and areas), relative motion, projectiles |
| 2 Force and Translational Dynamics | 18–23% | systems and centre of mass, free-body diagrams, Newton's three laws, gravity, friction, springs, circular motion |
| 3 Work, Energy, and Power | 18–23% | kinetic energy, work as force times displacement component, potential energy, energy bar charts, power |
| 4 Linear Momentum | 10–15% | momentum, impulse as area under F–t, conservation in collisions and explosions, elastic vs inelastic |
| 5 Torque and Rotational Dynamics | 10–15% | rotational kinematics, v = rω, torque, rotational inertia, rotational equilibrium, τ = Iα |
| 6 Energy and Momentum of Rotating Systems | 5–8% | rotational kinetic energy, work by a torque, angular momentum, rolling, satellites |
| 7 Oscillations | 5–8% | conditions for SHM, period of a spring and a pendulum, graphs of SHM, energy in SHM |
| 8 Fluids | 10–15% | density, pressure with depth, buoyancy, continuity, Bernoulli |

Units 2 and 3 together are about two-fifths of Section I, so forces and energy deserve the most review time. Units 6 and 7 are small in Section I but often supply free-response situations, because they combine everything else.

The three science practices run through every question: **1 Creating Representations, 2 Mathematical Routines, 3 Scientific Questioning and Argumentation.** Expect at least as many points for drawing, predicting and explaining as for calculating.

## The free-response questions and how they are scored

Four questions in 95 minutes. A sensible budget is about 25 minutes for Mathematical Routines, 30 for Translation Between Representations, 25 for Experimental Design and Analysis and 15 for Qualitative/Quantitative Translation. The situations change every year; the shapes do not.

- **Mathematical Routines (MR), 10 points.** A multi-step quantitative problem. You derive a symbolic expression from a physics principle (start from ΣF = ma, conservation of energy or momentum, or τ = Iα, not from a memorised result), calculate a value, then reason about what happens when something changes, often by checking a limiting case.
- **Translation Between Representations (TBR), 12 points.** You draw a representation (a free-body diagram or an energy bar chart), derive an equation from that representation, sketch or describe a graph, and explain why all of them are consistent. The points reward the links between the representations as much as each one alone.
- **Experimental Design and Analysis (LAB), 10 points.** You describe a procedure (what to measure, with what equipment, how to reduce uncertainty), then analyse given data: choose what to graph to get a straight line, compute the slope from the line, and say what the slope or intercept means physically.
- **Qualitative/Quantitative Translation (QQT), 8 points.** A paragraph-length argument with no equations, then a derivation, then an explanation of how the two agree.

**How the points work.** Each point is attached to something specific, and the scoring guides in this course name it, so read them after every attempt:

- **Start from a principle.** "Newton's second law along the ramp: ma = mg sin θ − μ_k mg cos θ" earns a point; writing down a final formula with no starting point often does not.
- **Diagrams are scored strictly.** Every arrow on a free-body diagram is a force exerted by a named object, drawn from the object, in the right direction, with relative lengths that match the motion. An extra arrow ("force of motion", "centripetal force" drawn as a separate force, "net force") costs the point.
- **Paragraph points need a chain.** A claim, the physics principle that supports it, and the step from principle to claim. "The hoop is slower because it has more rotational inertia" is incomplete until you say why that matters: a larger share of the same energy goes into rotation.
- **Symbols only, when asked.** "In terms of m, g and h" means no numbers and no quantities outside that list.
- **Consistency.** A wrong value carried correctly into later parts usually still earns their points, so finish every part, and never erase work you are unsure of.
- **Units and sensible figures.** Give every numerical answer a unit and two or three significant figures.

## Representations to know cold

| Representation | What to show | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Free-body diagram | a dot for the object; one arrow per force, from the object, labelled F_g, F_N, F_T, F_f, F_s; lengths in proportion | ΣF and the direction of the acceleration |
| Position–time graph | slope = velocity | where the object is and how fast it moves |
| Velocity–time graph | slope = acceleration; area = displacement | the acceleration's direction, which may differ from the motion |
| Force–position graph | area = work | the change in kinetic energy (work–energy theorem) |
| Force–time graph | area = impulse | the change in momentum |
| Energy bar chart | bars for K, U_g, U_s (and ΔE_internal, or work by outside forces) at each state | conservation or transfer of energy for a named system |
| Momentum bar chart | p of each object before and after | conservation of momentum, signs included |
| Linearised graph | the quantities that make a straight line (T² against m, v² against h) | the physical meaning of the slope |

For an energy bar chart, **name the system first**. Earth must be in the system for U_g to appear; if Earth is outside, gravity does work instead. A spring must be in the system for U_s to appear.

## Reasoning moves that earn points

- **Functional dependence.** Before calculating, ask how the answer scales. K ∝ v², so tripling v makes K nine times larger. A pendulum's period depends on √L, not on mass. Orbital speed v = √(GM/r), so a radius four times larger halves v. A mass on a spring has T ∝ √m.
- **Limiting cases.** Test a derived expression at extremes: μ_k → 0 should give the frictionless result; θ → 90° on a ramp should give free fall; a massless pulley should drop out. If the limit is wrong, the derivation is wrong.
- **Choosing the system.** Momentum is conserved for a system with no net external impulse; angular momentum for one with no net external torque; mechanical energy when only internal conservative forces do work. Say which system and why.
- **Acceleration from the slope, not the velocity.** An elevator moving upward while slowing has a downward acceleration and a scale reading below the weight.
- **Linearise, then interpret.** If theory says y ∝ x², graph y against x². Compute the slope from two points on the best-fit line far apart, never from a single data point, and say what the slope equals in physical terms.
- **Equal and opposite does not mean third-law pair.** Third-law partners act on different objects. Weight and normal force on a resting book are equal because of equilibrium.

## Unit-by-unit facts to know cold

- **Unit 1.** Average velocity = displacement/time, not distance/time. In projectile motion the horizontal velocity is constant and the vertical acceleration is 9.8 m/s² downward at every point, including the top. Relative velocity adds as vectors; a river current perpendicular to your heading changes where you land, not how long you take to cross.
- **Unit 2.** Centre of mass x_cm = Σmx/Σm. Normal force equals mg only on a level surface with no other vertical forces and no vertical acceleration. Static friction is whatever is needed, up to μ_s F_N. Spring force F = −kx. In circular motion the net force points to the centre, mv²/r, and is supplied by real forces (friction, tension, gravity, normal force). g at a planet's surface is GM/R².
- **Unit 3.** K = ½mv²; W = Fd cos θ; U_g = mgh near Earth; U_s = ½kx²; P = W/t = Fv. Kinetic friction transforms mechanical energy into internal energy.
- **Unit 4.** p = mv; impulse J = FΔt = Δp = area under F–t. Momentum is conserved in every collision of an isolated system; kinetic energy only in elastic ones. Perfectly inelastic means the objects move together.
- **Unit 5.** ω = Δθ/Δt, rotational kinematics mirror the linear equations; v = rω, a_t = rα. τ = rF sin θ. I = Σmr² for point masses; more mass farther from the axis means more I. Equilibrium needs both ΣF = 0 and Στ = 0. Στ = Iα.
- **Unit 6.** K_rot = ½Iω²; work by a torque W = τΔθ (radians). L = Iω for a rigid body, L = mvr⊥ for a point particle. Rolling without slipping: v = Rω, and the kinetic energy is shared between translation and rotation, so objects with smaller I/(MR²) reach the bottom of a ramp first, whatever their mass or radius. A circular orbit has v = √(GM/r).
- **Unit 7.** SHM needs a restoring force proportional to displacement. T = 2π√(m/k) for a spring (independent of g and amplitude); T = 2π√(L/g) for a small-angle pendulum (independent of mass). At the turning points v = 0 and |a| is maximum; at equilibrium speed is maximum and a = 0. Total energy ½kA².
- **Unit 8.** ρ = m/V; P = F/A; P = P₀ + ρgh, where ρgh alone is the gauge pressure. Buoyant force = weight of displaced fluid, ρ_fluid V_displaced g, and does not depend on the object's density. Continuity A₁v₁ = A₂v₂; Bernoulli P + ½ρv² + ρgh constant along a streamline; faster flow at the same height means lower pressure. Water leaving a hole a depth h below an open surface has v = √(2gh).

## Common errors that cost the most points

| Error | The fix |
|---|---|
| A force in the direction of motion on a free-body diagram | only interactions with named objects; a moving object needs no force to keep moving |
| Drawing the "component down the ramp" as an extra force | gravity is one arrow; components are for the equations |
| Normal force always equal to mg | find it from the perpendicular direction's equation every time |
| Zero acceleration at the top of a flight | vertical velocity is zero there; acceleration is still 9.8 m/s² downward |
| Kinetic energy conserved in a sticking collision | only momentum is conserved; compute K before and after to see the loss |
| Using cos where sin belongs | sketch the angle; the component adjacent to θ gets cos θ |
| Degrees or revolutions in τΔθ or v = rω | radians: one revolution is 2π rad |
| Buoyant force depends on the object's density | it depends on the fluid's density and the displaced volume |
| Pendulum period depends on the bob's mass | it does not: T = 2π√(L/g) |
| Slope taken from one data point | use two well-separated points on the best-fit line |
| Paragraph with a claim and no principle | claim, then the law, then the link |

## Two-week review plan

**Week 1 (week 33): re-teach yourself.**

- Day 1: Units 1 and 2. Reread the unit summaries, then redraw free-body diagrams for five situations from the weekly quizzes (ramp, elevator, pulley, curve, hanging spring) without looking.
- Day 2: Unit 3. Draw energy bar charts for three systems with and without Earth included; redo one unit-exam free-response question on paper.
- Day 3: Units 4 and 5. One collision, one explosion, one seesaw and one τ = Iα problem from the unit exams; check each with a limiting case.
- Day 4: Units 6 and 7. Rolling, angular momentum and satellites; then write out the SHM graphs (x, v, a against time) and energy at the turning points and at equilibrium.
- Day 5: Unit 8, then one lab question from any unit exam: design, linearise, slope, meaning.

Use the weekly flashcards for 15 minutes a day throughout, starting with the units that cost you most in their unit exams.

**Week 2 (week 34): the practice final.**

- Day 1: Questions 1–47 in one sitting with the AP timings: 85 minutes, a 10-minute break, then 95 minutes, with free responses written on paper.
- Day 2: Score it with the explanations and the scoring guides. For every lost point, write one line: what the question tested and what you will do differently. Count your losses by unit and by question type.
- Day 3: Re-teach yourself the two topics that cost you the most points, using their weekly guides and flashcards.
- Day 4: Questions 48–70, timed at 45 minutes.
- Day 5: Rewrite every free-response answer that lost three or more points, starting each from a principle and finishing every paragraph with the link from law to claim. Then stop. Rest matters more than one more set.

Until the exam, alternate a timed block of 10–15 multiple-choice questions with one hand-written free-response question every other day.

## Test-day reminders

- Bring an approved calculator, pencils and a pen for the paper booklet, and make sure Bluebook is installed and your device charged the night before.
- In Section I, flag any question that takes longer than three minutes and come back to it. Answer everything.
- In Section II, read every part of a question before writing. Label every diagram, start every derivation from a law, put units on every number, and when a part asks you to explain, write a claim, a principle and the link between them.
