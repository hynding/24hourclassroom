## The ideas that generate every question

1. **Chemistry is the study of matter and its changes**, answered by investigation: a testable hypothesis, one independent variable, a measured dependent variable, everything else controlled, and a control trial for comparison. Laws describe; theories explain; neither becomes the other.
2. **Matter sorts by composition.** Pure substances (elements, compounds) have fixed composition and properties; mixtures (homogeneous, heterogeneous) have any composition and separate by physical means. A compound comes apart only by chemical change and has properties unlike its elements.
3. **Physical versus chemical.** A physical change keeps the substance's identity (state, shape, dissolving); a chemical change makes a new substance. Gas, precipitate, colour, energy and irreversibility are clues, not proof.
4. **Measurements carry units and uncertainty.** SI units and prefixes; significant figures are certain digits plus one estimate; multiply/divide keeps the fewest significant figures, add/subtract keeps the fewest decimal places; accuracy is closeness to truth, precision is agreement among repeats.
5. **Dimensional analysis** solves conversions by cancelling units; **density = m ÷ V** is intensive and identifies substances.
6. **Particles in motion.** Temperature is average kinetic energy; heat is energy transferred hot to cold; the state depends on attraction versus motion; phase changes happen at constant temperature.

## Key terms and formulas

| Item | What to know |
|---|---|
| Independent / dependent / controlled variable | Changed / measured / held constant. A control trial has no treatment. |
| Element, compound, mixture | One kind of atom / two or more elements in a fixed ratio / substances physically combined in any ratio. |
| Homogeneous vs heterogeneous | Uniform, one phase (solution, alloy, air) / visibly different parts (granite, soup). |
| Separation methods | Filtration (particle size), evaporation and distillation (boiling point), chromatography (attraction to surface vs solvent), magnetism, decanting (density). |
| Intensive vs extensive | Independent of amount (density, boiling point) / scales with amount (mass, volume). |
| Significant figures | Non-zeros and sandwiched zeros count; leading zeros never; trailing zeros only with a decimal point; exact numbers are unlimited. |
| Rounding rules | × and ÷: fewest significant figures. + and −: fewest decimal places. Round once, at the end. |
| Percent error | (measured − accepted) ÷ accepted × 100, as an absolute value. |
| Prefixes | kilo 10³, centi 10⁻², milli 10⁻³, micro 10⁻⁶, nano 10⁻⁹. 1 mL = 1 cm³; 1 L = 1,000 mL. |
| Temperature | K = °C + 273.15. 0 K is absolute zero. |
| Density | D = m ÷ V; m = D × V; V = m ÷ D. Water 1.00 g/mL; Al 2.70, Fe 7.87, Cu 8.96, Pb 11.3, Au 19.3 g/cm³. |
| Energy units | Joule; 1 kJ = 1,000 J; 1 Cal (food) = 4.184 kJ. |
| States | Solid: fixed shape and volume, vibrating particles. Liquid: fixed volume, flows. Gas: fills container, compressible. |
| Phase changes | Melting, vaporization, sublimation absorb energy; freezing, condensation, deposition release it. |

## Common errors

- Calling air or brass a compound, or O₂ a compound. Variable composition means mixture; one kind of atom means element.
- Treating bubbles or a temperature change as proof of chemical change. Ask whether a new substance formed.
- Counting leading zeros as significant; using the add/subtract rule when multiplying.
- Forgetting that the subtraction in a water-displacement volume (31.4 − 25.0 = 6.4) can cut the significant figures of the density.
- Inverting a conversion factor so units do not cancel; forgetting to cube the factor for cm³ to m³.
- Mixing up heat and temperature; saying temperature rises during melting.
- Writing K = °C − 273.

## Worked example

A student is handed a bar of unknown metal, 4.00 cm × 2.50 cm × 1.20 cm, with a mass of 107.5 g. She also measures its mass three times on a second balance: 107.4, 107.6 and 107.5 g.

1. **Volume:** 4.00 × 2.50 × 1.20 = 12.0 cm³ (three significant figures).
2. **Density:** 107.5 ÷ 12.0 = 8.958 → **8.96 g/cm³** (three figures, limited by the volume).
3. **Identify:** copper, 8.96 g/cm³.
4. **Precision:** the three masses agree within 0.2 g, so the balance is precise. Whether it is accurate requires a known standard; the match with copper suggests it is.
5. **Convert the volume to litres:** 12.0 cm³ × (1 mL / 1 cm³) × (1 L / 1,000 mL) = 0.0120 L.
6. **What if** the bar were cut in half? Mass 53.8 g, volume 6.00 cm³, density still 8.96 g/cm³: density is intensive.
7. **Energy question:** the bar is warmed from 20 °C to 60 °C, which is 293 K to 333 K. Its particles' average kinetic energy rises; heat flowed from the warmer surroundings into the bar until the temperatures matched. Warming a metal is a physical change; the copper is still copper.

## What the unit exam looks like

About 42 items in roughly 70 minutes: multiple choice on every topic, two stimulus sets (a lab data table and a described experiment), select-all, true/false and fill-in-the-blank items, four numeric calculations (percent error, density, a conversion and an energy conversion), two short-answer questions with (a)(b)(c) parts, and one long answer on experimental design and classification. Show units on every numeric answer and round to significant figures only at the end.
