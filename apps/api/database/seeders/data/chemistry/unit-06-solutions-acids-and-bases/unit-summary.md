## The ideas that generate every question

1. **Like dissolves like.** Dissolving happens when solvent–solute attractions can replace the attractions broken. Polar and ionic in polar; nonpolar in nonpolar. Stirring, heating and crushing change the rate; only temperature (and pressure for gases) changes the solubility.
2. **A solubility curve is a saturation map.** On the line saturated, below it unsaturated, above it supersaturated. The mass that crystallises on cooling is the difference between the two readings, scaled to the mass of water. Gases run the other way: less soluble when warm, more soluble under pressure (Henry's law).
3. **Molarity counts moles per litre of solution.** M = n/V; n = MV. Dilution keeps moles fixed: M₁V₁ = M₂V₂. Ion concentrations follow the formula's subscripts.
4. **Colligative properties count particles.** Molality (mol per kg of solvent) times the van 't Hoff factor i gives the particle concentration; vapour pressure falls, boiling point rises (K_b = 0.512 °C/m), freezing point falls (K_f = 1.86 °C/m), osmosis pushes solvent toward more particles.
5. **An acid donates protons; a base accepts them.** Arrhenius: H⁺ or OH⁻ in water. Brønsted–Lowry: proton donor and acceptor, with conjugate pairs differing by one H⁺. Strong means completely ionised, not concentrated.
6. **K_w ties [H⁺] and [OH⁻] together.** [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C; pH = −log[H⁺]; pH + pOH = 14. One pH unit is a factor of ten.
7. **A titration is stoichiometry in solution.** Moles of the standard = MV; the balanced equation's ratio gives moles of the unknown; divide by its volume. The indicator's range must sit on the steep part of the curve, at the equivalence-point pH (7 only for strong with strong).

## Formulas and constants

| Formula | Use | Values to know |
|---|---|---|
| M = n/V | Molarity; V in litres of solution | — |
| M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ | Dilution of one solute (never a reaction) | — |
| m = n_solute / kg_solvent | Molality | — |
| P = X_solvent × P° | Vapour-pressure lowering (Raoult) | P° water 23.8 torr at 25 °C |
| ΔT_b = i K_b m | Boiling-point elevation | K_b = 0.512 °C/m (water) |
| ΔT_f = i K_f m | Freezing-point depression | K_f = 1.86 °C/m (water) |
| i | Particles per formula unit | sugar 1; NaCl 2; CaCl₂ 3; AlCl₃ 4 |
| K_w = [H⁺][OH⁻] | Ion product of water | 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C |
| pH = −log[H⁺]; [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ | pH; same pattern for pOH | pH + pOH = 14.00 |
| n = MV → ratio → M = n/V | Titration calculation | 1:1 for HCl/NaOH; 1:2 for H₂SO₄/NaOH |

## Lists to memorise

- **Strong acids:** HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄, HClO₄ (and HClO₃). Everything else is weak (HF, CH₃COOH, H₂CO₃, H₃PO₄).
- **Strong bases:** LiOH, NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂, Ba(OH)₂. Ammonia is weak.
- **Indicators:** methyl orange red → yellow at 3.1–4.4; bromothymol blue yellow → blue at 6.0–7.6; phenolphthalein colourless → pink at 8.2–10.0.
- **Equivalence-point pH:** strong acid + strong base 7; weak acid + strong base above 7; strong acid + weak base below 7.
- **Solubility values (g per 100 g water, 20 °C):** KNO₃ 32, NaCl 36.0, KCl 34. KNO₃ reaches 110 at 60 °C and 169 at 80 °C.

## Which equation when

| The problem asks… | Do this |
|---|---|
| Is it saturated / how much crystallises | Read the curve at the temperature, scale by (g water ÷ 100), subtract |
| Molarity from grams and mL | g ÷ molar mass = mol; mL ÷ 1,000 = L; divide |
| Grams in a volume of solution | n = MV; × molar mass |
| New concentration after adding water | M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ |
| Freezing or boiling point | molality → × i → × K → subtract from 0 or add to 100 |
| Molar mass from ΔT_f | m = ΔT_f/(iK_f); n = m × kg; M = g/n |
| pH of a strong acid | [H⁺] = molarity (× H per molecule); −log |
| pH of a strong base | [OH⁻] = molarity × OH per unit; pOH = −log; pH = 14 − pOH |
| [H⁺] from pH | 10⁻ᵖᴴ |
| Unknown concentration from a titration | n = MV of the standard; mole ratio; ÷ unknown's L |
| Choose an indicator | Decide the equivalence pH from acid/base strengths; pick the range that brackets it |

## Common errors

- Litres of water instead of litres of solution in molarity; grams instead of kilograms in molality.
- M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ used for a 1:2 titration, or for any reaction.
- i forgotten for an ionic solute, or i = 2 used for CaCl₂.
- ΔT_f reported as the freezing point instead of 0 − ΔT_f.
- "Concentrated" read as "strong"; a 6 M solution of acetic acid is still weak.
- pH of a base found as −log(base molarity): that is the pOH.
- Two OH⁻ per Ca(OH)₂ or Ba(OH)₂ forgotten.
- "pH 3 is twice as acidic as pH 6" (it is a thousand times).
- Equivalence point assumed to be pH 7 for a weak acid.
- Gas solubility thought to rise with temperature.

## Worked example: molarity to pH to titration

*Problem.* 2.00 g of NaOH (40.00 g/mol) is dissolved to make 500.0 mL of solution. (a) Molarity? (b) pH? (c) Volume of 0.0500 M H₂SO₄ needed to neutralise 20.00 mL of it?

1. n = 2.00/40.00 = 0.0500 mol; M = 0.0500/0.5000 = **0.100 M**.
2. [OH⁻] = 0.100 M; pOH = 1.00; pH = **13.00**.
3. Moles NaOH in 20.00 mL = 0.100 × 0.02000 = 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol. H₂SO₄ + 2 NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2 H₂O, so moles H₂SO₄ = 1.00 × 10⁻³ mol. V = 1.00 × 10⁻³/0.0500 = 0.0200 L = **20.0 mL**.

## Worked example: a colligative calculation

*Problem.* 5.85 g of NaCl is dissolved in 250.0 g of water. Freezing point and boiling point?

n = 5.85/58.44 = 0.100 mol; m = 0.100/0.2500 = 0.400 m; i = 2.
ΔT_f = 2 × 1.86 × 0.400 = 1.49 °C → freezes at **−1.49 °C**.
ΔT_b = 2 × 0.512 × 0.400 = 0.41 °C → boils at **100.41 °C**.
