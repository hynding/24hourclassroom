## The ideas that generate every question

1. **A gas is empty space and motion.** Kinetic molecular theory: tiny particles, constant random motion, elastic collisions, no attractions, average kinetic energy proportional to kelvin temperature. Pressure is the push of wall collisions. Every gas law is a consequence; every deviation from a gas law is one of the assumptions failing (attractions, particle volume).
2. **Kelvin or nothing.** Temperature in every gas law is kelvin, because only kelvin is proportional to kinetic energy and zero at absolute zero. K = °C + 273.15.
3. **Three laws are one law.** PV/T is constant for a fixed amount of gas. Hold one variable fixed and you get Boyle (PV), Charles (V/T) or Gay-Lussac (P/T). Add the amount and you get PV = nRT.
4. **Litres are moles, for gases.** Avogadro's law: equal volumes, equal numbers. 22.4 L/mol at STP; V = nRT/P elsewhere. Coefficients are litre ratios for gases at the same conditions.
5. **Gases in a mixture ignore each other.** Dalton: P_total = sum of partial pressures; P_A = X_A × P_total. A gas collected over water is wet: subtract water's vapour pressure.
6. **Phase changes cost energy without changing temperature.** Heat on a plateau is m × ΔH; heat on a slope is mcΔT. The size of ΔH and of vapour pressure both measure intermolecular forces.
7. **Boiling is a pressure match.** A liquid boils when its vapour pressure equals the external pressure. A phase diagram maps which phase is stable; the triple point and critical point are its two landmarks.

## Key terms in one line each

| Term | One line |
|---|---|
| Partial pressure | What one gas in a mixture would exert alone at the same T and V |
| Mole fraction | Moles of one component ÷ total moles; all mole fractions sum to 1 |
| Vapour pressure | Pressure of vapour in equilibrium with its liquid at a given T; rises steeply with T, falls with stronger IMFs |
| Normal boiling point | Temperature where vapour pressure = 1 atm |
| Heat of fusion / vaporisation | Energy per gram (or mole) to melt / to vaporise at the transition temperature |
| Sublimation / deposition | Solid ⇌ gas directly; dry ice and frost |
| Triple point | The one (P, T) where solid, liquid and gas coexist; water 0.01 °C and 0.006 atm; CO₂ −56.6 °C and 5.11 atm |
| Critical point | End of the liquid–gas line; water 374 °C, 218 atm; beyond it, a supercritical fluid |

## Which equation when

| The problem gives… | Use |
|---|---|
| The same gas sample before and after a change in P, V or T | Combined gas law (cancel what is constant) |
| Three of P, V, n, T for one sample, asks for the fourth | PV = nRT, with the R matching the pressure unit |
| A mass or density of gas and asks for molar mass (or the reverse) | M = mRT/(PV) or d = PM/(RT); at STP, M = d × 22.4 |
| Grams of a solid or liquid, asks for litres of gas (or reverse) | grams → moles → mole ratio → moles → litres (22.4 at STP, else nRT/P) |
| Litres of one gas, litres of another, same conditions | Coefficients as litre ratios |
| Moles of each gas in a mixture and a total pressure | Mole fractions, then P_A = X_A P_total |
| A gas "collected over water" | Subtract the vapour pressure of water at that temperature first |
| Heat to take a substance across a temperature range with phase changes | Split into segments: mcΔT for each phase, mΔH for each plateau, add |
| "At what temperature does it boil at pressure P?" | Read the vapour-pressure curve (or phase diagram) at P |

## Common errors

- Celsius in a gas law. Convert first; a negative volume is the giveaway.
- Gauge pressure used as absolute pressure; add atmospheric pressure.
- Mismatched R: 0.08206 with atm, 8.314 with kPa; torr must be converted to atm (÷ 760).
- 22.4 L/mol used away from STP.
- Litre ratios applied to a solid or liquid reactant.
- Vapour-pressure correction skipped, or 23.8 torr subtracted from a pressure in atm.
- q = mcΔT used across a phase change (ΔT is 0 on a plateau; use mΔH).
- 4.184 J/(g·°C) used for ice or steam.
- "Boiling point = 100 °C" instead of "vapour pressure = external pressure".
- Mole fraction computed from grams instead of moles.

## Worked example: a gas-collection problem from start to finish

*Problem.* 0.500 g of calcium carbonate reacts with excess acid: CaCO₃(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g). The CO₂ is collected over water at 25 °C (vapour pressure 23.8 torr) when the barometer reads 760 torr. (a) What volume of CO₂ is expected? (b) The student actually collects 118 mL. What is the percent yield?

*Solution.*
1. Moles of CaCO₃: 0.500 g ÷ 100.09 g/mol = 4.995 × 10⁻³ mol → 4.995 × 10⁻³ mol CO₂ (1:1).
2. Pressure of the CO₂: 760 − 23.8 = 736.2 torr = 0.9687 atm.
3. Volume: V = nRT/P = (4.995 × 10⁻³ × 0.08206 × 298)/0.9687 = 0.12215/0.9687 = 0.1261 L = **126 mL**.
4. Percent yield: 118/126 × 100 = **93.7%**.

*Checks.* The volume is a little larger than at STP (4.995 × 10⁻³ × 22.4 = 112 mL) because the gas is warmer and at slightly less than 1 atm. The yield is under 100% as some CO₂ dissolves in the water.

## Worked example: a heating curve

*Problem.* Heat needed to convert 30.0 g of ice at −5.0 °C to steam at 100 °C.

1. Ice warms: 30.0 × 2.09 × 5.0 = 313.5 J.
2. Melts: 30.0 × 334 = 10,020 J.
3. Water warms: 30.0 × 4.184 × 100 = 12,552 J.
4. Boils: 30.0 × 2,260 = 67,800 J.
5. Total: 90,686 J = **90.7 kJ**. Boiling is 75% of it.

## What to review from earlier units

- **1.4, 1.5:** significant figures (R has four; the data usually set the limit), unit conversions through 1 atm, density = m/V.
- **1.6:** K = °C + 273.15; heat versus temperature; kinetic versus potential energy; the states of matter.
- **3.6:** intermolecular forces (dispersion, dipole–dipole, hydrogen bonding) rank vapour pressures, boiling points and heats of vaporisation, and explain why real gases deviate from the ideal model.
- **4.1, 4.4, 4.6, 4.7:** balanced equations, molar mass, the mole-ratio method, limiting reactant and percent yield; gas stoichiometry is the same method with litres at one end.

## Last-minute checklist

- Write the equation in symbols, rearrange, then substitute with units.
- Convert temperatures to kelvin before anything else.
- Decide the direction of each change before computing (should the volume go up or down?).
- For over-water problems, subtract the vapour pressure first.
- For heating curves, list the segments before calculating any of them.
- Round once, at the end, to the fewest significant figures in the data.
