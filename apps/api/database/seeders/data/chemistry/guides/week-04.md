## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Law of conservation of mass | In a chemical change the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants. |
| Law of definite proportions | A given compound always contains its elements in the same ratio by mass. |
| Law of multiple proportions | When two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in small whole-number ratios (CO and CO₂: 1:2 in oxygen). |
| Dalton's atomic theory (1808) | Matter is made of indivisible atoms; atoms of one element are identical; compounds are atoms combined in fixed ratios; reactions rearrange atoms. |
| Cathode ray | A beam of electrons in an evacuated tube; Thomson showed it was made of negative particles far lighter than any atom. |
| Nucleus | The tiny, dense, positive centre of the atom, holding the protons and neutrons and nearly all the mass. |
| Proton | Charge +1, mass about 1 u, in the nucleus; its number defines the element. |
| Neutron | Charge 0, mass about 1 u, in the nucleus; its number varies among isotopes. |
| Electron | Charge −1, mass about 1/1,836 u, outside the nucleus; its number sets the charge of the atom. |
| Atomic number (Z) | Number of protons. |
| Mass number (A) | Protons + neutrons, a whole number for one atom. |
| Isotopes | Atoms of the same element (same Z) with different numbers of neutrons (different A). |
| Ion | An atom that has gained or lost electrons, so it carries a charge. |
| Atomic mass unit (u) | 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom; 1 u = 1.661 × 10⁻²⁴ g. |

## The two laws and Dalton's explanation (2.1)

Late in the eighteenth century careful weighing showed two regularities. First, **mass is conserved**: when a sealed flask of tin is heated so that the tin reacts with the air inside, the flask weighs the same after as before. Second, **a compound has a fixed composition**: water from a river, a glacier or a chemical reaction is always 11.2% hydrogen and 88.8% oxygen by mass. That is the **law of definite proportions**.

John Dalton explained both in 1808 with an **atomic theory**: all matter is made of tiny indivisible atoms; every atom of an element is identical in mass; atoms of different elements differ; compounds form when atoms combine in small whole-number ratios; a chemical reaction rearranges atoms but does not create, destroy or change them. If atoms are conserved, mass is conserved. If a compound is a fixed combination of atoms, its mass ratio is fixed.

The theory also predicted a third law, **multiple proportions**. Carbon forms two oxides. In one, 1.00 g of carbon combines with 1.33 g of oxygen; in the other, with 2.66 g. The oxygen masses are in the ratio 1:2, exactly what you expect if one compound is CO and the other CO₂. Whole-number ratios are the fingerprint of atoms.

Two of Dalton's claims were later revised: atoms are not indivisible (they have parts), and atoms of one element are not all identical in mass (isotopes exist). The theory survived because its core, atoms rearranging in whole-number ratios, still explains chemistry.

**Worked example.** 12.0 g of magnesium burns completely in oxygen to give 20.0 g of magnesium oxide. (a) What mass of oxygen reacted? (b) What is the mass percent of magnesium in magnesium oxide? (c) What mass of oxide forms from 30.0 g of magnesium?

(a) Conservation of mass: 20.0 − 12.0 = **8.0 g** of oxygen. (b) 12.0 ÷ 20.0 × 100 = **60.0% Mg**. (c) Definite proportions: the ratio is fixed, so 30.0 g Mg × (20.0 g oxide / 12.0 g Mg) = **50.0 g** of oxide.

**Try it.** Copper(II) oxide is 79.9% copper. How much copper is in 25.0 g of the oxide, and how much oxygen?
*Answer:* 0.799 × 25.0 = 20.0 g Cu; 25.0 − 20.0 = 5.0 g O.

## Taking the atom apart (2.1)

**Thomson (1897).** A high voltage across a tube with most of the air pumped out produces a glowing beam from the negative electrode, the cathode ray. Thomson showed the beam bent toward a positive plate, so it was negatively charged, and from how much it bent in electric and magnetic fields he found that its particles had a charge-to-mass ratio about 1,800 times that of a hydrogen ion. The particles were the same whatever metal the cathode was made of. Conclusion: every atom contains tiny negative **electrons**. Since atoms are neutral, Thomson pictured a ball of positive charge with electrons dotted through it, the **plum pudding model**.

**Rutherford (1909–1911).** Geiger and Marsden fired alpha particles (positive, heavy, fast) at a gold foil a few hundred atoms thick. Plum pudding predicted they would all pass straight through with tiny deflections, and most did. But about 1 in 8,000 bounced back at large angles. Rutherford's explanation: nearly all the atom's mass and all its positive charge are packed into a **nucleus** about 1/10,000 of the atom's diameter; the rest of the atom is mostly empty space where the electrons move. Only a direct hit on the tiny nucleus turns an alpha particle around.

**The neutron (Chadwick, 1932).** Nuclear masses were roughly twice what the protons accounted for; Chadwick identified a neutral particle of about the same mass as the proton.

**Bohr (1913)** put the electrons in fixed orbits with definite energies to explain the colours atoms emit; that story is told in week 6. The **modern model** replaces orbits with regions of probability (orbitals), but the nucleus-plus-electrons picture from Rutherford still stands.

**Figure description:** three sketches. (1) Plum pudding: a large grey circle of positive charge with small black dots (electrons) scattered through it. (2) Gold foil: alpha particles as arrows approaching a thin vertical foil; most pass straight through, a few bend slightly, one is turned back toward the source. (3) Nuclear model: a small dense dot at the centre labelled "nucleus: protons and neutrons", surrounded by a much larger empty circle in which electrons are shown as small dots.

## Protons, neutrons, electrons (2.2)

| Particle | Charge | Mass (u) | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proton | +1 | 1.007 (≈ 1) | nucleus |
| Neutron | 0 | 1.009 (≈ 1) | nucleus |
| Electron | −1 | 0.00055 (≈ 1/1,836) | outside the nucleus |

The **atomic number** Z is the number of protons, and it defines the element: every atom with 6 protons is carbon, no matter what else it has. The **mass number** A is protons plus neutrons. Neutrons = A − Z. In a neutral atom, electrons = protons. The **isotope symbol** puts A at top left and Z at bottom left of the element symbol; in plain text we write it as carbon-14 or ¹⁴C (Z is redundant because the symbol fixes it).

**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element with different neutron counts. Carbon-12 (6 p, 6 n) and carbon-14 (6 p, 8 n) are both carbon and behave the same chemically, because chemistry is done by electrons and both have six. They differ in mass and in nuclear stability: carbon-14 is radioactive, which week 5 uses for dating.

**Ions.** Losing electrons gives a positive ion (cation); gaining gives a negative ion (anion). The nucleus is untouched. Mg²⁺ has 12 protons, 10 electrons. Cl⁻ has 17 protons, 18 electrons.

**Worked example.** How many protons, neutrons and electrons are in (a) ⁵⁶Fe, (b) ³⁵Cl⁻, (c) ⁴⁰Ca²⁺?

(a) Iron is Z = 26: 26 p, 56 − 26 = 30 n, 26 e. (b) Chlorine Z = 17: 17 p, 18 n, 18 e (one extra for the −1 charge). (c) Calcium Z = 20: 20 p, 20 n, 18 e.

**Worked example.** An atom has 29 protons and 36 neutrons. Identify it and write its symbol.

Z = 29 is copper; A = 29 + 36 = 65; the isotope is copper-65, ⁶⁵Cu.

**Try it.** Which pairs are isotopes of each other: (i) 8 p 8 n and 8 p 10 n; (ii) 8 p 8 n and 9 p 8 n; (iii) 19 p 20 n and 19 p 22 n?
*Answer:* (i) and (iii) (same protons, different neutrons). (ii) are different elements (oxygen and fluorine).

## Common mistakes

- Taking the mass number from the periodic table. The table lists the *average atomic mass* (a decimal); the mass number is a whole number for one specific isotope.
- Subtracting the wrong way for neutrons: neutrons = A − Z, never Z − A.
- Changing the number of protons when an atom becomes an ion. Only electrons move.
- Saying isotopes have different chemical properties. Same electrons, same chemistry; only mass and nuclear stability differ.
- Thinking Rutherford's result was "most particles went through" (that was expected); the surprise was the few that bounced back.
- Forgetting conservation of mass includes gases: a candle "loses" mass only because CO₂ and water vapour leave.
