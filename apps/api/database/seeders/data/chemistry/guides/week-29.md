## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Oxidation | Loss of electrons; the oxidation number increases. |
| Reduction | Gain of electrons; the oxidation number decreases. |
| Redox reaction | A reaction in which electrons are transferred, so that at least one element's oxidation number rises and another's falls. Always both at once. |
| Oxidation number (state) | A bookkeeping charge assigned to each atom as if every bond were ionic. Real charges for monatomic ions; formal for atoms in molecules. |
| Oxidising agent | The species that takes electrons, and is itself reduced. |
| Reducing agent | The species that gives electrons, and is itself oxidised. |
| Half-reaction | One half of a redox reaction, showing either the oxidation or the reduction with its electrons written explicitly. |
| Net ionic equation | The balanced equation showing only the species that change, with spectator ions removed. |
| OIL RIG | Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons). |

## 1. Why we need a second kind of bookkeeping

When zinc metal sits in blue copper(II) sulfate solution, the blue fades, the zinc dissolves and a brown crust of copper grows on what is left. Atoms are conserved, and so is charge, yet something has moved: each zinc atom has given two electrons to a copper ion.

Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)

The zinc lost electrons: it was **oxidised**. The copper ion gained them: it was **reduced**. Because electrons cannot pile up or vanish, every oxidation is matched by a reduction; the combined process is a **redox** reaction. The word "oxidation" comes from reactions with oxygen, which remains the most familiar example (iron rusting, fuel burning), but the definition is about electrons, and oxygen need not be present.

To track electrons in reactions where no ions are visible, chemists assign every atom an **oxidation number**: the charge it would carry if every bond were fully ionic, with the shared electrons handed to the more electronegative atom (2.8). A change in oxidation number is the signature of electron transfer.

## 2. The rules for oxidation numbers

Apply the rules in order; the earlier rule wins when two conflict.

1. An atom in a pure element is 0: Fe, O₂, S₈, Cl₂ are all 0.
2. A monatomic ion has its charge: Na⁺ is +1, Cl⁻ is −1, Al³⁺ is +3.
3. Group 1 metals in compounds are +1; group 2 metals are +2; aluminium is +3.
4. Fluorine in compounds is −1.
5. Hydrogen in compounds is +1 (except in metal hydrides such as NaH, where it is −1).
6. Oxygen in compounds is −2 (except in peroxides such as H₂O₂, where it is −1, and when bonded to fluorine).
7. The sum of oxidation numbers equals the charge: 0 for a neutral compound, the ion's charge for a polyatomic ion.

Rule 7 lets you find the one unknown. Oxidation numbers can be fractions and can exceed the number of bonds; they are bookkeeping, not real charges.

**Worked example.** Sulfur in H₂SO₄: hydrogen 2 × (+1) = +2; oxygen 4 × (−2) = −8; the compound is neutral, so S = 0 − 2 + 8 = **+6**. Chromium in Cr₂O₇²⁻: oxygen 7 × (−2) = −14; the ion is −2, so 2Cr = −2 + 14 = +12 and each Cr is **+6**. Manganese in MnO₄⁻: −2 + 8 = **+7**. Nitrogen in HNO₃: +1 + N − 6 = 0, so N = **+5**. Carbon in CH₄: +4 from hydrogen must be balanced, so C = **−4**; in CO₂, C = **+4**. Oxygen in H₂O₂: hydrogen +2, so two oxygens share −2 and each is **−1**.

**Try it.** Find the oxidation number of (a) P in PO₄³⁻, (b) Cl in ClO₃⁻, (c) Fe in Fe₃O₄. (Answers: (a) +5; (b) +5; (c) +8/3, an average because Fe₃O₄ contains both Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺.)

## 3. Spotting redox and naming the players

Assign oxidation numbers to every atom on both sides. If any changed, the reaction is redox; if none changed, it is not.

- 2Na(s) + Cl₂(g) → 2NaCl(s): Na goes 0 → +1 (oxidised), Cl goes 0 → −1 (reduced). Redox.
- AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq): Ag stays +1, Cl stays −1, everything else unchanged. Not redox (a precipitation).
- HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O: no changes. Not redox (neutralisation).
- CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O: C goes −4 → +4, O goes 0 → −2. Redox (combustion).

From unit 4: every single replacement, every combustion and every synthesis or decomposition involving a free element is redox. Double replacements and acid–base reactions are not.

The vocabulary trips people up, so set it out:

| Species | What happens to it | Electrons | Oxidation number | It is the … |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Zn in Zn + Cu²⁺ | Oxidised | Loses | Rises (0 → +2) | Reducing agent |
| Cu²⁺ in Zn + Cu²⁺ | Reduced | Gains | Falls (+2 → 0) | Oxidising agent |

The **oxidising agent** does the oxidising to something else, and in the process is itself reduced. The **reducing agent** is itself oxidised. Name the agent as the whole species in the equation (Cu²⁺, or MnO₄⁻, or O₂), not just the atom whose number changed.

**Worked example.** In 2KClO₃ → 2KCl + 3O₂, chlorine goes +5 → −1 (reduced) and oxygen goes −2 → 0 (oxidised). Both changes happen inside the same compound, so KClO₃ is both the oxidising and the reducing agent.

## 4. Half-reactions

Splitting a redox reaction into its two halves makes the electrons visible:

Oxidation: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ (electrons on the right: lost)
Reduction: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (electrons on the left: gained)

Each half must balance in atoms and in charge; the electrons are what make the charge balance. Here both halves involve two electrons, so adding them cancels the electrons directly.

When the counts differ, multiply. For aluminium reducing copper(II):

Al → Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ (×2 gives 6e⁻)
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (×3 gives 6e⁻)
Sum: 2Al + 3Cu²⁺ → 2Al³⁺ + 3Cu

Check: 2 Al, 3 Cu on each side; charge +6 on each side. Electrons transferred: 6 per equation as written.

## 5. Half-reactions in acidic solution

Many oxidising agents are oxygen-rich ions such as permanganate, MnO₄⁻, and dichromate, Cr₂O₇²⁻. Their half-reactions need water and hydrogen ions to balance the oxygen and hydrogen. The procedure, for acidic solution:

1. Balance every element except O and H.
2. Balance O by adding H₂O to the side that needs oxygen.
3. Balance H by adding H⁺ to the other side.
4. Balance charge by adding electrons to the more positive side.

**Worked example.** Permanganate to manganese(II) in acid.

MnO₄⁻ → Mn²⁺. Mn is balanced. Four oxygens on the left need 4H₂O on the right: MnO₄⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O. Eight hydrogens on the right need 8H⁺ on the left: MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O. Charge: left −1 + 8 = +7; right +2. Add 5e⁻ to the left: **MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O**. Manganese went from +7 to +2, a gain of five electrons, which agrees.

Now combine with iron(II) being oxidised, Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻. Multiply the iron half by 5 and add:

**MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5Fe²⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O + 5Fe³⁺**

Check atoms (Mn 1, O 4, H 8, Fe 5) and charge (left: −1 + 8 + 10 = +17; right: +2 + 15 = +17). This is the reaction behind a permanganate titration: the purple MnO₄⁻ turns colourless Mn²⁺, and it is its own indicator.

**Try it.** Balance Cu + NO₃⁻ → Cu²⁺ + NO₂ in acid. (Answer: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻; NO₃⁻ + 2H⁺ + e⁻ → NO₂ + H₂O, doubled; sum Cu + 2NO₃⁻ + 4H⁺ → Cu²⁺ + 2NO₂ + 2H₂O; the brown gas you see when copper meets concentrated nitric acid.)

## 6. Counting electrons

Because a half-reaction shows the electrons, you can count them like any other species in stoichiometry. Oxidising 0.600 mol of aluminium to Al³⁺ releases 0.600 × 3 = 1.80 mol of electrons. Reducing 0.500 mol of Fe³⁺ to iron metal needs 1.50 mol of electrons. Next week those moles of electrons become coulombs through a battery, and the mole count becomes the amount of metal plated in electrolysis.

**Worked example.** What mass of copper is deposited when 3.27 g of zinc dissolves in copper(II) sulfate? Zinc: 3.27 g ÷ 65.38 g/mol = 0.0500 mol. The equation is 1:1, so 0.0500 mol of copper forms: 0.0500 mol × 63.55 g/mol = **3.18 g**. The 0.100 mol of electrons transferred never appears as a substance, but it is why the ratio is 1:1: each zinc gives two, each copper ion takes two.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating oxidation numbers as real charges.** S is not really +6 in sulfuric acid; the number is a device for counting electron shifts.
- **Forgetting the subscript.** The −2 of oxygen counts four times in SO₄²⁻.
- **Hydrogen peroxide and metal hydrides.** O is −1 in peroxides; H is −1 in NaH.
- **Swapping agent and process.** The oxidising agent is reduced. If you say "Cu²⁺ is reduced, so Cu²⁺ is the oxidising agent," you have it right.
- **Naming the atom instead of the species.** The oxidising agent is MnO₄⁻, not "Mn."
- **Adding half-reactions with unequal electrons.** Multiply first so the electrons cancel exactly.
- **Checking atoms but not charge.** A balanced redox equation balances both.
- **Putting H⁺ and H₂O on the wrong sides.** Water goes where oxygen is short; H⁺ goes on the opposite side.
