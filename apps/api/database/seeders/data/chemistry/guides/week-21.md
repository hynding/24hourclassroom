## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Solution | A homogeneous mixture; one phase throughout, with particles too small to see or filter. |
| Solute / solvent | The substance dissolved / the substance doing the dissolving (the one present in larger amount; water in an aqueous solution). |
| Solubility | The maximum mass of solute that dissolves in a given amount of solvent at a given temperature, often in g per 100 g of water. |
| Saturated | Holding as much dissolved solute as it can at that temperature; extra solute stays undissolved, in equilibrium with the solution. |
| Unsaturated | Holding less than the maximum; more solute can dissolve. |
| Supersaturated | Holding more than the equilibrium maximum, made by cooling a saturated solution carefully; unstable, crystallises when disturbed. |
| Solubility curve | A graph of solubility against temperature; a point on the line is saturated, below it unsaturated, above it supersaturated. |
| Miscible | Two liquids that dissolve in each other in any proportion (ethanol and water). |
| Henry's law | The solubility of a gas is proportional to its partial pressure above the liquid. |
| Molarity (M) | Moles of solute per litre of solution: M = n/V. |
| Dilution | Adding solvent to lower the concentration; moles of solute stay fixed, so M₁V₁ = M₂V₂. |
| Stock solution | A concentrated solution kept for diluting to working concentrations. |

## What dissolves in what (6.1)

Dissolving is a competition of attractions. For a solute to disperse in a solvent, solvent–solute attractions must be strong enough to replace the solute–solute attractions being broken and the solvent–solvent attractions that must open up to make room. The rule of thumb **like dissolves like** follows: polar and ionic solutes dissolve in polar solvents; nonpolar solutes dissolve in nonpolar solvents.

- Sodium chloride in water: water's partial charges pull Na⁺ and Cl⁻ out of the crystal and surround each ion (hydration), oxygen ends toward Na⁺ and hydrogen ends toward Cl⁻. The ion–dipole attractions pay for breaking the ionic lattice.
- Sugar in water: sucrose has eight O–H groups that hydrogen-bond with water, so it dissolves without forming ions.
- Oil in water: oil's hydrocarbon chains have only dispersion forces. Water would have to break hydrogen bonds to surround them and gets nothing comparable in return, so the oil stays separate.
- Oil in hexane: dispersion forces on both sides; they mix freely.

Three things speed up dissolving without changing how much eventually dissolves: stirring, heating (for most solids) and crushing the solute to expose more surface. Solubility itself is an equilibrium amount, a property of the solute–solvent pair and the temperature.

### Solubility of solids: the solubility curve

Most solids dissolve more at higher temperatures, but by very different amounts. Classroom solubility values in g per 100 g of water:

| Temperature (°C) | KNO₃ | NaCl | KCl | Ce₂(SO₄)₃ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 13 | 35.7 | 28 | 20 |
| 20 | 32 | 36.0 | 34 | 10 |
| 40 | 64 | 36.6 | 40 | 6 |
| 60 | 110 | 37.3 | 46 | 4 |
| 80 | 169 | 38.4 | 51 | 3 |

Potassium nitrate's solubility rises thirteenfold from 0 to 80 °C; sodium chloride's barely moves; cerium sulfate's falls. Reading the curve answers three question types:

1. **Is it saturated?** Compare the amount present with the curve value at that temperature. 50 g of KNO₃ in 100 g of water at 40 °C: the curve says 64 g, so it is unsaturated; 14 g more would saturate it.
2. **How much crystallises on cooling?** A saturated solution of KNO₃ in 100 g of water at 60 °C holds 110 g. Cooled to 20 °C it can hold only 32 g, so 110 − 32 = **78 g crystallises**. This is how recrystallisation purifies a solid.
3. **Scale to other amounts of water.** Solubility is per 100 g of water, so for 250 g of water multiply by 2.5: KNO₃ at 40 °C, 64 × 2.5 = 160 g.

**Worked example.** 80 g of KCl is stirred into 200 g of water at 20 °C. Does it all dissolve? If the solution is then heated to 60 °C, is it saturated?

At 20 °C the solubility is 34 g per 100 g, so 68 g per 200 g: only 68 g dissolves and 12 g remains. At 60 °C the limit is 46 × 2 = 92 g, so all 80 g dissolves and the solution is unsaturated (12 g short of saturation).

**Try it.** How much NaCl crystallises when a saturated solution in 100 g of water cools from 80 °C to 0 °C?
*Answer:* 38.4 − 35.7 = 2.7 g. Cooling is a poor way to recover salt; evaporation is used instead.

### Solubility of gases

Gases behave the opposite way: **solubility falls as temperature rises** (warm soda goes flat; fish struggle in warm water) and **rises with pressure**. Henry's law says the amount of gas that dissolves is proportional to its partial pressure above the liquid, which is why a soda is bottled under several atmospheres of CO₂ and fizzes when the cap releases the pressure. Shaking does not change the solubility; it just speeds the escape.

## Molarity (6.2)

Chemists measure solutions by volume, so the most useful concentration unit counts moles of solute per litre of **solution** (not per litre of solvent):

M = n / V, with n in mol and V in L

A 0.500 M (read "0.500 molar") NaCl solution holds 0.500 mol of NaCl in every litre, 0.0500 mol in every 100 mL. The definition rearranges two ways: n = M × V, and V = n / M.

**Worked example.** What is the molarity of a solution made by dissolving 11.7 g of NaCl (58.44 g/mol) in enough water to make 250.0 mL?

1. Moles: 11.7 g ÷ 58.44 g/mol = 0.200 mol.
2. Litres: 250.0 mL = 0.2500 L.
3. M = 0.200 mol / 0.2500 L = **0.800 M**.

**Worked example.** How many grams of glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆, 180.16 g/mol) are in 150.0 mL of a 0.250 M solution?

n = M × V = 0.250 mol/L × 0.1500 L = 0.0375 mol; mass = 0.0375 × 180.16 = **6.76 g**.

**Worked example.** What volume of 2.00 M HCl contains 0.0500 mol of HCl?

V = n / M = 0.0500 mol ÷ 2.00 mol/L = 0.0250 L = **25.0 mL**.

### Making a solution of known molarity

To prepare 500.0 mL of 0.100 M CuSO₄·5H₂O (249.69 g/mol): n = 0.100 × 0.5000 = 0.0500 mol; mass = 0.0500 × 249.69 = 12.48 g. Weigh 12.48 g into a 500 mL volumetric flask, add water to dissolve it, then add water **to the mark**. The last step is the one students skip: adding 500 mL of water would give slightly more than 500 mL of solution and a molarity a little below 0.100.

### Ion concentrations

Molarity of a dissolved ionic compound tells you the ion concentrations through the formula. 0.10 M CaCl₂ is 0.10 M in Ca²⁺ and 0.20 M in Cl⁻. 0.25 M Na₂SO₄ is 0.50 M in Na⁺ and 0.25 M in SO₄²⁻.

**Try it.** Molarity of 4.00 g of NaOH (40.00 g/mol) in 500.0 mL of solution? Concentration of OH⁻?
*Answer:* 0.100 mol / 0.5000 L = 0.200 M; [OH⁻] = 0.200 M.

## Dilution (6.2)

Diluting a solution adds solvent and leaves the solute alone, so the moles before equal the moles after:

M₁V₁ = M₂V₂

Any volume unit works as long as both V's share it, because the units cancel. The equation is for **dilution only**; it says nothing about reactions (that is next week's titration arithmetic, where mole ratios enter).

**Worked example.** What volume of 12.0 M concentrated HCl is needed to make 250.0 mL of 0.500 M HCl?

V₁ = M₂V₂ / M₁ = (0.500 M × 250.0 mL) / 12.0 M = **10.4 mL**. Measure 10.4 mL of the concentrated acid and add it to water, then make up to 250.0 mL. (Acid into water, never water into acid: the heat released could spatter concentrated acid.)

**Worked example.** 50.0 mL of 3.00 M NaOH is diluted to 2.00 L. New molarity?

M₂ = M₁V₁ / V₂ = (3.00 × 0.0500 L) / 2.00 L = **0.0750 M**. The volume grew forty times, so the concentration fell forty times.

**Try it.** 25.0 mL of 0.800 M KCl is mixed with 75.0 mL of water. What is the new concentration, assuming the volumes add?
*Answer:* M₂ = (0.800 × 25.0)/100.0 = 0.200 M.

## Common mistakes

- Dividing by the volume of **water** instead of the volume of **solution**. Molarity is per litre of the final solution.
- Forgetting to convert millilitres to litres in M = n/V (a factor of 1,000 error).
- Reading a solubility curve for 100 g of water and forgetting to scale when the problem uses a different mass of water.
- Confusing rate of dissolving (stirring, crushing) with solubility (an equilibrium amount).
- Thinking gases dissolve better in warm water. The opposite is true.
- Using M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ for a reaction between two different solutions. It is for dilution of one solute.
- Adding a fixed volume of water instead of making the solution up to the mark.

2. How much KNO₃ crystallises when a saturated solution in 100 g of water cools from 80 °C to 40 °C?
3. Molarity of 0.250 mol of KBr in 400.0 mL of solution?
