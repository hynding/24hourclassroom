## What you need to be able to do

- **4.7** Explain what a limiting reactant is and why the reactant present in the smaller mass is not necessarily the one that limits.
- **4.7** Identify the limiting reactant from the moles of each reactant and the mole ratio.
- **4.7** Calculate the theoretical yield from the limiting reactant, and the amount of excess reactant left over.
- **4.7** Define theoretical, actual and percent yield, calculate any one from the other two, and explain why actual yields fall short of theoretical.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Limiting reactant | The reactant that is completely consumed first; it determines the maximum amount of product. |
| Excess reactant | A reactant that is not used up; some remains when the reaction stops. |
| Theoretical yield | The maximum mass of product calculated from the limiting reactant. |
| Actual yield | The mass of product really obtained in the experiment. |
| Percent yield | (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100%. |

## The idea: who runs out first (4.7)

A sandwich takes two slices of bread and one slice of cheese: 2 bread + 1 cheese → 1 sandwich. With 10 slices of bread and 3 slices of cheese you can make only 3 sandwiches; 4 slices of bread are left over. Cheese is the **limiting reactant**, bread the **excess reactant**, and 3 sandwiches is the **theoretical yield**. Notice that cheese limits even though there is "more" bread by count; what matters is the amount of each compared with the ratio the recipe demands.

Reactions behave exactly this way. 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O with 5.0 mol of H₂ and 2.0 mol of O₂: the hydrogen would need 2.5 mol of oxygen (5.0 × 1 ÷ 2), but only 2.0 mol is present. **Oxygen limits.** Product: 2.0 mol O₂ × (2 mol H₂O ÷ 1 mol O₂) = 4.0 mol H₂O. Hydrogen used: 4.0 mol; left over: 1.0 mol.

### A method that always works

1. Balance the equation.
2. Convert each reactant's mass to moles.
3. Pick one reactant and calculate how many moles of the *other* it would need, using the mole ratio. Compare with what you have. If you have less than needed, the other reactant is limiting; if you have more, the one you picked is limiting.
4. Use the **limiting** reactant's moles, and only those, to find the moles and then the mass of product (week 15's route). That mass is the theoretical yield.
5. If asked, find the excess left: moles of excess needed (from the limiting reactant) subtracted from moles of excess present, then convert to grams.

An equivalent shortcut for step 3: divide each reactant's moles by its own coefficient; the smaller result marks the limiting reactant. (5.0 ÷ 2 = 2.5 for H₂; 2.0 ÷ 1 = 2.0 for O₂; oxygen is smaller.) Use whichever you can explain.

**Worked example.** 10.0 g of aluminum reacts with 30.0 g of chlorine: 2 Al + 3 Cl₂ → 2 AlCl₃ (Al 26.98, Cl₂ 70.90, AlCl₃ 133.33 g/mol). Find the limiting reactant, the theoretical yield of AlCl₃ and the mass of excess reactant left.
Moles: Al = 10.0 ÷ 26.98 = 0.371 mol; Cl₂ = 30.0 ÷ 70.90 = 0.423 mol.
Chlorine needed by 0.371 mol Al: 0.371 × (3 ÷ 2) = 0.556 mol. Only 0.423 mol is present, so **Cl₂ is limiting**, even though there is three times its mass. (Shortcut: 0.371 ÷ 2 = 0.185; 0.423 ÷ 3 = 0.141; chlorine smaller.)
Product from Cl₂: 0.423 mol × (2 mol AlCl₃ ÷ 3 mol Cl₂) = 0.282 mol × 133.33 = **37.6 g AlCl₃** (theoretical yield).
Aluminum used: 0.423 × (2 ÷ 3) = 0.282 mol × 26.98 = 7.61 g. Left over: 10.0 − 7.61 = **2.4 g Al**.
Conservation check: 7.61 g Al + 30.0 g Cl₂ = 37.6 g AlCl₃. ✓

**Worked example.** 28.0 g of N₂ and 9.00 g of H₂ react: N₂ + 3 H₂ → 2 NH₃.
N₂: 28.0 ÷ 28.02 = 0.999 mol; H₂: 9.00 ÷ 2.016 = 4.46 mol. Nitrogen needs 3 × 0.999 = 3.00 mol H₂; 4.46 is present, so **N₂ limits**. NH₃: 0.999 × 2 = 2.00 mol × 17.03 = **34.0 g**. Hydrogen left: 4.46 − 3.00 = 1.46 mol = 2.95 g.

**Try it.** 10.0 g of zinc is heated with 5.00 g of sulfur: Zn + S → ZnS (Zn 65.38, S 32.06, ZnS 97.44). Which limits, and what is the theoretical yield?
*Answer:* Zn 0.153 mol; S 0.156 mol. The ratio is 1 : 1, so the smaller mole count, **zinc**, limits. ZnS: 0.153 mol × 97.44 = **14.9 g**. Sulfur left: 0.003 mol, about 0.1 g.

**Try it.** 10.0 g of CaCO₃ and 5.00 g of HCl: CaCO₃ + 2 HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂ (CaCO₃ 100.09, HCl 36.46, CO₂ 44.01). Mass of CO₂?
*Answer:* CaCO₃ 0.0999 mol needs 0.200 mol HCl; only 0.137 mol present → **HCl limits**. CO₂: 0.137 × (1 ÷ 2) = 0.0686 mol × 44.01 = **3.02 g**.

The mass of a reactant tells you nothing by itself; a small mass of a light substance can be many moles, and the coefficients may demand several moles of one reactant per mole of the other. Always go to moles and always use the ratio.

## Theoretical, actual and percent yield (4.7)

The calculation gives the **theoretical yield**: the most product the limiting reactant can possibly make. A real experiment gives the **actual yield**, measured on a balance, and it is almost always less. **Percent yield** compares them:

percent yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100%.

**Worked example.** In the aluminum-chlorine reaction the theoretical yield was 37.6 g of AlCl₃. A student recovers 30.1 g. Percent yield = 30.1 ÷ 37.6 × 100% = **80.1%**.

**Worked example (working backwards).** A synthesis has a known 65.0% yield. What mass of product should a chemist expect from a theoretical yield of 14.9 g? Actual = 0.650 × 14.9 = **9.69 g**. And if 12.0 g of product is needed, the theoretical yield must be 12.0 ÷ 0.650 = 18.5 g, so more reactant must be used.

Why is the actual yield less than 100%?

- Some product stays behind: on the filter paper, dissolved in the solvent, stuck to the glassware.
- The reaction does not go to completion, or stops at an equilibrium (Unit 8).
- Side reactions consume reactant to make other products.
- Impure reactants: 10.0 g of 95% pure sample contains only 9.5 g of the reactant.
- Transfer and weighing losses at each step.

A yield **above 100%** means the product is impure or wet (water adds mass), or an error was made; it never means extra product was created. Mass is conserved.

**Try it.** 10.0 g of zinc and excess sulfur gave 12.8 g of ZnS. Percent yield?
*Answer:* Theoretical 14.9 g (above); 12.8 ÷ 14.9 × 100% = **85.9%**.

Percent yield is the number a chemist or a manufacturer watches most closely. A process with a 40% yield wastes more than half its raw material; raising it to 80% doubles the output for the same input. Yield, not speed, is often what decides whether a synthesis is worth running.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing the limiting reactant by the smaller **mass** (or the smaller mole count when the ratio is not 1 : 1). Compare moles *through the ratio*.
- Calculating the product from the excess reactant. Only the limiting reactant's moles give the theoretical yield.
- Adding the two reactants' products together. The reaction makes one amount of product, set by the limiting reactant.
- Using the theoretical yield as the "actual" value, or inverting the percent yield fraction (a value over 100% is a warning).
- Forgetting to convert the excess reactant's leftover **moles** to grams when grams are asked for.
- Treating a percent yield as a percent composition; they measure different things.
