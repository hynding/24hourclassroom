## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Arrhenius acid / base | Produces H⁺ (as H₃O⁺) in water / produces OH⁻ in water. |
| Brønsted–Lowry acid / base | A proton (H⁺) donor / a proton acceptor. Wider: works outside water and for bases without OH⁻, such as NH₃. |
| Hydronium ion, H₃O⁺ | A proton attached to a water molecule; "H⁺(aq)" and "H₃O⁺" mean the same thing. |
| Conjugate pair | Two species that differ by one proton: HCl/Cl⁻, NH₄⁺/NH₃, H₂O/OH⁻. |
| Amphoteric | Can act as either acid or base; water is the classic case. |
| Strong acid / base | Ionises completely in water: 0.10 M HCl is 0.10 M in H⁺. |
| Weak acid / base | Ionises only partly; most molecules stay intact. Acetic acid is about 1% ionised at 0.1 M. |
| K_w | The ion-product constant of water, [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C. |
| pH / pOH | −log[H⁺] / −log[OH⁻]; pH + pOH = 14.00 at 25 °C. |
| Neutral | [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M; pH 7.00 at 25 °C. |
| Indicator | A dye whose colour depends on pH: litmus (red in acid, blue in base), phenolphthalein (colourless below pH 8.2, pink above). |

## What acids and bases are (6.4)

**Acids** taste sour, turn blue litmus red, conduct electricity in solution, react with active metals to release hydrogen, and react with carbonates to release carbon dioxide. **Bases** taste bitter, feel slippery, turn red litmus blue, conduct electricity, and neutralise acids. These properties were the definitions for centuries; the modern definitions explain them.

**Arrhenius (1884):** an acid releases hydrogen ions in water and a base releases hydroxide ions.

HCl(g) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)
NaOH(s) → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

The sour taste, the litmus change and the reactions with metals and carbonates are all properties of H₃O⁺; the slipperiness and the other litmus change are properties of OH⁻.

**Brønsted–Lowry (1923):** an acid donates a proton and a base accepts one. This definition covers ammonia, which has no OH⁻ yet makes a basic solution:

NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

Water donates a proton to ammonia, so water is the acid here and ammonia the base. In the HCl equation water accepts a proton, so water is the base there. Water is **amphoteric**. Every Brønsted–Lowry reaction has two **conjugate pairs**, each pair differing by exactly one H⁺: in the ammonia equation, NH₃/NH₄⁺ and H₂O/OH⁻. The acid on the left becomes the conjugate base on the right, and the base on the left becomes the conjugate acid on the right.

**Worked example.** Identify the conjugate pairs in HNO₂(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NO₂⁻(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq).
HNO₂ donates a proton to become NO₂⁻ (acid / conjugate base). H₂O accepts it to become H₃O⁺ (base / conjugate acid).

### Strong and weak

Strength is about **extent of ionisation**, not concentration. A strong acid ionises completely, so every HCl molecule becomes H₃O⁺ and Cl⁻. Memorise the strong acids: **HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄, HClO₄** (and HClO₃). Every other common acid is weak: acetic acid CH₃COOH, carbonic acid H₂CO₃, hydrofluoric acid HF, phosphoric acid H₃PO₄. A weak acid's equation is written with ⇌ because the ionisation stops at a small fraction:

CH₃COOH(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq)

Strong bases are the soluble hydroxides: **LiOH, NaOH, KOH** (and the heavier group 1 hydroxides) plus **Ca(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂, Ba(OH)₂**. Ammonia is the standard weak base.

"Concentrated" and "dilute" describe how much is dissolved; "strong" and "weak" describe how completely it ionises. Concentrated acetic acid (vinegar is 5%; glacial is 100%) is still weak; 0.001 M HCl is dilute but strong.

### Reactions of acids

- With active metals (above hydrogen in the activity series): Zn(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → ZnCl₂(aq) + H₂(g).
- With carbonates: CaCO₃(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g). The fizz test for limestone and the reason antacids work.
- With bases (neutralisation, next week): HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l). The net ionic equation for any strong acid–strong base reaction is H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l).

**Try it.** Write the equation for magnesium reacting with sulfuric acid.
*Answer:* Mg(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → MgSO₄(aq) + H₂(g).

## Water's self-ionisation and K_w (6.5)

Even pure water contains a few ions, because occasionally one water molecule passes a proton to another:

2 H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

At 25 °C the product of the two concentrations is fixed:

K_w = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴

In pure water [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M. Add acid and [H⁺] rises, so [OH⁻] must fall to keep the product constant; add base and the reverse. Every aqueous solution contains both ions; "acidic" means [H⁺] > [OH⁻], "basic" means [OH⁻] > [H⁺].

**Worked example.** [OH⁻] in 0.0050 M HCl? [H⁺] = 0.0050 M (strong acid, complete ionisation), so [OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ / 5.0 × 10⁻³ = **2.0 × 10⁻¹² M**.

## The pH scale (6.5)

Concentrations of H⁺ run from about 10 M to 10⁻¹⁵ M, so chemists compress the range with a logarithm:

pH = −log[H⁺] and [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ
pOH = −log[OH⁻] and [OH⁻] = 10⁻ᵖᴼᴴ
pH + pOH = 14.00 at 25 °C

The scale runs backwards: a **lower** pH means **more** H⁺. Each unit is a factor of ten: pH 3 has ten times the [H⁺] of pH 4 and a hundred times that of pH 5. At 25 °C, pH 7 is neutral, below 7 acidic, above 7 basic.

| Solution | [H⁺] (M) | pH |
|---|---|---|
| 1 M HCl | 1 | 0 |
| gastric juice | ~10⁻² | ~2 |
| lemon juice | ~10⁻² to 10⁻³ | 2–3 |
| vinegar | ~10⁻³ | ~3 |
| black coffee | ~10⁻⁵ | ~5 |
| pure water (25 °C) | 10⁻⁷ | 7 |
| blood | 4 × 10⁻⁸ | 7.4 |
| household ammonia | ~10⁻¹¹ | ~11 |
| 1 M NaOH | 10⁻¹⁴ | 14 |

**Worked example (pH from a power of ten).** pH of 0.001 M HNO₃? [H⁺] = 1 × 10⁻³ M, so pH = **3.00**.

**Worked example (pH from any concentration).** pH of 0.025 M HCl? pH = −log(0.025) = −(−1.602) = **1.60**. On a calculator: enter 0.025, press log, change the sign.

**Worked example (strong base).** pH of 0.0020 M NaOH? [OH⁻] = 2.0 × 10⁻³ M. pOH = −log(0.0020) = 2.70. pH = 14.00 − 2.70 = **11.30**.

**Worked example (a base with two hydroxides).** pH of 0.010 M Ba(OH)₂? Each Ba(OH)₂ gives two OH⁻, so [OH⁻] = 0.020 M. pOH = 1.70; pH = **12.30**.

**Worked example (concentration from pH).** A soft drink has pH 3.20. [H⁺] = 10⁻³·²⁰ = **6.3 × 10⁻⁴ M**. On a calculator: enter −3.20, then 10ˣ.

**Worked example (significant figures).** pH is a logarithm, so only the digits after the decimal point carry the significant figures of the concentration. 0.025 M (two significant figures) gives pH 1.60 (two decimal places). The 1 in front just records the power of ten.

**Try it.** Find the pH of 0.0040 M HCl, the pOH of 0.0040 M KOH, and the pH of the KOH solution.
*Answers:* pH = −log(0.0040) = 2.40; pOH = 2.40; pH = 11.60.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a concentrated acid "strong" or a dilute acid "weak". Strength is extent of ionisation.
- Finding pH of a base by taking −log of the base concentration. That gives the pOH; subtract from 14.
- Forgetting that Ca(OH)₂ and Ba(OH)₂ give two OH⁻ per formula unit.
- Thinking pH 2 is "twice as acidic" as pH 4. It is 100 times.
- Assuming a solution with pH 7 contains no H⁺ or OH⁻; it contains 10⁻⁷ M of each.
- Treating a weak acid as fully ionised: 0.10 M acetic acid has a pH near 2.9, not 1.0. (Weak-acid calculations beyond that are not required this year.)
