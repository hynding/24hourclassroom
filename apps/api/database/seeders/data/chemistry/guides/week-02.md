## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Physical property | Observable or measurable without changing what the substance is: colour, density, melting point, conductivity, solubility. |
| Chemical property | Describes a substance's ability to change into another substance: flammability, reactivity with acid, tendency to rust. |
| Physical change | Changes form or state but not identity: melting, dissolving, cutting, crushing. |
| Chemical change | Produces one or more new substances: burning, rusting, baking, digestion. |
| Intensive property | Independent of sample size: density, temperature, boiling point, colour. |
| Extensive property | Depends on the amount: mass, volume, length, energy content. |
| SI base unit | Metre (length), kilogram (mass), second (time), kelvin (temperature), mole (amount), ampere (current), candela (light). |
| Derived unit | Built from base units: volume in m³ or L, density in g/cm³ or g/mL. |
| Significant figures | The digits in a measurement that are known with certainty plus one estimated digit. |
| Accuracy | How close a measurement is to the true or accepted value. |
| Precision | How close repeated measurements are to each other. |
| Percent error | |measured − accepted| ÷ accepted × 100. |

## Properties and changes (1.3)

A **physical property** can be observed or measured without turning the substance into something else. The density of aluminium, the colour of sulfur, the boiling point of water, whether copper conducts, whether sugar dissolves: measuring any of these leaves the substance as it was. A **chemical property** describes what a substance *can become*: iron rusts, methane burns, sodium reacts with water, gold does not react with most acids. You cannot see a chemical property without letting the change happen.

The same split applies to changes. A **physical change** alters appearance, shape or state while the substance keeps its identity. Ice melting, salt dissolving, paper being torn, iron being magnetised: in every case the same substance is still there, and the change can usually be reversed by physical means. A **chemical change** makes new substances with new properties. Burning paper gives carbon dioxide, water vapour and ash; nothing physical gets the paper back.

Signs that a chemical change has probably occurred:

- a gas forms (bubbles when no boiling is happening);
- a **precipitate** (an insoluble solid) forms when two clear solutions are mixed;
- a colour change that is not just mixing of colours;
- heat or light is given off, or the mixture gets cold on its own;
- a change that is hard to reverse.

None of these is proof on its own. Boiling water makes bubbles (physical). Dissolving ammonium nitrate in water makes the solution cold (physical). The real test is always: **is a new substance present?**

**Intensive and extensive.** Mass, volume and length depend on how much you have, so they are **extensive**. Density, boiling point, colour and conductivity are the same for a drop and a bucket, so they are **intensive**, and intensive properties identify substances. A ratio of two extensive properties (mass ÷ volume) is intensive, which is why density is so useful.

**Worked example.** Classify each as physical or chemical: (a) a silver spoon tarnishes black; (b) an ice cube shrinks in the freezer; (c) a cake rises in the oven; (d) ethanol evaporates.

*Solution.* (a) Chemical: the black layer is silver sulfide, a new substance. (b) Physical: ice sublimes to water vapour, still H₂O. (c) Chemical: baking powder releases CO₂ by a reaction, and proteins and starches change. (d) Physical: liquid ethanol becomes gaseous ethanol.

**Try it.** Which of these are intensive properties: mass of a gold ring, melting point of gold, volume of a gold bar, density of gold?
*Answer:* melting point and density are intensive; mass and volume are extensive.

## Units and prefixes (1.4)

A measurement without a unit is meaningless. Chemistry uses **SI** units: the metre (m), kilogram (kg), second (s), kelvin (K) and mole (mol) are the ones you will meet most. Volume is usually in litres (L) and millilitres (mL): 1 mL = 1 cm³, 1 L = 1,000 mL = 1 dm³. Mass is usually in grams in the lab.

| Prefix | Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| kilo- | k | 10³ (1 km = 1,000 m) |
| centi- | c | 10⁻² (100 cm = 1 m) |
| milli- | m | 10⁻³ (1,000 mL = 1 L) |
| micro- | µ | 10⁻⁶ |
| nano- | n | 10⁻⁹ |

Converting between prefixes is multiplying by a power of ten. 2.5 kg = 2,500 g. 350 mL = 0.350 L. 4.2 × 10⁻⁷ m = 420 nm. Scientific notation keeps big and small numbers readable: 0.000 052 g = 5.2 × 10⁻⁵ g.

Temperature in the lab is read in degrees Celsius, but gas laws and energy calculations use kelvin: **K = °C + 273.15** (273 is close enough for most classroom work). 25 °C = 298 K. A kelvin and a Celsius degree are the same size; only the zero differs.

## Significant figures (1.4)

Every measuring instrument has a limit. A ruler marked in millimetres lets you read 23.4 mm: the 2 and 3 are certain, the 4 is estimated between marks. The **significant figures** of a measurement are the certain digits plus that one estimated digit. Reporting 23.40 mm would claim a precision the ruler does not have.

**Counting significant figures.**

1. Every non-zero digit is significant: 4.73 has three.
2. Zeros *between* non-zero digits are significant: 4.073 has four.
3. Leading zeros are never significant: 0.0047 has two.
4. Trailing zeros are significant only if there is a decimal point: 4.700 has four; 4700 is ambiguous and is best written 4.7 × 10³ (two) or 4.700 × 10³ (four).
5. Exact numbers (counted objects, defined conversions such as 1,000 mL in 1 L) have unlimited significant figures and never limit a result.

**Rounding results.**

- **Multiplying or dividing:** the answer has as many significant figures as the measurement with the fewest. 2.4 × 3.567 = 8.56 → **8.6**.
- **Adding or subtracting:** the answer has as many *decimal places* as the measurement with the fewest. 12.11 + 0.3 + 1.045 = 13.455 → **13.5**.
- Round at the end, not after each step; carry a guard digit through the work.

**Worked example.** A beaker's mass is 48.22 g; with liquid it is 61.7 g. The liquid's volume is 13.60 mL. Find the mass of liquid and its density, each to the right number of significant figures.

*Solution.* Mass = 61.7 − 48.22 = 13.48 → the fewer decimal places is one (61.7), so **13.5 g**. Density = 13.5 g ÷ 13.60 mL = 0.9926 g/mL → three significant figures (13.5 limits): **0.993 g/mL**. Note that the subtraction cut the precision from four figures to three before the division happened.

**Try it.** Count the significant figures: (a) 0.00340, (b) 1,200, (c) 1.200 × 10³, (d) 100.0. Then compute 5.08 × 0.20.
*Answers:* (a) 3, (b) ambiguous, probably 2, (c) 4, (d) 4. 5.08 × 0.20 = 1.016 → **1.0** (two significant figures, set by 0.20).

## Accuracy, precision and error (1.4)

**Accuracy** is closeness to the true value; **precision** is closeness of repeated measurements to each other. A balance that reads 10.00, 10.01 and 10.00 g for a 12.00 g standard is precise but not accurate (probably miscalibrated). Readings of 11.4, 12.7 and 11.9 g average close to 12 but scatter widely: accurate on average, imprecise. Good measurement needs both.

**Percent error** compares a result to the accepted value:

percent error = |measured − accepted| ÷ accepted × 100

**Worked example.** A student finds the density of aluminium to be 2.58 g/cm³; the accepted value is 2.70 g/cm³. Percent error = |2.58 − 2.70| ÷ 2.70 × 100 = 0.12 ÷ 2.70 × 100 = **4.4%**.

**Try it.** Three measurements of a 25.00 mL volume read 24.1, 24.2 and 24.1 mL. Describe their accuracy and precision and find the percent error of the mean.
*Answer:* Precise (within 0.1 mL of each other) but not very accurate (all about 0.9 mL low; a systematic error such as a misread meniscus). Mean = 24.13 mL; percent error = 0.87 ÷ 25.00 × 100 = 3.5%.

## Common mistakes

- Treating "bubbles" as automatic proof of a chemical change. Boiling, fizzing from a shaken soda and bubbles of dissolved air leaving warm water are all physical.
- Calling dissolving a chemical change. Sugar dissolved in water is still sugar; evaporation gets it back.
- Counting leading zeros as significant (0.025 has two, not four).
- Using the significant-figure rule for addition when multiplying, or vice versa.
- Rounding at every step, so the final answer drifts.
- Forgetting that density is intensive: doubling the sample doubles mass and volume but leaves the ratio unchanged.
- Writing K = °C − 273 instead of + 273.
