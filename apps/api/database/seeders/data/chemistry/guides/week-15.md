- **4.6** Convert moles of one substance to moles of another (mole-to-mole).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Stoichiometry | The quantitative relationships among reactants and products in a chemical reaction. |
| Mole ratio | A conversion factor built from two coefficients of a balanced equation, e.g. 2 mol NH₃ ÷ 1 mol N₂. |
| Mole-to-mole | Converting an amount of one substance to the amount of another using only the mole ratio. |
| Mass-to-mass | Converting a mass of one substance to a mass of another via moles. |
| Given / wanted | The substance whose amount you know / the substance whose amount you are asked for. |
| Excess | More of a reactant than is needed; the other reactant then sets the amount of product (week 16). |

## The mole ratio (4.6)

Week 14 ended with this: the coefficients of 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O are a mole ratio. Two moles of hydrogen react with one mole of oxygen to make two moles of water. Any two coefficients can be paired into a conversion factor:

| Ratio | Reads as |
|---|---|
| 2 mol H₂ / 1 mol O₂ | hydrogen needed per mole of oxygen |
| 2 mol H₂O / 2 mol H₂ | water made per mole of hydrogen |
| 2 mol H₂O / 1 mol O₂ | water made per mole of oxygen |

The ratio is exact: it comes from counting atoms, so it has unlimited significant figures and never limits your answer.

**Worked example (mole-to-mole).** How many moles of ammonia form from 6.00 mol of hydrogen in N₂ + 3 H₂ → 2 NH₃, with excess nitrogen?
6.00 mol H₂ × (2 mol NH₃ ÷ 3 mol H₂) = **4.00 mol NH₃**.
Set it up so the given unit (mol H₂) cancels and the wanted unit (mol NH₃) remains.

**Worked example.** How many moles of oxygen are needed to burn 0.250 mol of propane, C₃H₈ + 5 O₂ → 3 CO₂ + 4 H₂O?
0.250 mol C₃H₈ × (5 mol O₂ ÷ 1 mol C₃H₈) = **1.25 mol O₂**.

**Try it.** In 4 Fe + 3 O₂ → 2 Fe₂O₃, how many moles of Fe₂O₃ form from 1.50 mol of iron? How many moles of O₂ are used?
*Answers:* 1.50 × (2 ÷ 4) = 0.750 mol Fe₂O₃; 1.50 × (3 ÷ 4) = 1.13 mol O₂.

The mole ratio is the only step where one substance turns into another. Everything else in a stoichiometry problem is week 14's grams-to-moles arithmetic, applied before and after.

## Mass-to-mass: the three-step route (4.6)

Balances weigh grams, not moles, so a real problem starts and ends in grams. The route always has the same three legs:

1. **Grams of given → moles of given** (divide by the given's molar mass).
2. **Moles of given → moles of wanted** (multiply by the mole ratio).
3. **Moles of wanted → grams of wanted** (multiply by the wanted's molar mass).

**Figure description:** four boxes in a row joined by arrows. "Grams of given" → "Moles of given" (arrow labelled "÷ molar mass of given") → "Moles of wanted" (arrow labelled "× mole ratio from the equation") → "Grams of wanted" (arrow labelled "× molar mass of wanted"). A bracket above the two middle boxes is labelled "the only place the equation is used". A crossed-out arrow runs directly from the first box to the last, labelled "no shortcut".

Written as one chain of conversion factors, the units cancel from left to right:

grams given × (1 mol given ÷ M given) × (mol wanted ÷ mol given) × (M wanted ÷ 1 mol wanted) = grams wanted.

**Worked example.** What mass of copper is produced when 5.40 g of aluminum reacts with excess copper(II) chloride? 2 Al + 3 CuCl₂ → 2 AlCl₃ + 3 Cu.
5.40 g Al × (1 mol Al ÷ 26.98 g) × (3 mol Cu ÷ 2 mol Al) × (63.55 g Cu ÷ 1 mol Cu)
= 0.2002 mol Al → 0.3002 mol Cu → **19.1 g Cu**.

**Worked example.** What mass of carbon dioxide is released when 50.0 g of calcium carbonate decomposes? CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂.
50.0 g ÷ 100.09 g/mol = 0.4996 mol CaCO₃ × (1 ÷ 1) = 0.4996 mol CO₂ × 44.01 g/mol = **22.0 g CO₂**.
And the calcium oxide: 0.4996 mol × 56.08 g/mol = 28.0 g. Check: 22.0 + 28.0 = 50.0 g, the mass we started with. Conservation of mass holds.

**Worked example (reactant needed).** What mass of chlorine is needed to convert 11.5 g of sodium completely to sodium chloride? 2 Na + Cl₂ → 2 NaCl.
11.5 g ÷ 22.99 g/mol = 0.500 mol Na × (1 mol Cl₂ ÷ 2 mol Na) = 0.250 mol Cl₂ × 70.90 g/mol = **17.7 g Cl₂**.
The salt formed: 0.500 mol NaCl × 58.44 = 29.2 g. Check: 11.5 + 17.7 = 29.2. ✓

**Worked example (a kilogram scale).** How much iron can be extracted from 1.000 kg of iron(III) oxide? Fe₂O₃ + 3 CO → 2 Fe + 3 CO₂.
1000 g ÷ 159.70 = 6.262 mol Fe₂O₃ × (2 mol Fe ÷ 1) = 12.52 mol Fe × 55.85 = **699 g Fe**. This is the 69.94% iron of week 14's percent composition, reached by a different road.

**Try it.** (a) Mass of water formed when 8.02 g of methane burns: CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O. (b) Mass of hydrogen released when 13.08 g of zinc reacts with excess HCl: Zn + 2 HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂. (c) Mass of glucose a plant can make from 88.0 g of CO₂: 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂.
*Answers:* (a) 8.02 ÷ 16.04 = 0.500 mol CH₄ → 1.00 mol H₂O → 18.0 g. (b) 13.08 ÷ 65.38 = 0.200 mol Zn → 0.200 mol H₂ → 0.403 g. (c) 88.0 ÷ 44.01 = 2.00 mol CO₂ → 0.333 mol glucose → 60.1 g.

## Reading a result (4.6)

A stoichiometry answer should survive two checks:

- **Direction.** If the wanted substance has the larger coefficient, you get more moles of it; if it has the larger molar mass, each mole weighs more. 5.40 g of aluminum gave 19.1 g of copper because copper atoms are heavier *and* the ratio is 3 : 2.
- **Conservation.** The total mass of reactants consumed equals the total mass of products formed. If you compute every substance in a reaction, the sums must agree (29.2 g of NaCl from 11.5 g Na and 17.7 g Cl₂).

A common trap is skipping the moles: 5.40 g Al × (3 ÷ 2) = 8.10 g is wrong, because coefficients count particles, not grams. Grams of different substances cannot be compared until each is turned into moles.

Significant figures follow week 2: the molar masses and mole ratio are not the limiting values; the measured mass is. 5.40 g (three figures) gives 19.1 g (three figures).

## Common mistakes

- Using an unbalanced equation. The mole ratio is wrong, so everything after it is wrong.
- Applying the mole ratio to grams instead of moles.
- Inverting the ratio (writing 2 mol Al ÷ 3 mol Cu when converting Al to Cu). Check that the given's unit cancels.
- Using the molar mass of the wrong substance in step 1 or 3.
- Reporting moles when grams were asked for (or the reverse). Read the question's unit.
- Forgetting that an element written as a diatomic molecule has that molar mass: Cl₂ is 70.90 g/mol, not 35.45.
