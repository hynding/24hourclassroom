## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Matter | Anything that has mass and takes up space. |
| Chemistry | The study of the composition, structure and properties of matter and the changes it undergoes. |
| Observation | A fact recorded with the senses or an instrument ("the liquid turned blue"). |
| Inference | A conclusion drawn from observations ("so copper ions are present"). |
| Hypothesis | A testable, tentative explanation or prediction, often written as "if … then … because …". |
| Theory | A well-tested explanation of *why* something happens, supported by much evidence (atomic theory). |
| Scientific law | A statement of *what* always happens, often as an equation, without explaining why (law of conservation of mass). |
| Independent variable | The one thing the experimenter deliberately changes. |
| Dependent variable | The thing measured to see the effect. |
| Controlled variables | Everything held the same so the result can be blamed on the independent variable alone. |
| Pure substance | Matter with a fixed composition and fixed properties: an element or a compound. |
| Element | A substance made of one kind of atom; it cannot be broken down chemically (Cu, O₂, S₈). |
| Compound | Two or more elements bonded in a fixed ratio; it has its own properties, unlike its elements (H₂O, NaCl). |
| Mixture | Two or more substances physically combined in any ratio; separable by physical means. |
| Homogeneous mixture (solution) | Uniform throughout; one visible phase (salt water, air, brass). |
| Heterogeneous mixture | Not uniform; visibly different parts (sand and water, granite, a salad). |
| Alloy | A homogeneous mixture of metals (brass is copper and zinc). |

## What chemistry is, and how chemists find things out (1.1)

Chemistry is the science of matter: what things are made of, how their parts are arranged, what properties follow from that, and how one kind of matter turns into another. A chemist looks at rust on a bicycle and asks three questions in turn: what is it (iron oxide), how did it form (iron reacting with oxygen and water), and how could it be prevented (a coating that keeps oxygen away). Those three questions, composition, change and control, are the whole subject in miniature.

Chemists answer such questions by investigation, not by authority. The **scientific method** is less a rigid recipe than a loop:

1. **Observe** something and **ask a question** about it.
2. **Form a hypothesis**, a tentative answer that can be tested and could turn out wrong.
3. **Design an experiment** in which only one factor changes.
4. **Collect data**, both qualitative (colour, smell, bubbles) and quantitative (mass, temperature, volume).
5. **Analyse** the data and **conclude** whether the hypothesis is supported.
6. **Communicate** the result so others can repeat it, and go round again.

A good hypothesis is specific and falsifiable. "Salt affects the boiling point of water" is too vague to test. "If 10 g of salt is dissolved in 100 g of water, then the boiling point will be higher than 100 °C, because dissolved particles interfere with boiling" names what will be changed, what will be measured and why.

**Variables.** In that experiment, the **independent variable** is the mass of salt added (you choose it). The **dependent variable** is the boiling point (you measure it). The **controlled variables** are the mass of water, the pressure, the heater, the thermometer and the container. A **control** trial uses plain water so the salted result has something to be compared with. If two things change at once, you cannot tell which one caused the effect.

**Theory versus law.** A **law** summarises what is always observed: mass is conserved in a chemical reaction. A **theory** explains why: atoms are rearranged but never created or destroyed. A theory never "grows up" to become a law; they answer different questions. Both are supported by evidence, and both can be refined.

**Observation versus inference.** "The flame turned green" is an observation. "Copper is present" is an inference, and inferences can be wrong (barium also gives a green flame). Keep the two separate in a lab notebook.

**Worked example.** A student wants to know whether the temperature of water affects how fast a sugar cube dissolves. Identify the variables and one weakness in this plan: "I put a cube in hot water from the tap and one in cold water from the fridge and timed them."

*Solution.* Independent: water temperature. Dependent: time for the cube to dissolve. Controlled: volume of water, cube size, stirring, container. Weakness: the temperatures were never measured, so "hot" and "cold" are not reproducible, and a single trial of each cannot show whether the difference is bigger than the random scatter. Measure the temperature with a thermometer, use the same volume, and run three trials at each temperature.

**Try it.** A gardener claims that plants watered with cold tea grow taller. Write a testable hypothesis and name the three kinds of variable.
*Answer:* "If seedlings are watered with cold tea instead of water for four weeks, then they will be taller, because tea contains dissolved minerals." Independent: the liquid used (tea or water). Dependent: height after four weeks. Controlled: plant type, soil, light, volume of liquid, temperature.

## Sorting matter (1.2)

Everything around you is either a **pure substance** or a **mixture**. The test is composition. A pure substance has one fixed composition, so every sample has the same properties: all pure water freezes at 0 °C and is 11.2% hydrogen by mass. A mixture can have any proportions: tea can be weak or strong, and it still counts as tea.

Pure substances split into two kinds:

- An **element** contains only one kind of atom. There are 118 known, each with a symbol on the periodic table. Some exist as single atoms (He, Ar), some as molecules of the same atom (O₂, N₂, Cl₂, S₈). Elements cannot be decomposed by chemical means.
- A **compound** contains two or more elements chemically bonded in a fixed ratio by mass. Water is always two hydrogen atoms to one oxygen atom. A compound's properties are nothing like its elements': sodium is a soft metal that reacts violently with water, chlorine is a poisonous yellow-green gas, and sodium chloride is table salt. Compounds can be broken into their elements only by a chemical change (electrolysis of water, for instance).

Mixtures split by uniformity:

- A **homogeneous mixture**, or **solution**, looks the same throughout and has a single phase. Salt water, vinegar, air, 14-karat gold and stainless steel are solutions; solutions can be solid, liquid or gas.
- A **heterogeneous mixture** has visibly distinct parts or regions: oil and water, sand in water, granite, a bowl of cereal with milk.

Two questions settle most cases. **Can it be separated by physical means?** If yes, it is a mixture. Boiling salt water drives off the water and leaves the salt, and no chemical change happened. Decomposing water into hydrogen and oxygen needs electricity and makes new substances, so water is not a mixture. **Is the ratio fixed?** If any proportion works, it is a mixture; if the ratio is fixed by formula, it is a compound.

### Separating mixtures

Each method uses a property that differs between the parts.

| Method | Property used | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Filtration | Particle size; one part is an insoluble solid | sand from water |
| Decanting | Density; layers or settled solid | pouring water off settled mud |
| Evaporation | One part is a volatile liquid, the other a dissolved solid | salt from salt water |
| Distillation | Different boiling points | pure water from salt water, ethanol from a water mixture |
| Chromatography | Different attraction to a surface versus a moving solvent | dyes in a marker ink |
| Magnetism | One part is magnetic | iron filings from sulfur powder |

**Worked example.** A beaker holds a mixture of sand, salt and iron filings. Plan a separation that recovers all three.

*Solution.* (1) Pass a magnet over the dry mixture to pull out the iron filings. (2) Add water and stir; the salt dissolves, the sand does not. (3) Filter: sand stays on the paper. (4) Evaporate the filtrate to leave the salt. Each step exploits one property that only one component has: magnetism, insolubility, then volatility of the water.

**Try it.** Classify each: (a) carbon dioxide, (b) a brass doorknob, (c) a chocolate-chip cookie, (d) neon in a sign, (e) orange juice with pulp.
*Answers:* (a) compound; (b) homogeneous mixture (alloy); (c) heterogeneous mixture; (d) element; (e) heterogeneous mixture (the pulp is a visible second phase; strained, pulp-free juice is close to homogeneous).

## A first taste of quantities

Classifying matter is qualitative, but chemistry is a measuring science and even this week has numbers. Mixtures are described by **mass percent**: the mass of one component divided by the total mass, times 100.

**Worked example.** A 50.0 g sample of brass contains 33.0 g of copper and the rest zinc. What is the mass percent of zinc?

*Solution.* Zinc mass = 50.0 − 33.0 = 17.0 g. Percent zinc = 17.0 ÷ 50.0 × 100 = **34.0%**. Check: copper is 66.0%, and 66.0 + 34.0 = 100.0.

Compounds, by contrast, have a composition fixed by their formula. Any sample of water is 11.2% hydrogen and 88.8% oxygen by mass; you will learn to calculate such numbers from molar masses in Unit 4. For now, remember the contrast: a mixture's percent is whatever you made it; a compound's percent is a property of the compound.

## Common mistakes

- Calling air an element or a compound. Air is a homogeneous mixture of N₂, O₂, Ar, CO₂ and water vapour; its composition varies from place to place.
- Thinking a homogeneous mixture must be a liquid. Alloys are solid solutions; air is a gaseous one.
- Assuming "pure" on a label means a pure substance. "Pure orange juice" is still a mixture.
- Treating a molecule like O₂ as a compound. A compound needs at least two *different* elements.
- Confusing a theory with a guess. In science, a theory is the best-supported explanation we have, not an untested idea.
- Changing two variables at once and then claiming cause and effect.
