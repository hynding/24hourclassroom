## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Organic compound | A compound of carbon, usually with hydrogen, often with oxygen, nitrogen or halogens. |
| Hydrocarbon | A compound of carbon and hydrogen only. |
| Alkane | A hydrocarbon with only single bonds, CₙH₂ₙ₊₂. Saturated. |
| Alkene | A hydrocarbon with at least one C=C double bond, CₙH₂ₙ. Unsaturated. |
| Alkyne | A hydrocarbon with a C≡C triple bond, CₙH₂ₙ₋₂. Unsaturated. |
| Structural isomers | Compounds with the same molecular formula but different arrangements of atoms. |
| Functional group | An atom or group of atoms that gives a family of compounds its characteristic reactions: −OH, −COOH, −NH₂. |
| Monomer | A small molecule that can join with many others to form a polymer. |
| Polymer | A very large molecule made of repeating units from monomers. |
| Addition polymerisation | Monomers with C=C bonds join by opening the double bond; nothing is lost. |
| Condensation polymerisation | Monomers join with the loss of a small molecule, usually water, between each pair. |

## 1. Why carbon has a chemistry of its own

Carbon has four valence electrons and forms four covalent bonds (3.3), and a carbon–carbon bond is strong (347 kJ/mol) and stable in air and water. So carbon can bond to carbon to carbon, in chains of any length, in branches and in rings, with single, double or triple bonds, and still have bonds left over for hydrogen and other elements. No other element does all of this. The result is that there are more known carbon compounds than compounds of all the other elements together, and every living thing is built from them.

Around each singly bonded carbon the four bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron (3.5), bond angle 109.5°. A chain of carbons is therefore a zigzag, not a straight line, even though we draw it straight.

## 2. Alkanes: the simplest hydrocarbons

A **hydrocarbon** contains only carbon and hydrogen. If every bond is single, it is an **alkane**, with formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂: each carbon in the chain has two hydrogens, plus one extra at each end.

| n | Name | Formula | Boiling point (°C) | State at 25 °C |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | methane | CH₄ | −162 | gas |
| 2 | ethane | C₂H₆ | −89 | gas |
| 3 | propane | C₃H₈ | −42 | gas |
| 4 | butane | C₄H₁₀ | −0.5 | gas |
| 5 | pentane | C₅H₁₂ | 36 | liquid |
| 6 | hexane | C₆H₁₄ | 69 | liquid |
| 7 | heptane | C₇H₁₆ | 98 | liquid |
| 8 | octane | C₈H₁₈ | 126 | liquid |
| 9 | nonane | C₉H₂₀ | 151 | liquid |
| 10 | decane | C₁₀H₂₂ | 174 | liquid |

The prefixes (meth-, eth-, prop-, but-, then the Greek numbers pent-, hex-, hept-, oct-, non-, dec-) are used throughout organic chemistry; learn them once. Alkanes are nonpolar, so the only forces between their molecules are London dispersion forces (3.6), and those grow with the size of the molecule. That is why the boiling point climbs steadily down the table: methane is a gas that must be cooled to −162 °C to liquefy, while candle wax, around C₃₀, is a solid.

Alkanes are **saturated**: every carbon holds as many hydrogens as it can, there are no double bonds to open, and they are unreactive except toward burning and a few other vigorous reagents.

## 3. Isomers

From butane onward, the same atoms can be joined in more than one way. C₄H₁₀ can be a straight chain of four carbons (butane, boiling point −0.5 °C) or a chain of three with the fourth carbon attached to the middle one (2-methylpropane, boiling point −12 °C). Same formula, different compounds: **structural isomers**. The branched isomer boils lower because it is more compact and its molecules cannot pack as closely, which weakens the dispersion forces.

C₅H₁₂ has three isomers: pentane, 2-methylbutane and 2,2-dimethylpropane. The number explodes with size: C₁₀H₂₂ has 75 and C₂₀H₄₂ has over 366,000.

The naming rule for a branch: find the longest continuous chain (that is the parent name), number it from the end nearer the branch, and name the branch as an alkyl group (methyl, CH₃; ethyl, C₂H₅) with its position number. Repeated branches take di-, tri-. So the five-carbon isomer with a single methyl on the second carbon of a four-carbon chain is 2-methylbutane.

**Try it.** Name the C₆H₁₄ isomer in which a four-carbon chain carries methyl groups on carbons 2 and 3. (Answer: 2,3-dimethylbutane.)

## 4. Alkenes and alkynes: unsaturated hydrocarbons

Replace one C–C single bond with a double bond and two hydrogens must go: **alkenes** have the formula CₙH₂ₙ and names ending in -ene. Ethene, C₂H₄, is the most produced organic chemical in the world; propene is C₃H₆. A triple bond gives an **alkyne**, CₙH₂ₙ₋₂, ending in -yne: ethyne, C₂H₂, is the fuel of the welding torch.

These are **unsaturated**: the double or triple bond can open to add more atoms. That makes them far more reactive than alkanes, and it gives the standard test. Shake a hydrocarbon with orange **bromine water**: an alkane leaves it orange; an alkene turns it colourless as bromine adds across the double bond, C₂H₄ + Br₂ → C₂H₄Br₂. Hydrogen adds the same way over a nickel catalyst, turning liquid vegetable oils (unsaturated) into solid margarine (more saturated). And, most importantly, alkene molecules can add to one another, which is where polymers come from (section 8).

## 5. Combustion and crude oil

Hydrocarbons are fuels because their combustion is strongly exothermic (7.1): strong C=O and O–H bonds replace weaker C–H and O=O bonds (7.3).

Complete combustion, with plenty of oxygen: C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O. Balance carbon first, then hydrogen, then oxygen last, as oxygen appears alone on the left and can take any coefficient.

Incomplete combustion, with limited oxygen: carbon monoxide (toxic, colourless, odourless) and soot (carbon) appear in place of some of the CO₂, and less energy is released. A yellow, smoky flame is the sign; a blue flame means complete combustion. 2C₃H₈ + 7O₂ → 6CO + 8H₂O is one possibility.

**Worked example.** What mass of CO₂ forms when 57.1 g of octane burns completely? Equation: 2C₈H₁₈ + 25O₂ → 16CO₂ + 18H₂O. Molar mass of octane: 8(12.01) + 18(1.008) = 114.22 g/mol. Moles: 57.1 ÷ 114.22 = 0.500 mol. CO₂: 0.500 × 8 = 4.00 mol × 44.01 g/mol = 176 g. Burning a kilogram of petrol releases about three kilograms of CO₂.

Crude oil is a mixture (1.2) of hundreds of hydrocarbons. **Fractional distillation** separates it by boiling point (1.3): the oil is vaporised at the bottom of a tall column that is cooler toward the top, and each fraction condenses where the temperature matches its boiling range: refinery gases at the top, then petrol (C₅ to C₁₀), kerosene, diesel, lubricating oils, and bitumen at the bottom. Demand for the short fractions exceeds supply, so long alkanes are **cracked**, heated with a catalyst to break them into a shorter alkane and an alkene, for example C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄. Cracking is both a source of petrol and the main source of the ethene that makes plastics.

## 6. Functional groups

Replace a hydrogen on a hydrocarbon with another atom or group and you get a new family of compounds whose chemistry is set by that group, the **functional group**. The carbon skeleton is the frame; the functional group does the reacting.

| Family | Group | Example | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alcohol | −OH | ethanol, C₂H₅OH | Names end -ol. Polar, hydrogen-bonds, so ethanol mixes with water and boils at 78 °C (ethane: −89 °C). |
| Carboxylic acid | −COOH | ethanoic acid, CH₃COOH | Names end -oic acid. Weak acids (6.4); ethanoic acid is the acid of vinegar. |
| Ester | −COO− | ethyl ethanoate, CH₃COOC₂H₅ | Made from an acid and an alcohol. Fruity smells; solvents; fats are esters. |
| Amine | −NH₂ | methylamine, CH₃NH₂ | Weak bases. The group in amino acids. |
| Halide | −Cl, −Br | chloroethane, C₂H₅Cl | Refrigerants, solvents; vinyl chloride is the PVC monomer. |

Two reactions to know:

**Oxidation of an alcohol.** Ethanol is oxidised, by acidified dichromate in the lab or by bacteria in an open bottle of wine, to ethanoic acid: C₂H₅OH + 2[O] → CH₃COOH + H₂O. Carbon's oxidation number rises (8.1); orange dichromate turns green. This is why wine turns to vinegar.

**Esterification.** A carboxylic acid and an alcohol, warmed with a little concentrated sulfuric acid as catalyst, join with the loss of water:

CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O

ethanoic acid + ethanol ⇌ ethyl ethanoate + water. The ester is named alcohol part first (ethyl) then acid part (ethanoate). It is a reversible reaction (7.6), and the reverse, hydrolysis, is how fats are broken down in digestion and in soap-making. Esters are the smells of pears, bananas and nail-varnish remover.

Fermentation makes the ethanol in the first place: yeast converts glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂, at about 35 °C without oxygen.

## 7. Addition polymers

Heat ethene under pressure with a catalyst and the double bonds open and link, each molecule joining the next, thousands of times:

n C₂H₄ → (−CH₂−CH₂−)ₙ

The product, **polyethene** (polythene), is a chain of up to 50,000 carbons with no double bonds left. The **monomer** is ethene; the **repeat unit** is −CH₂−CH₂−; the number of repeats n is the degree of polymerisation. Nothing is lost: the polymer's formula is exactly n times the monomer's. This is **addition polymerisation**, and any molecule with a C=C bond can do it:

| Monomer | Polymer | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ethene, CH₂=CH₂ | Polyethene (PE) | Bags, bottles, film |
| Propene, CH₂=CHCH₃ | Polypropene (PP) | Ropes, crates, car parts |
| Chloroethene (vinyl chloride), CH₂=CHCl | Poly(vinyl chloride) (PVC) | Pipes, window frames, cable insulation |
| Tetrafluoroethene, CF₂=CF₂ | PTFE | Non-stick coatings |
| Phenylethene (styrene) | Polystyrene | Packaging foam, cups |

**Worked example.** A polyethene molecule has a molar mass of 56,100 g/mol. How many ethene units does it contain? Ethene is 28.05 g/mol, so n = 56,100 ÷ 28.05 = 2,000.

## 8. Condensation polymers

A second route needs monomers with a reactive group at each end. A molecule with two −COOH groups (a dicarboxylic acid) and a molecule with two −OH groups (a diol) link by esterification at both ends, releasing a water molecule at every link, to give a **polyester** such as PET, the plastic of drink bottles and fleece. A diacid and a diamine (two −NH₂ groups) give **nylon**. Because a small molecule is lost at each join, the polymer's mass is less than the sum of its monomers, which is the test for condensation polymerisation.

Nature uses the same chemistry. Amino acids carry both an −NH₂ and a −COOH; linked head to tail with the loss of water they form **proteins**, condensation polymers with twenty different monomers. Starch and cellulose are condensation polymers of glucose.

## 9. Plastics: properties and problems

Polymer properties follow from structure. Long chains held only by dispersion forces (polyethene) slide past each other when warmed: these **thermoplastics** soften, can be moulded and remoulded, and are recyclable. Chains cross-linked by covalent bonds (bakelite, epoxy, vulcanised rubber) cannot slide: these **thermosets** are rigid, do not melt and cannot be recycled by remelting. Polar groups (PVC's chlorine, nylon's hydrogen-bonding amide links) raise strength and melting point.

The same unreactivity that makes alkanes good packaging makes polyalkenes nearly indestructible in the environment: no bacterium has enzymes for a chain of CH₂ groups, so polyethene persists for centuries and breaks only into smaller fragments, microplastics. Responses: sorting and mechanical recycling (limited by mixed polymers and degraded chains), chemical recycling by cracking plastics back to monomers, incineration for energy, and biodegradable polyesters such as polylactic acid, made from plant sugars and digestible by soil organisms. Condensation polymers can be hydrolysed back toward their monomers, which is why polyesters and nylons are the easier plastics to recycle chemically.

## Common mistakes

- **Wrong hydrogen count.** Alkanes CₙH₂ₙ₊₂, alkenes CₙH₂ₙ, alkynes CₙH₂ₙ₋₂. Hexane is C₆H₁₄, not C₆H₁₂.
- **Calling a branched isomer a different formula.** Isomers share the molecular formula; only the arrangement differs.
- **Balancing oxygen first.** Balance C, then H, then O.
- **Saying alkanes decolourise bromine water.** Only unsaturated hydrocarbons do.
- **Thinking a polymer has double bonds.** In addition polymerisation the double bond is used up; polyethene is fully saturated.
- **Forgetting the water in condensation polymerisation.** It is the defining feature.
- **Confusing ethanol with ethanoic acid.** −OH is the alcohol; −COOH is the acid; the acid is what vinegar contains.
- **Naming the ester backwards.** Alcohol part first (ethyl), acid part second (ethanoate).
