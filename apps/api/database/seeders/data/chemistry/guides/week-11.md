## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Intermolecular force (IMF) | An attraction between separate molecules; much weaker than a covalent bond. |
| Intramolecular force | The bond within a molecule (covalent). Breaking it is a chemical change. |
| London dispersion force | A weak attraction from momentary, shifting dipoles in electron clouds; present in all substances; grows with molecular size (more electrons). |
| Dipole-dipole force | Attraction between the δ+ end of one polar molecule and the δ− end of another. |
| Hydrogen bond | An especially strong dipole-dipole attraction between an H bonded to N, O or F and a lone pair on N, O or F of a neighbouring molecule. |
| Vapour pressure | The pressure of a vapour above its liquid in a closed container; higher when IMFs are weak. |
| Volatile | Evaporates readily; a substance with weak IMFs and high vapour pressure. |
| Viscosity | Resistance to flow; higher with stronger IMFs and larger molecules. |
| Surface tension | The tendency of a liquid surface to contract; high in water because of hydrogen bonding. |
| Metallic bond | The attraction between metal cations and the delocalised "sea" of valence electrons shared among them. |
| Alloy | A mixture of a metal with other elements, usually other metals, with properties tuned for a purpose. |
| Covalent-network solid | A solid in which every atom is covalently bonded to its neighbours in one giant structure (diamond, quartz). |
| Molecular solid | A solid of discrete molecules held by IMFs (ice, sugar, dry ice). |

## Forces between molecules (3.6)

When water boils, no O–H bonds break; what breaks is the attraction *between* water molecules. Those attractions are **intermolecular forces**, and although any one of them is far weaker than a covalent bond (a few kJ/mol to about 40 kJ/mol, against roughly 150 to 1,000 for bonds), they decide whether a substance is a gas, a liquid or a solid at a given temperature and how easily it evaporates.

### London dispersion forces

Electrons are never perfectly evenly spread. At any instant one side of a molecule may have slightly more electron density, giving a momentary dipole that induces a matching dipole in a neighbour; the two attract for an instant. These **London dispersion forces** exist between *all* molecules and atoms, including nonpolar ones and noble gases, and they are the *only* force between nonpolar molecules. Their strength grows with the number of electrons, so larger, heavier molecules have stronger dispersion forces. That is why the halogens go from gas (F₂, Cl₂) to liquid (Br₂) to solid (I₂), and why the alkane boiling points climb steadily:

| Alkane | CH₄ | C₂H₆ | C₃H₈ | C₄H₁₀ | C₅H₁₂ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boiling point (°C) | −162 | −89 | −42 | −1 | 36 |

Shape matters too: a long, thin molecule has more contact with its neighbours than a compact one of the same formula.

### Dipole-dipole forces

Polar molecules line up δ+ to δ−. This **dipole-dipole** attraction adds to the dispersion forces that are always present, so a polar molecule generally boils higher than a nonpolar one of similar size. Compare acetone (C₃H₆O, polar, boils at 56 °C) with butane (C₄H₁₀, nonpolar, similar mass, boils at −1 °C).

### Hydrogen bonding

When hydrogen is bonded to nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine, three small and very electronegative atoms, the H is left almost bare of electrons, a concentrated δ+ that is strongly attracted to a lone pair on N, O or F of a neighbouring molecule. This **hydrogen bond** is the strongest intermolecular force (typically 10–40 kJ/mol). It is not a covalent bond: it is an attraction between molecules.

The evidence is in the group 16 hydrides. By dispersion forces alone, boiling point should rise steadily with size: H₂S −60 °C, H₂Se −41 °C, H₂Te −2 °C. Water, the smallest, should boil near −80 °C. Instead it boils at 100 °C, because of hydrogen bonding. The same anomaly appears for NH₃ (−33 °C, against −88 °C for PH₃) and HF (20 °C, against −85 °C for HCl).

**Figure description:** a graph of boiling point (°C, vertical) against period of the central atom (2 to 5, horizontal) for the hydrides of groups 14, 15, 16 and 17. The group 14 line (CH₄ to SnH₄) rises smoothly. The group 15, 16 and 17 lines also rise from period 3 to 5, but their period-2 points (NH₃, H₂O, HF) sit far above the trend, with H₂O the highest at 100 °C.

Hydrogen bonding also explains why ice floats (the hydrogen-bonded network in ice is open, so ice is less dense than liquid water), why water has high surface tension and a high heat capacity, and why DNA's two strands hold together.

### Which force? A decision procedure

1. Is the substance ionic or metallic? Then it is not held by IMFs at all (see 3.7).
2. Is the molecule nonpolar? **Dispersion only.** Strength scales with size.
3. Is it polar? **Dispersion plus dipole-dipole.**
4. Does it have H bonded directly to N, O or F? **Dispersion plus dipole-dipole plus hydrogen bonding.**

**Worked example.** Rank by boiling point, lowest to highest: CH₄, CH₃OH, CH₃F. Explain.
CH₄ is nonpolar: dispersion only. CH₃F is polar (C–F): dispersion plus dipole-dipole. CH₃OH has an O–H bond: hydrogen bonding as well. Order: CH₄ (−162 °C) < CH₃F (−78 °C) < CH₃OH (65 °C).

**Worked example.** Ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH) and dimethyl ether (CH₃OCH₃) have the same formula, C₂H₆O. Which boils higher?
Ethanol has an O–H and can hydrogen-bond; the ether's hydrogens are all on carbon, so it cannot. Ethanol boils at 78 °C, dimethyl ether at −24 °C.

**Try it.** Name the strongest IMF in each: Br₂, HCl, NH₃, CO₂, H₂O₂.
*Answers:* Br₂ dispersion; HCl dipole-dipole; NH₃ hydrogen bonding; CO₂ dispersion (nonpolar); H₂O₂ hydrogen bonding.

### Properties that follow

- **Boiling and melting points** rise with IMF strength.
- **Vapour pressure** falls with IMF strength: weak forces let molecules escape easily (volatile liquids such as acetone), strong forces hold them in (water, glycerol).
- **Viscosity** rises with IMF strength and with molecular size and tangling; glycerol, with three O–H groups, pours slowly.
- **Surface tension** rises with IMF strength; water beads up and insects walk on it.

## Metallic bonding (3.7)

Metal atoms have few valence electrons and low ionization energies. In a piece of metal the valence electrons are not held by any one atom; they are **delocalised**, free to move throughout the whole sample, while the metal cations sit in an orderly lattice. The **electron-sea model** pictures positive ions in a sea of mobile electrons, and the attraction between them is the **metallic bond**.

The model explains every familiar metal property:

- **Electrical conductivity:** the mobile electrons carry current; metals conduct as solids and as liquids, unlike ionic compounds.
- **Thermal conductivity:** the same electrons carry kinetic energy quickly through the sample.
- **Malleability and ductility:** when a layer of cations is pushed sideways, the electron sea flows with it and keeps the attraction; nothing lines up like-charge against like-charge, so the metal bends rather than shattering. Compare the ionic crystal of week 8.
- **Lustre:** mobile electrons absorb and re-emit light of all visible frequencies, so polished metal is shiny.
- **Range of melting points:** more delocalised electrons and smaller cations mean stronger metallic bonding. Sodium (one valence electron) melts at 98 °C; aluminium (three) at 660 °C; iron at 1,538 °C; tungsten at 3,422 °C. Mercury, unusually, is a liquid (−39 °C).

**Alloys** are mixtures of metals (sometimes with a little nonmetal). Atoms of a different size disrupt the orderly layers, so the layers slide less easily: alloys are usually harder and stronger than the pure metal. Steel is iron with up to about 2% carbon; brass is copper and zinc; bronze is copper and tin; sterling silver is 92.5% silver with copper.

## The four kinds of solids (3.7)

| Type | Particles | Held by | Melting point | Hardness | Conducts? | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ionic | cations and anions | ionic bonds throughout a lattice | high (NaCl 801 °C) | hard, brittle | no as solid; yes molten or dissolved | NaCl, MgO, CaCO₃ |
| Molecular | molecules | intermolecular forces | low (ice 0 °C, sugar 186 °C, dry ice sublimes at −78 °C) | soft | no | H₂O, CO₂, C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁, I₂ |
| Covalent-network | atoms | covalent bonds in one giant structure | very high (diamond about 3,550 °C, quartz 1,710 °C) | very hard | no (graphite is the exception) | diamond, graphite, SiO₂, SiC |
| Metallic | metal cations and delocalised electrons | metallic bonds | wide range (Hg −39 °C to W 3,422 °C) | variable; malleable | yes, solid and liquid | Cu, Fe, Al, brass |

Two carbon solids show how structure rules properties. **Diamond** bonds every carbon to four others in a rigid tetrahedral network: the hardest natural substance, an insulator. **Graphite** bonds each carbon to three others in flat sheets with the fourth electron delocalised across the sheet; the sheets are held together only by dispersion forces, so they slide (pencil "lead", lubricant) and the delocalised electrons conduct electricity.

**Worked example.** An unknown solid melts at 1,710 °C, is very hard, does not conduct electricity as a solid or when molten, and does not dissolve in water. What kind of solid is it?
The melting point rules out molecular; no conduction when molten rules out ionic and metallic. Hard, high-melting, insulating: **covalent-network** (quartz, SiO₂, fits).

**Try it.** Classify: dry ice, copper wire, table salt, silicon carbide, candle wax, brass.
*Answers:* molecular; metallic; ionic; covalent-network; molecular; metallic (alloy).

## Common mistakes

- Saying boiling water breaks O–H bonds. It breaks hydrogen bonds between molecules; the molecules stay H₂O.
- Thinking only polar molecules have intermolecular forces. Dispersion forces act on everything and dominate for large nonpolar molecules (I₂ is a solid).
- Calling any bond involving hydrogen a "hydrogen bond". It needs H on N, O or F attracting a lone pair on N, O or F of another molecule; C–H does not count.
- Ranking boiling points by mass alone. Mass is a proxy for dispersion strength, but hydrogen bonding overrides it (water versus H₂S).
- Confusing metallic with ionic conductivity: metals conduct as solids because of free electrons; ionic compounds conduct only when the ions are free.
- Calling diamond a molecular solid. There are no molecules; the whole crystal is one bonded network.
