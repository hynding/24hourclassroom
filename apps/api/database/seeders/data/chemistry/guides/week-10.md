# Week 10 · Molecular Shape and Polarity

**Topics:** 3.5 Molecular Shape (VSEPR) and Polarity
**Practices:** 2 (developing and using models), 6 (constructing explanations), 7 (arguing from evidence)
**Spiral:** Week 9's Lewis structures are flat drawings. Real molecules are three-dimensional, and their shapes follow from one rule about electron pairs. Bond polarity (week 9) plus shape decides whether the whole molecule is polar, which week 11 uses to explain boiling points and solubility.

## What you need to be able to do

- **3.5** State the VSEPR idea (electron domains around a central atom spread out as far as possible) and count electron domains from a Lewis structure, treating a multiple bond as one domain.
- **3.5** Predict the electron-domain geometry and the molecular shape for two, three and four domains, with bond angles: linear, trigonal planar, bent, tetrahedral, trigonal pyramidal.
- **3.5** Explain why lone pairs compress bond angles.
- **3.5** Decide whether a molecule is polar by combining bond polarity with shape, and sketch the direction of the molecular dipole.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| VSEPR | Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion: electron domains around an atom arrange to be as far apart as possible. |
| Electron domain | A region of electron density: a lone pair, or one bond whether single, double or triple. |
| Electron-domain geometry | The arrangement of all domains (bonds and lone pairs): linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral. |
| Molecular shape | The arrangement of the atoms only, ignoring lone pairs: what the molecule looks like. |
| Bond angle | The angle between two bonds at the central atom. |
| Dipole | A separation of partial positive and partial negative charge; drawn as an arrow toward δ−. |
| Polar molecule | A molecule with a net dipole: polar bonds that do not cancel. |
| Nonpolar molecule | No net dipole: nonpolar bonds, or polar bonds arranged symmetrically so they cancel. |
| Symmetric / asymmetric | Whether the polar bonds around the central atom are balanced in every direction. |

## The one idea (3.5)

Electrons repel. Around a central atom, every bonding pair and every lone pair is a cloud of negative charge, and the clouds settle as far from one another as geometry allows. Count the **electron domains**: each lone pair is one domain, and each bond, single, double or triple, is one domain. The domain count fixes the geometry.

| Domains | Electron-domain geometry | Ideal angle |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | linear | 180° |
| 3 | trigonal planar | 120° |
| 4 | tetrahedral | 109.5° |

Then look at how many of the domains are atoms. The **molecular shape** names the arrangement of atoms alone; lone pairs are there, pushing, but invisible in the shape name.

| Domains | Bonding | Lone pairs | Molecular shape | Angle | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 2 | 0 | linear | 180° | CO₂, BeCl₂, HCN |
| 3 | 3 | 0 | trigonal planar | 120° | BF₃, SO₃, CH₂O |
| 3 | 2 | 1 | bent | about 118° | SO₂, O₃ |
| 4 | 4 | 0 | tetrahedral | 109.5° | CH₄, CCl₄, NH₄⁺ |
| 4 | 3 | 1 | trigonal pyramidal | about 107° | NH₃, PCl₃ |
| 4 | 2 | 2 | bent | about 104.5° | H₂O, H₂S |

Period 3 central atoms can have five or six domains (trigonal bipyramidal PCl₅, octahedral SF₆); recognise the names, but the course focuses on two to four.

**Lone pairs squeeze.** A lone pair is held by one nucleus only, so it spreads wider than a bonding pair and pushes harder. Methane's bonds sit at 109.5°; ammonia's one lone pair compresses the H–N–H angle to about 107°; water's two lone pairs compress H–O–H to about 104.5°.

**Figure description:** four ball-and-stick sketches. CO₂: a straight line, O–C–O. BF₃: boron at the centre of a flat triangle with fluorines at the corners. CH₄: carbon at the centre of a tetrahedron, two hydrogens in the plane of the page, one wedged toward the viewer, one dashed away. H₂O: oxygen with two hydrogens forming a V, and two lone pairs drawn as lobes above the oxygen pushing the hydrogens together.

### Working a shape

**Worked example (SO₂).** Lewis structure: 18 electrons; S central; one S=O double bond, one S–O single bond (resonance), and one lone pair on S. Domains on S: 2 bonds + 1 lone pair = 3 → trigonal planar electron geometry. Atoms: 2 → **bent**, angle a little under 120°.

**Worked example (NH₄⁺).** 5 + 4 − 1 = 8 electrons; four N–H bonds, no lone pairs. Four domains, four atoms → **tetrahedral**, 109.5°.

**Worked example (HCN).** 10 electrons; H–C≡N. On carbon: one single bond and one triple bond, no lone pairs → 2 domains → **linear**, 180°. The triple bond counts once.

**Try it.** Give the electron-domain geometry, molecular shape and approximate bond angle for PH₃, CCl₄ and OF₂.
*Answers:* PH₃: 4 domains (3 bonds, 1 lone pair), tetrahedral, trigonal pyramidal, about 107°. CCl₄: 4 domains, tetrahedral, tetrahedral, 109.5°. OF₂: 4 domains (2 bonds, 2 lone pairs), tetrahedral, bent, about 104°.

## From bond polarity to molecular polarity (3.5)

A polar bond is a small arrow pointing toward the δ− atom. Whether the molecule as a whole is polar depends on whether the arrows cancel. Two questions settle it:

1. **Are the bonds polar?** If every bond is nonpolar (H₂, Cl₂, CH₄ to a good approximation), the molecule is nonpolar; stop.
2. **Do the polar bonds cancel?** They cancel when the central atom has no lone pairs and every outer atom is the same (a **symmetric** molecule). They do not cancel when the central atom has lone pairs (bent, trigonal pyramidal) or when the outer atoms differ.

| Molecule | Bond polarity | Shape | Cancel? | Molecule |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CO₂ | polar C=O | linear, symmetric | yes | nonpolar |
| H₂O | polar O–H | bent | no | polar |
| BF₃ | polar B–F | trigonal planar, symmetric | yes | nonpolar |
| NH₃ | polar N–H | trigonal pyramidal | no | polar |
| CH₄ | nearly nonpolar C–H | tetrahedral | yes | nonpolar |
| CCl₄ | polar C–Cl | tetrahedral, symmetric | yes | nonpolar |
| CHCl₃ | polar C–Cl, C–H | tetrahedral, asymmetric | no | polar |
| HCl | polar H–Cl | linear (two atoms) | no | polar |

CO₂ is the classic case: two strongly polar bonds, but they point in exactly opposite directions and cancel. Bend the molecule, as in water, and the two arrows add to a net dipole pointing from the hydrogens toward the oxygen.

**Figure description:** two molecules drawn with arrows. CO₂: arrows from C to each O point left and right along the same line, equal and opposite, labelled "net dipole zero". H₂O: arrows from each H toward O point up-left and up-right; a larger arrow between them points straight up through the oxygen, labelled "net dipole".

**Worked example.** Is CH₃Cl polar? Four domains around carbon, tetrahedral shape; three C–H bonds are nearly nonpolar and one C–Cl bond is polar. The outer atoms differ, so nothing cancels the C–Cl arrow: **polar**, with the δ− end at chlorine.

**Worked example.** Is SO₃ polar? Three S=O domains, trigonal planar, no lone pairs on S, three identical outer atoms. The three arrows at 120° cancel: **nonpolar**. SO₂, with a lone pair and a bent shape, is polar.

**Try it.** Classify as polar or nonpolar: PCl₃, SiH₄, HBr, O₃, BeCl₂.
*Answers:* PCl₃ polar (pyramidal, lone pair); SiH₄ nonpolar (symmetric tetrahedral); HBr polar; O₃ polar (bent with a lone pair on the central O, even though all atoms are oxygen); BeCl₂ nonpolar (linear, symmetric).

Why it matters: polar molecules attract one another more strongly than nonpolar ones of similar size, which raises boiling points and governs what dissolves in what. Water's bent shape, and the polarity that follows from it, is the reason it is a liquid at room temperature and dissolves salts. Week 11 develops this.

## Common mistakes

- Counting a double bond as two domains. It is one region of density: one domain.
- Naming the shape after the electron geometry when lone pairs are present (water is bent, not tetrahedral).
- Forgetting the lone pair on the central atom in the Lewis structure, which changes the shape (SO₂ bent, not linear).
- Assuming "polar bonds means polar molecule". CO₂ and CCl₄ have polar bonds and are nonpolar.
- Assuming a symmetric formula means a symmetric shape: H₂O looks like CO₂ on paper but is bent.
- Giving water a 109.5° angle; lone pairs compress it to about 104.5°.
