# Week 24 · Neutralisation and Titration

**Topics:** 6.6 Neutralization and Titration
**Practices:** 3 (Planning and Carrying Out Investigations), 4 (Analyzing and Interpreting Data), 5 (Using Mathematics and Computational Thinking)
**Spiral:** A titration is unit 4 stoichiometry (4.6) run with solutions: moles = M × V (6.2) replaces moles = grams ÷ molar mass, and the mole ratio comes from the balanced neutralisation equation (4.1). Strong and weak acids and the pH scale (6.4, 6.5) decide the shape of the titration curve and the choice of indicator. Precision and significant figures (1.4) matter because a burette reads to 0.05 mL.

## What you need to be able to do

- **6.6** Write and balance neutralisation equations, including the net ionic equation; describe how a titration is carried out and define equivalence point, end point and indicator; calculate the concentration or volume of an acid or base from titration data using mole ratios, including acids and bases that are not 1:1; and sketch and interpret a titration curve, choosing a suitable indicator from the pH at the equivalence point.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Neutralisation | Acid + base → salt + water; for strong acid and strong base, H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l). |
| Salt | The ionic compound formed from the cation of the base and the anion of the acid (NaCl from NaOH and HCl). |
| Titration | Adding a solution of known concentration (the titrant) from a burette to a measured volume of the unknown until the reaction is just complete. |
| Standard solution | A solution whose concentration is accurately known. |
| Equivalence point | The point at which moles of H⁺ added equal moles of OH⁻ present (or the reverse), according to the equation's ratio. |
| End point | The point at which the indicator changes colour; a good indicator makes it coincide with the equivalence point. |
| Indicator | A dye with different colours in its acid and base forms, changing over a narrow pH range. |
| Titration curve | A graph of pH against volume of titrant added; nearly flat, then a steep jump through the equivalence point, then flat again. |
| Polyprotic acid | An acid with more than one ionisable hydrogen (H₂SO₄, H₃PO₄); each H⁺ needs one OH⁻. |

## Neutralisation (6.6)

An acid and a base cancel each other's properties: the H⁺ of the acid and the OH⁻ of the base combine into water, and the remaining ions make a salt.

HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)
H₂SO₄(aq) + 2 KOH(aq) → K₂SO₄(aq) + 2 H₂O(l)
2 HNO₃(aq) + Ca(OH)₂(aq) → Ca(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2 H₂O(l)

The mole ratio is set by how many H⁺ the acid supplies and how many OH⁻ the base supplies: sulfuric acid needs two hydroxides per molecule, calcium hydroxide supplies two per formula unit. For any strong acid and strong base the net ionic equation is the same, H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l), with the salt's ions as spectators. The reaction releases heat (about 57 kJ per mole of water formed), which unit 7 measures.

**Try it.** Balance H₃PO₄ + NaOH → Na₃PO₄ + H₂O, and name the salt.
*Answer:* H₃PO₄ + 3 NaOH → Na₃PO₄ + 3 H₂O; sodium phosphate.

## Running a titration (6.6)

The point of a titration is to measure a concentration by reacting an unknown with a **standard solution** until the stoichiometric amounts have been mixed.

1. Pipette a measured volume of the unknown (say 25.00 mL of an acid) into a flask and add two or three drops of indicator.
2. Fill a burette with the standard base and record the initial reading (to 0.05 mL).
3. Run the base in with swirling. Near the end, add it drop by drop; the colour flickers and then holds.
4. Record the final reading. The volume delivered is the difference.
5. Repeat until two titrations agree within about 0.1 mL (concordant results); a first "rough" titration is usually discarded.

The **equivalence point** is a chemical fact: the moles of OH⁻ added exactly match the moles of H⁺ in the flask. The **end point** is what you see: the indicator's colour change. They coincide only if the indicator changes colour at the pH of the equivalence point, which is the subject of the last section.

## Titration calculations (6.6)

The calculation is three steps every time:

1. Moles of the known: n = M × V (litres).
2. Mole ratio from the balanced equation to get moles of the unknown.
3. Divide by the unknown's volume (litres) for its molarity, or multiply by the molar mass for a mass.

**Worked example (1:1).** 25.00 mL of hydrochloric acid needs 31.40 mL of 0.100 M NaOH to reach the end point. Find the acid's molarity.

1. Moles NaOH = 0.100 mol/L × 0.03140 L = 3.14 × 10⁻³ mol.
2. HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O, 1:1, so 3.14 × 10⁻³ mol HCl.
3. M = 3.14 × 10⁻³ mol / 0.02500 L = **0.126 M**.

For a 1:1 reaction the three steps collapse into M_aV_a = M_bV_b, which looks like the dilution equation but means something different: it balances moles of acid against moles of base. It must **not** be used when the ratio is not 1:1.

**Worked example (2:1).** 20.00 mL of sulfuric acid requires 27.50 mL of 0.150 M NaOH. Molarity of the acid?

1. Moles NaOH = 0.150 × 0.02750 = 4.125 × 10⁻³ mol.
2. H₂SO₄ + 2 NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2 H₂O: moles H₂SO₄ = 4.125 × 10⁻³ ÷ 2 = 2.0625 × 10⁻³ mol.
3. M = 2.0625 × 10⁻³ / 0.02000 = **0.103 M**.

Using M_aV_a = M_bV_b here would give 0.206 M, double the true value.

**Worked example (volume needed).** What volume of 0.200 M KOH neutralises 15.0 mL of 0.350 M HNO₃?

Moles HNO₃ = 0.350 × 0.0150 = 5.25 × 10⁻³ mol; 1:1, so 5.25 × 10⁻³ mol KOH; V = n/M = 5.25 × 10⁻³ / 0.200 = 0.02625 L = **26.3 mL**.

**Worked example (an unknown solid).** A 0.500 g antacid tablet (active ingredient CaCO₃) is dissolved in excess acid and the leftover acid is titrated. The tablet is found to have consumed 8.40 × 10⁻³ mol of HCl. Mass of CaCO₃ in the tablet?

CaCO₃ + 2 HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂: moles CaCO₃ = 8.40 × 10⁻³ ÷ 2 = 4.20 × 10⁻³ mol; mass = 4.20 × 10⁻³ × 100.09 = **0.420 g**, so the tablet is 84% calcium carbonate.

**Try it.** 10.00 mL of Ba(OH)₂ solution needs 18.60 mL of 0.100 M HCl. Molarity of the Ba(OH)₂?
*Answer:* moles HCl = 1.86 × 10⁻³; Ba(OH)₂ + 2 HCl → BaCl₂ + 2 H₂O, so 9.30 × 10⁻⁴ mol Ba(OH)₂; ÷ 0.01000 L = 0.0930 M.

## Titration curves and indicators (6.6)

Plot pH against the volume of base added to an acid:

- **Strong acid with strong base** (HCl with NaOH): the pH starts low (1 for 0.1 M), rises slowly, then shoots almost vertically from about pH 3 to pH 11 within a drop or two of the equivalence point, and levels off above 12. The equivalence point is at **pH 7.00**, because the salt (NaCl) is neutral.
- **Weak acid with strong base** (acetic acid with NaOH): the start is higher (pH near 3), there is a gently sloping buffer region, and the jump is shorter. The equivalence point is **above 7** (around 8.7 for 0.1 M acetic acid), because the acetate ion left behind is a weak base.
- **Strong acid with weak base** (HCl with ammonia): the equivalence point is **below 7**, because the ammonium ion left behind is a weak acid.

An indicator works if its colour-change range lies on the steep part of the curve, which is why the equivalence-point pH matters:

| Indicator | Colour change (acid → base) | pH range | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| methyl orange | red → yellow | 3.1–4.4 | strong acid with weak base |
| bromothymol blue | yellow → blue | 6.0–7.6 | strong acid with strong base |
| phenolphthalein | colourless → pink | 8.2–10.0 | weak acid with strong base; also fine for strong–strong because the jump covers it |

Phenolphthalein is the everyday choice when a strong base is the titrant: colourless in the acid, a lasting pale pink at the first excess of base, easy to see. Methyl orange would signal too early for a weak acid, long before equivalence.

## Common mistakes

- Using M_aV_a = M_bV_b for a 2:1 or 1:2 reaction. Go through moles and the balanced equation unless the ratio is 1:1.
- Forgetting to convert mL to L before M × V, or converting one volume and not the other.
- Reading the burette to the nearest mL. It reads to 0.05 mL, and the volume used is final minus initial.
- Confusing the end point (colour change) with the equivalence point (stoichiometric completion).
- Assuming the equivalence point is always pH 7. It is 7 only for strong acid with strong base.
- Adding so much indicator that it consumes titrant; two or three drops are enough.
