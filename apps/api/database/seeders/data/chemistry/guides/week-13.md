# Week 13 · Predicting Products: the Activity Series and Solubility Rules

## What you need to be able to do

- **4.3** Use the halogen series (F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂) in the same way.
- **4.3** Use the solubility rules to decide whether mixing two solutions gives a precipitate, and identify it.
- **4.3** Recognise the three things that drive a double replacement to completion: a precipitate, a gas, or water.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Activity series | Metals ranked by how readily they give up electrons; a metal replaces any metal (or hydrogen) below it. |
| Reactive / noble metal | High in the series (K, Na, Ca) / low in the series (Cu, Ag, Au), which stays uncombined. |
| Solubility rules | A short list saying which ionic compounds dissolve in water and which do not. |
| Precipitate | An insoluble solid formed when two solutions are mixed; written with (s). |
| Spectator ion | An ion present in solution before and after the reaction, unchanged. |
| Net ionic equation | The equation with spectator ions removed: only the particles that actually change. |
| Driving force | A precipitate, a gas or water: without one of these a double replacement does not occur. |

## The activity series (4.3)

Put a strip of zinc in blue copper(II) sulfate solution and the strip turns brown with copper while the blue fades: Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu. Put copper in zinc sulfate and nothing happens. Zinc gives up electrons more readily than copper, so zinc can push copper out of its compound but not the reverse. Rank every metal this way and you have the **activity series**:

| Rank | Metal | Reacts with |
|---|---|---|
| most active | Li, K, Ba, Ca, Na | cold water (→ metal hydroxide + H₂), acids, any metal compound below |
| | Mg, Al, Zn, Fe | steam (→ metal oxide + H₂), acids (→ salt + H₂), compounds of metals below |
| | Ni, Sn, Pb | acids slowly; compounds of metals below |
| | **H** | the dividing line: metals above it displace H₂ from acids |
| least active | Cu, Hg, Ag, Au | no reaction with water or ordinary acids; displaced by every metal above |

**The rule:** a free metal replaces, from a compound, any metal that is **below** it in the series. Hydrogen counts as a member: a metal above hydrogen reacts with an acid to release H₂; a metal below hydrogen does not. If the free metal is below the metal in the compound, **no reaction**.

The series follows week 7's trends loosely: the alkali metals, with the lowest ionization energies, are at the top; gold, which holds its electrons tightly, is at the bottom. The order is measured, not deduced, and a few neighbours (Li above K, for instance, because of how strongly Li⁺ is held by water) are not what the ionization energies alone would predict. Use the table.

**Worked example.** Will aluminum react with copper(II) chloride solution? Aluminum is above copper: yes. Aluminum forms Al³⁺, so the product with chloride is AlCl₃, and copper metal is released:
2 Al(s) + 3 CuCl₂(aq) → 2 AlCl₃(aq) + 3 Cu(s).
Note that the new compound's formula comes from the **replacing** metal's charge, not from the old compound's subscript.

**Worked example.** Will silver react with hydrochloric acid? Silver is below hydrogen: **no reaction**. Will magnesium? Magnesium is above hydrogen: Mg(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g).

**Worked example.** Sodium is dropped into water. Sodium is in the "cold water" group: 2 Na(s) + 2 H₂O(l) → 2 NaOH(aq) + H₂(g). (Treat water as H–OH for the pattern: sodium replaces one hydrogen, the hydroxide stays with the sodium.)

**Halogens** have their own short series, F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂, which does match the week 7 trend (nonmetals are more reactive toward the top of a group). A halogen replaces any halogen below it: Cl₂(g) + 2 NaBr(aq) → 2 NaCl(aq) + Br₂(l), and the solution turns orange-brown with bromine. Bromine added to sodium chloride: no reaction.

**Try it.** Predict, writing a balanced equation or "no reaction": (a) Fe + Pb(NO₃)₂; (b) Cu + Fe(NO₃)₂; (c) Zn + H₂SO₄; (d) I₂ + KCl.
*Answers:* (a) Fe is above Pb: Fe + Pb(NO₃)₂ → Fe(NO₃)₂ + Pb (iron(II) is the usual product). (b) Cu is below Fe: no reaction. (c) Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂. (d) I₂ is below Cl₂: no reaction.

## Solubility rules (4.3)

When two solutions of ionic compounds are mixed, four kinds of ion are present. If some pair of them makes an insoluble compound, it drops out as a precipitate and a double replacement has occurred. If every possible pair stays dissolved, the ions simply share the water and nothing has reacted. The **solubility rules** tell you which compounds are insoluble. A version short enough to learn:

| Ion | Soluble? | Exceptions |
|---|---|---|
| Group 1 (Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺ …) and NH₄⁺ | always soluble | none |
| NO₃⁻ and acetate C₂H₃O₂⁻ | always soluble | none |
| Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻ | soluble | insoluble with Ag⁺, Pb²⁺, Hg₂²⁺ |
| SO₄²⁻ | soluble | insoluble with Ba²⁺, Sr²⁺, Pb²⁺, Ca²⁺ (slightly) |
| CO₃²⁻, PO₄³⁻, S²⁻ | insoluble | soluble with group 1 and NH₄⁺ |
| OH⁻ | insoluble | soluble with group 1, NH₄⁺; Ca²⁺, Sr²⁺, Ba²⁺ moderately |

The first two rows are the ones to lean on. Any compound of a group 1 ion, ammonium or nitrate dissolves, which means those ions are nearly always spectators.

### Predicting a double replacement

1. Write the ions in each reactant (from Unit 3).
2. Swap partners: each cation with the other anion.
3. Write each new formula correctly from the charges (never by copying subscripts across).
4. Check each product against the solubility rules. Label (aq) or (s).
5. If at least one product is a precipitate, a gas or water, write the balanced equation. Otherwise write **no reaction**.

**Worked example.** Lead(II) nitrate + potassium iodide. Ions: Pb²⁺, NO₃⁻, K⁺, I⁻. Swap: PbI₂ and KNO₃. Lead iodide: iodide is insoluble with Pb²⁺ → (s). Potassium nitrate: group 1 and nitrate → (aq).
Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2 KI(aq) → PbI₂(s) + 2 KNO₃(aq). A yellow precipitate.

**Worked example.** Sodium chloride + potassium nitrate. Swap: NaNO₃ and KCl. Both soluble (group 1 with nitrate; group 1 with chloride). **No reaction**: the four ions remain in solution.

**Worked example.** Barium chloride + sodium sulfate. Swap: BaSO₄ and NaCl. Barium sulfate is a sulfate exception → (s). BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2 NaCl(aq). This white precipitate is how sulfate is tested for.

**Try it.** Predict: (a) AgNO₃ + CaCl₂; (b) NH₄Cl + Na₂SO₄; (c) FeCl₃ + NaOH; (d) K₂CO₃ + MgBr₂.
*Answers:* (a) 2 AgNO₃ + CaCl₂ → 2 AgCl(s) + Ca(NO₃)₂; white precipitate. (b) No reaction; every product is soluble. (c) FeCl₃ + 3 NaOH → Fe(OH)₃(s) + 3 NaCl; rust-brown precipitate. (d) K₂CO₃ + MgBr₂ → MgCO₃(s) + 2 KBr.

### Gases and water as driving forces

Some double replacements have no precipitate but still occur because a product escapes as a gas or forms the very stable molecule water:

- **Carbonate (or hydrogen carbonate) + acid → salt + water + CO₂.** The expected product, carbonic acid H₂CO₃, falls apart at once: CaCO₃(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g). This is the fizz of an antacid tablet, and the test for a carbonate.
- **Sulfite + acid → salt + water + SO₂.** Same pattern.
- **Sulfide + acid → salt + H₂S** (the rotten-egg gas).
- **Ammonium salt + hydroxide → salt + water + NH₃** on warming.
- **Acid + base → salt + water** (neutralization): H₂SO₄(aq) + 2 KOH(aq) → K₂SO₄(aq) + 2 H₂O(l). Water forming is enough; the salt may stay dissolved.

### Spectator ions and the net ionic equation

In Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2 KI(aq) → PbI₂(s) + 2 KNO₃(aq), the K⁺ and NO₃⁻ ions begin dissolved and end dissolved. They are **spectators**. Strip them out and what is left is the **net ionic equation**: Pb²⁺(aq) + 2 I⁻(aq) → PbI₂(s). It says what actually happened, and it is the same whatever soluble lead salt and soluble iodide you started with. You will write these more in Unit 6; for now, be able to name the spectators.

## Common mistakes

- Carrying a subscript from the reactant into the product (writing CuCl from CuCl₂ + Al). Rebuild every formula from its ions.
- Forgetting that hydrogen is in the activity series: Cu + HCl is "no reaction", not "slow".
- Treating every double replacement as a reaction. Two soluble products means nothing happened.
- Confusing "insoluble" with "does not react": the insoluble product is the reason the reaction goes.
- Mixing up the halogen order with the metal order: halogens are more reactive at the **top** of the group.
- Writing H₂CO₃ as a final product; it decomposes to H₂O + CO₂.
