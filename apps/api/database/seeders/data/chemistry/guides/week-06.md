## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Wavelength (λ) | Distance between successive crests, in m or nm (1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m). |
| Frequency (f) | Waves passing a point per second, in hertz (Hz = s⁻¹). |
| Speed of light (c) | 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s in a vacuum; c = λf. |
| Photon | A packet of light energy; E = hf, with h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s. |
| Ground state | The lowest energy arrangement of an atom's electrons. |
| Excited state | Any higher-energy arrangement, reached by absorbing energy. |
| Line (emission) spectrum | The set of specific wavelengths an element emits; each line is one electron transition. |
| Energy level (n) | A principal shell: n = 1, 2, 3 …; higher n means higher energy and larger size. |
| Sublevel | s, p, d, f within a level; they hold 1, 3, 5, 7 orbitals. |
| Orbital | A region where an electron is likely to be found; holds at most 2 electrons. |
| Aufbau principle | Fill orbitals from lowest energy upward. |
| Pauli exclusion principle | An orbital holds at most two electrons, and they must have opposite spins. |
| Hund's rule | Within a sublevel, put one electron in each orbital before pairing any. |
| Valence electrons | Electrons in the outermost level; they do the chemistry. |

## Light as waves and photons (2.5)

Light is an electromagnetic wave. Its **wavelength** λ and **frequency** f are tied together by the speed of light: **c = λf**. Long wavelength means low frequency; short wavelength means high frequency. The visible range runs from about 700 nm (red) to 400 nm (violet); radio waves are longer, infrared slightly longer than red; ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays are shorter than violet.

Light also arrives in packets, **photons**, each with energy **E = hf** (Planck's constant h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s). Higher frequency means more energetic photons, which is why ultraviolet light causes sunburn and radio waves do not. Combining the two equations, E = hc/λ: short wavelength, high energy.

**Worked example.** Green light has a wavelength of 500 nm. Find its frequency and the energy of one photon.
λ = 500 nm = 5.00 × 10⁻⁷ m. f = c/λ = 2.998 × 10⁸ ÷ 5.00 × 10⁻⁷ = **6.00 × 10¹⁴ Hz**.
E = hf = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ × 6.00 × 10¹⁴ = **3.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ J**.

**Try it.** A radio station broadcasts at 100 MHz (1.00 × 10⁸ Hz). What is the wavelength? Compare the photon energy with green light.
*Answer:* λ = c/f = 2.998 × 10⁸ ÷ 1.00 × 10⁸ = 3.00 m. E = 6.63 × 10⁻²⁶ J, about six million times less than a green photon.

## Line spectra and the Bohr model (2.5)

Pass sunlight through a prism and you get a continuous rainbow. Pass the light from a hydrogen discharge tube through the same prism and you get only four visible lines: red (656 nm), blue-green (486 nm), violet (434 nm) and a fainter violet (410 nm). Every element has its own **line spectrum**, as distinctive as a fingerprint; neon signs glow red-orange and sodium streetlights yellow for this reason.

Bohr's explanation (1913): the electron in hydrogen can occupy only certain orbits, each with a fixed energy, numbered n = 1, 2, 3 … The lowest, n = 1, is the **ground state**. When the atom absorbs exactly the right energy, the electron jumps to a higher level, an **excited state**. When it falls back, it emits a photon whose energy equals the difference between the levels: E_photon = E_high − E_low. Because the levels are fixed, only certain photon energies, and so certain colours, appear. The four visible hydrogen lines are drops to n = 2 from n = 3, 4, 5 and 6; drops to n = 1 are in the ultraviolet, drops to n = 3 in the infrared.

The levels get closer together as n increases (like rungs of a ladder that bunch up at the top). So the n = 3 → 2 drop releases less energy (red) than the 4 → 2 drop (blue-green).

**Figure description:** an energy-level diagram. Horizontal lines labelled n = 1 at the bottom, then n = 2 well above it, n = 3 closer above that, n = 4, 5, 6 crowding together near the top. Downward arrows from n = 3, 4, 5 and 6 all ending on n = 2 are labelled 656 nm, 486 nm, 434 nm and 410 nm; the longest arrow (from n = 6) carries the shortest wavelength.

**Worked example.** Which transition in hydrogen emits more energetic light: n = 4 → 2 or n = 3 → 2? Which has the longer wavelength?
The 4 → 2 drop covers a larger energy gap, so its photon is more energetic (486 nm). The 3 → 2 photon has less energy and the longer wavelength (656 nm, red).

Bohr's model worked only for hydrogen. The modern **quantum model** keeps the idea of fixed energy levels but replaces orbits with **orbitals**, regions where an electron is likely to be found.

## Levels, sublevels, orbitals (2.6)

Each level n contains n sublevels. Sublevels are named s, p, d, f, with 1, 3, 5 and 7 orbitals, and each orbital holds two electrons (Pauli). So a level n holds 2n² electrons.

| Level | Sublevels | Orbitals | Max electrons |
|---|---|---|---|
| n = 1 | 1s | 1 | 2 |
| n = 2 | 2s, 2p | 1 + 3 | 8 |
| n = 3 | 3s, 3p, 3d | 1 + 3 + 5 | 18 |
| n = 4 | 4s, 4p, 4d, 4f | 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 | 32 |

An **s orbital** is spherical; the three **p orbitals** are dumbbell-shaped and point along x, y and z. Higher-n orbitals of the same type are larger.

## Writing electron configurations (2.6)

Three rules:

1. **Aufbau:** fill from lowest energy up, in the order 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p 5s 4d 5p … Note that 4s fills before 3d.
2. **Pauli:** two electrons per orbital, opposite spins.
3. **Hund:** in a sublevel with several orbitals, spread electrons out one per orbital, spins parallel, before pairing.

Write the configuration as a list of sublevels with superscript counts; the superscripts add up to the number of electrons.

- Carbon (6): 1s² 2s² 2p²
- Sodium (11): 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹
- Sulfur (16): 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁴
- Calcium (20): 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s²
- Iron (26): 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² 3d⁶
- Bromine (35): 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² 3d¹⁰ 4p⁵

**Noble-gas (shorthand) notation** replaces the filled core with the previous noble gas in brackets: sulfur is [Ne] 3s² 3p⁴; iron is [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶.

**Orbital diagrams** show each orbital as a box with arrows for electrons. Nitrogen (7), 1s² 2s² 2p³:

```
1s: [↑↓]   2s: [↑↓]   2p: [↑ ][↑ ][↑ ]
```

Hund's rule puts the three 2p electrons in separate boxes with parallel spins. Oxygen (8) adds one more, pairing in the first 2p box: [↑↓][↑ ][↑ ]. Nitrogen has three unpaired electrons, oxygen two.

**Valence electrons** are those in the highest n. Sulfur, 3s² 3p⁴, has six; calcium, 4s², has two; bromine, 4s² 4p⁵, has seven (the filled 3d is not counted). Main-group elements in the same column have the same number of valence electrons, which is the reason for the periodic table's shape (week 7).

**Worked example.** Write the full and shorthand configurations of chlorine (17), draw the orbital diagram of its 3p sublevel, and count unpaired and valence electrons.
1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁵; [Ne] 3s² 3p⁵. The 3p boxes: [↑↓][↑↓][↑ ]. One unpaired electron; seven valence electrons (3s² 3p⁵).

**Worked example.** An element's configuration ends in 4s² 3d¹⁰ 4p³. Identify it and give its valence electron count.
Count: [Ar] holds 18; plus 2 + 10 + 3 = 33, arsenic. Valence: 4s² 4p³ = five.

**Try it.** Write shorthand configurations for magnesium (12), phosphorus (15) and zinc (30); give each one's valence count.
*Answers:* [Ne] 3s² (2); [Ne] 3s² 3p³ (5); [Ar] 4s² 3d¹⁰ (2).

**Ions.** Cations lose electrons from the highest n first: Mg²⁺ is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶, the same as neon; Fe²⁺ loses the two 4s electrons, [Ar] 3d⁶. Anions add to the next open spot: Cl⁻ is [Ne] 3s² 3p⁶, the same as argon. Ions with a noble-gas configuration are the theme of Unit 3.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing wavelength and frequency when ranking energy: *short* wavelength is *high* energy.
- Forgetting to convert nm to m before using c = λf.
- Thinking an electron emits light when it absorbs energy. Absorption lifts it; emission happens on the way down.
- Filling 3d before 4s. The Aufbau order is 4s then 3d (the superscript total still has to match Z).
- Pairing electrons early. Hund's rule spreads them out first.
- Counting d electrons as valence electrons for main-group elements (bromine has 7, not 17).
