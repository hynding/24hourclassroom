## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Reaction rate | How fast a reactant is used up or a product forms: change in concentration (or mass, volume, moles) divided by the time taken. Common units M/s, mL/s, g/s. |
| Average rate | Δ(quantity) ÷ Δt over a chosen interval. Rates are quoted as positive numbers. |
| Collision theory | Particles must collide to react; only collisions with enough energy and the right orientation succeed. |
| Activation energy, Ea | The minimum energy a collision must have for the reaction to occur; the height of the hump on an energy diagram, measured from the reactants. |
| Activated complex (transition state) | The unstable, highest-energy arrangement of atoms at the top of the hump, with old bonds partly broken and new bonds partly formed. |
| Effective collision | A collision with energy ≥ Ea and the right orientation, which leads to products. |
| Catalyst | A substance that speeds a reaction by providing a different pathway with a lower Ea, and is not used up. |
| Inhibitor | A substance that slows a reaction, often by blocking a catalyst or interrupting a chain. |
| Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution | The spread of kinetic energies among particles at a given temperature; a graph of number of particles against energy, a lopsided hump with a long tail to high energies. |

## 1. What a rate is and how to measure one

A rate always compares a change to the time it took. For a reaction, the change is in the amount of a reactant or product. If a solution of hydrogen peroxide drops from 0.800 M to 0.620 M in 60 s, the average rate of its decomposition is (0.800 − 0.620) M ÷ 60 s = 0.0030 M/s. Rates are quoted as positive numbers whether the substance is appearing or disappearing.

To measure a rate you need something that changes and that you can record against a clock:

- **A gas is produced.** Collect it in a gas syringe or an inverted measuring cylinder over water and read the volume every 10 s; or put the flask on a balance and watch the mass fall as gas escapes.
- **A colour changes.** Time how long a known amount of colour takes to appear or vanish, or measure light absorption with a colorimeter.
- **A precipitate forms.** Time how long a cross drawn under the flask takes to disappear from view.
- **Temperature or pH changes.** Record it with a probe.

A graph of amount of product against time starts steep and flattens into a plateau. The steep part is the fast early rate; the plateau means the limiting reactant (4.7) is gone. The rate at any moment is the slope of the curve there, and since the slope keeps decreasing, **the rate falls as the reaction proceeds**. Collision theory explains why: as reactant particles are used up, there are fewer of them to collide.

**Worked example.** Magnesium ribbon reacts with excess dilute hydrochloric acid, and the hydrogen is collected.

| Time (s) | 0 | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 | 100 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Volume of H₂ (mL) | 0 | 24 | 40 | 50 | 55 | 55 |

Average rate for 0–20 s: 24 mL ÷ 20 s = 1.2 mL/s. For 20–40 s: 16 mL ÷ 20 s = 0.8 mL/s. For 80–100 s: 0 mL/s; the magnesium is used up at 55 mL. Rate falls because the acid near the ribbon is being consumed and the ribbon itself is shrinking.

**Try it.** What is the average rate over the whole reaction, 0–80 s? (Answer: 55 mL ÷ 80 s = 0.69 mL/s, smaller than the early rate and larger than the late rate, as an average must be.)

## 2. Collision theory

Reactions happen when particles collide. But at room temperature a molecule in a gas collides billions of times a second, and if every collision reacted, every reaction would be over instantly. Most collisions are ineffective, for two reasons:

1. **Not enough energy.** Bonds must be stretched and partly broken before new ones can form. That takes energy, and a gentle collision simply bounces. The minimum energy that works is the **activation energy, Ea**.
2. **Wrong orientation.** Atoms have to meet at the right places. If NO₂ and CO collide, the oxygen of NO₂ must hit the carbon of CO to transfer; a collision nitrogen-first does nothing.

Only an **effective collision**, with enough energy and the right orientation, makes products. The rate of a reaction is the rate of effective collisions, so anything that increases either the number of collisions or the fraction that are effective increases the rate.

## 3. The energy diagram with the hump

Draw energy on the vertical axis and "progress of reaction" on the horizontal. Reactants start at one level; products end at another. Between them the curve rises to a peak and falls. The peak is the **activated complex** (or transition state): the moment when the colliding particles are a single strained cluster, old bonds half broken and new bonds half formed. It cannot be isolated; it lasts about as long as one vibration.

- **Ea (forward)** is the height from the reactants' level up to the peak.
- **ΔH** is the difference between the products' level and the reactants' level (negative if the products are lower).
- **Ea (reverse)** is the height from the products' level up to the same peak, so Ea(reverse) = Ea(forward) − ΔH.

**Worked example.** A reaction has Ea(forward) = 80 kJ and ΔH = −50 kJ. The products sit 50 kJ below the reactants, so from the products the climb to the peak is 80 + 50 = 130 kJ: Ea(reverse) = 80 − (−50) = 130 kJ. The reverse reaction is slower because its hump is higher.

The height of the hump has nothing to do with the sign of ΔH. A strongly exothermic reaction such as the burning of wood has a large Ea, which is why logs do not burst into flame in the air, and why a match is needed. Once started, the energy released supplies the activation energy for the next collisions, and the fire sustains itself.

## 4. The spread of energies and why temperature matters so much

Not all particles at a given temperature have the same energy. Described in words, the **Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution** graph plots the number of particles (vertical) against kinetic energy (horizontal): it starts at zero, rises to a peak at a moderate energy, then falls away slowly in a long tail toward high energies. Draw a vertical line at Ea; only the particles in the tail to the right of that line can react on collision.

Raise the temperature and the whole curve shifts right and flattens: the peak moves to higher energy and the tail grows. The area to the right of Ea, the fraction of particles able to react, can double or more for a modest rise. That is why temperature is the most powerful ordinary rate factor: a 10 °C rise roughly doubles the rate of many reactions near room temperature. Two things happen at once: particles collide a little more often (they move faster) and, far more importantly, a much larger fraction of collisions have energy above Ea.

A catalyst does something different to the same graph. It does not move the curve; it moves the Ea line to the left, so that a larger fraction of the existing particles already qualifies.

## 5. The factors that change a rate

| Factor | Effect on rate | Collision-theory reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Higher concentration of a reactant in solution | Faster | More particles per volume, so more collisions per second. |
| Higher pressure of a gas reactant (smaller volume) | Faster | Same as concentration: gas particles are packed closer. |
| Higher temperature | Much faster | More collisions, and a far larger fraction with energy ≥ Ea. |
| Greater surface area of a solid (powder, not lump) | Faster | Only particles on the surface can be hit; a powder exposes far more surface. |
| Catalyst | Faster | Provides a different pathway with lower Ea, so more collisions are effective. Not used up; does not change ΔH or the final amount of product. |
| Nature of the reactants | Varies | Ions in solution react almost instantly (no bonds to break); reactions that break strong covalent bonds are slow. |

Two reminders. First, each of these is a fair test only when the others are held constant. Second, a catalyst appears in the same amount at the end: it takes part (it is not merely a bystander) but is regenerated. Enzymes are biological catalysts; the platinum and rhodium in a car's catalytic converter speed the conversion of CO and nitrogen oxides to CO₂ and N₂; manganese dioxide catalyses the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide, which otherwise sits in its bottle for months.

**Worked example.** A student drops a 1.0 g lump of calcium carbonate into 50 mL of 1.0 M HCl and a 1.0 g sample of powdered calcium carbonate into another 50 mL of 1.0 M HCl at the same temperature. The powder fizzes violently and finishes in under a minute; the lump bubbles for ten minutes. Same mass, same acid, same temperature: surface area is the only difference. The final volume of CO₂ is the same in both, because the amount of reactant is the same; only the time differs.

**Try it.** Explain why a lit splint goes out in a jar of flour but a cloud of flour dust in a mill can explode. (Answer: in the jar only the surface grains touch the flame; dispersed in air, every grain is surrounded by oxygen and the combined surface is enormous, so the burning spreads almost instantly.)

## 6. Rate versus amount

The most common confusion this week: a factor that changes the **rate** does not necessarily change the **amount** of product. If magnesium is the limiting reactant, warming the acid makes the hydrogen come off faster, but the final volume is fixed by the magnesium. On a graph of product against time, a faster run is steeper and reaches the same plateau sooner. A catalyst, likewise, changes how soon you get the product, not how much. Only changing the amount of limiting reactant changes the plateau (4.7).

## Common mistakes

- **Saying "the particles have more energy" for a concentration change.** Concentration affects how often particles collide, not how hard. Energy is a temperature effect.
- **Saying temperature only makes particles collide more often.** The larger effect is the bigger fraction with energy above Ea.
- **Thinking a catalyst changes ΔH, or is used up, or makes more product.** It lowers Ea; everything else about the energy diagram stays.
- **Reading Ea from the products' level** when the question asks about the forward reaction. Ea(forward) is measured from the reactants.
- **Confusing rate with the time taken.** Rate is amount per time; a short time means a high rate.
- **Negative rates.** Quote rates as positive.
- **Claiming exothermic reactions are fast.** The sign of ΔH and the size of Ea are independent.

## Self-check

1. A reaction's concentration falls from 1.20 M to 0.90 M in 25 s. What is the average rate?
2. State the two conditions a collision must meet to be effective.
3. Ea(forward) = 95 kJ and ΔH = +30 kJ. What is Ea(reverse)?
4. Why does crushing a tablet make it dissolve and react faster?
5. A catalyst is added to an equilibrium mixture already at the end of its reaction. How much more product forms?

**Answers.** 1. 0.30 M ÷ 25 s = 0.012 M/s. 2. Energy at least Ea, and the correct orientation. 3. Ea(reverse) = 95 − 30 = 65 kJ; the products are higher, so the climb back is shorter. 4. The powder exposes far more surface, so many more particles can be hit per second. 5. None; a catalyst changes the rate, not the amount of product.
