## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mole (mol) | The amount of substance containing 6.022 × 10²³ particles, as many as there are atoms in 12 g of carbon-12. |
| Avogadro's number (N_A) | 6.022 × 10²³ per mole. |
| Molar mass (M) | The mass of one mole of a substance, in g/mol. Numerically equal to the atomic or formula mass in u. |
| Formula mass | The sum of the atomic masses in a formula, in u; for molecules, the molecular mass. |
| Percent composition | The mass percent of each element in a compound. |
| Empirical formula | The simplest whole-number ratio of atoms (CH₂O). |
| Molecular formula | The actual number of atoms in one molecule (C₆H₁₂O₆); a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula. |

## Counting by weighing (4.4)

Atoms are too small to count one at a time, so chemists count them the way a bank counts coins: by weighing. A **mole** is a counting unit like a dozen, but enormous: 6.022 × 10²³ particles. The number is chosen so that one mole of any element has a mass in grams equal to its atomic mass in atomic mass units. Carbon's average atomic mass is 12.01 u, so one mole of carbon atoms has a mass of 12.01 g. One mole of magnesium is 24.31 g. The atomic mass on the table, read in g/mol, is the element's **molar mass**.

For a compound, add up the atoms in the formula:

| Compound | Calculation | Molar mass |
|---|---|---|
| H₂O | 2(1.008) + 16.00 | 18.02 g/mol |
| CO₂ | 12.01 + 2(16.00) | 44.01 g/mol |
| NaCl | 22.99 + 35.45 | 58.44 g/mol |
| CaCO₃ | 40.08 + 12.01 + 3(16.00) | 100.09 g/mol |
| Mg(NO₃)₂ | 24.31 + 2[14.01 + 3(16.00)] | 148.33 g/mol |
| C₆H₁₂O₆ | 6(12.01) + 12(1.008) + 6(16.00) | 180.16 g/mol |

Multiply everything inside parentheses by the subscript outside. The molar mass of an ionic compound refers to one mole of formula units; of a molecular compound, to one mole of molecules.

Use the atomic masses on your periodic table to two decimal places (H 1.008, C 12.01, N 14.01, O 16.00, Na 22.99, Mg 24.31, Al 26.98, S 32.06, Cl 35.45, K 39.10, Ca 40.08, Fe 55.85, Cu 63.55, Zn 65.38, Ag 107.87).

### The mole as the hub

Every conversion in this unit goes through moles. Two conversion factors do the work:

- **Molar mass** links grams and moles: g ÷ (g/mol) = mol; mol × (g/mol) = g.
- **Avogadro's number** links moles and particles: mol × 6.022 × 10²³ = particles.

**Figure description:** a triangle of three boxes. "Mass (g)" at the left, "Moles" at the top, "Particles" at the right. The arrow between mass and moles is labelled "÷ or × molar mass"; the arrow between moles and particles is labelled "× or ÷ 6.022 × 10²³". There is no direct arrow between mass and particles: every route passes through moles.

**Worked example.** How many moles are in 25.0 g of calcium carbonate?
25.0 g × (1 mol ÷ 100.09 g) = **0.250 mol**.

**Worked example.** What is the mass of 0.750 mol of sodium chloride?
0.750 mol × 58.44 g/mol = **43.8 g**.

**Worked example.** How many molecules are in 9.00 g of water?
9.00 g ÷ 18.02 g/mol = 0.499 mol; 0.499 mol × 6.022 × 10²³ = **3.01 × 10²³ molecules**. (And since each molecule holds 3 atoms, that is 9.02 × 10²³ atoms.)

**Worked example.** A sample contains 1.505 × 10²⁴ atoms of iron. What is its mass?
1.505 × 10²⁴ ÷ 6.022 × 10²³ = 2.50 mol; 2.50 mol × 55.85 g/mol = **140 g**.

**Try it.** (a) Moles in 11.0 g of CO₂. (b) Mass of 2.00 mol of Mg(NO₃)₂. (c) Formula units in 29.22 g of NaCl.
*Answers:* (a) 11.0 ÷ 44.01 = 0.250 mol. (b) 2.00 × 148.33 = 297 g. (c) 29.22 ÷ 58.44 = 0.5000 mol; × 6.022 × 10²³ = 3.011 × 10²³ formula units.

A useful sense of scale: a mole of water is 18 mL, a tablespoon; a mole of carbon is a lump of charcoal you can hold; a mole of gold (197 g) would be a cube about 2 cm on a side. Moles are human-sized amounts of atom-sized things.

## Percent composition (4.5)

Week 4's law of definite proportions says a compound always has the same mass ratio of elements. The **percent composition** states that ratio: for each element,

mass % = (mass of that element in one mole of compound ÷ molar mass of compound) × 100%.

**Worked example.** Water: H: 2(1.008) = 2.016 g per 18.02 g → 2.016 ÷ 18.02 × 100% = **11.19% H**; O: 16.00 ÷ 18.02 = **88.81% O**. (The two percentages sum to 100.)

**Worked example.** Iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃, molar mass 2(55.85) + 3(16.00) = 159.70 g/mol. %Fe = 111.70 ÷ 159.70 × 100% = **69.94%**. A tonne of pure hematite ore therefore contains at most 699 kg of iron.

**Worked example.** Which fertilizer carries more nitrogen by mass, ammonium nitrate NH₄NO₃ or urea CO(NH₂)₂? NH₄NO₃: M = 80.05; %N = 28.02 ÷ 80.05 = 35.00%. Urea: M = 12.01 + 16.00 + 2(14.01) + 4(1.008) = 60.06; %N = 28.02 ÷ 60.06 = 46.65%. **Urea.**

**Try it.** Find the percent of carbon in CO₂ and in glucose C₆H₁₂O₆.
*Answers:* CO₂: 12.01 ÷ 44.01 = 27.29%. Glucose: 72.06 ÷ 180.16 = 40.00%.

## Empirical formulas from data (4.5)

Run the calculation backwards. If you know the percent composition (from an analysis in the lab), you can find the simplest ratio of atoms, the **empirical formula**. Percent to mass to moles to ratio:

1. Assume 100 g of compound, so each percent becomes a mass in grams.
2. Convert each mass to moles with the element's molar mass.
3. Divide every mole value by the smallest one.
4. If the results are whole numbers (within about ±0.1), those are the subscripts. If one is 1.5, 1.33, 1.25 or 1.67, multiply all of them by 2, 3, 4 or 3 respectively to clear the fraction.

**Worked example.** A compound is 40.0% C, 6.7% H and 53.3% O.
C: 40.0 ÷ 12.01 = 3.33 mol. H: 6.7 ÷ 1.008 = 6.65 mol. O: 53.3 ÷ 16.00 = 3.33 mol.
Divide by 3.33: C 1, H 2, O 1. Empirical formula **CH₂O**.

**Worked example (lab masses).** 2.43 g of magnesium is burned and 4.03 g of magnesium oxide is collected. Oxygen combined: 4.03 − 2.43 = 1.60 g.
Mg: 2.43 ÷ 24.31 = 0.100 mol. O: 1.60 ÷ 16.00 = 0.100 mol. Ratio 1 : 1 → **MgO**.

**Worked example (a fraction to clear).** An iron oxide is 69.9% Fe and 30.1% O.
Fe: 69.9 ÷ 55.85 = 1.25 mol. O: 30.1 ÷ 16.00 = 1.88 mol. Divide by 1.25: Fe 1, O 1.50. Multiply by 2: **Fe₂O₃**.

**Try it.** A compound is 5.93% H and 94.07% O. Find the empirical formula.
*Answer:* H: 5.93 ÷ 1.008 = 5.88 mol; O: 94.07 ÷ 16.00 = 5.88 mol. Ratio 1 : 1 → **HO**.

## Molecular formulas (4.5)

The empirical formula is a ratio; the **molecular formula** is the real molecule, some whole-number multiple n of the empirical formula. Find n by comparing molar masses:

n = (molar mass of compound) ÷ (molar mass of empirical formula).

**Worked example.** The compound with empirical formula CH₂O has a molar mass of 180 g/mol. CH₂O weighs 12.01 + 2.016 + 16.00 = 30.03 g/mol. n = 180 ÷ 30.03 = 5.99 → 6. Molecular formula **C₆H₁₂O₆** (glucose). Acetic acid, with the same empirical formula and M = 60, is C₂H₄O₂; formaldehyde, M = 30, is CH₂O itself. One empirical formula, three compounds.

**Worked example.** The HO compound above has M = 34.0 g/mol. HO weighs 17.01; n = 34.0 ÷ 17.01 = 2 → **H₂O₂**, hydrogen peroxide.

**Try it.** A hydrocarbon is 85.6% C and 14.4% H with M = 56.1 g/mol. Find both formulas.
*Answer:* C: 85.6 ÷ 12.01 = 7.13; H: 14.4 ÷ 1.008 = 14.3; ratio 1 : 2 → CH₂ (14.03 g/mol); n = 56.1 ÷ 14.03 = 4 → **C₄H₈**.

## Common mistakes

- Dividing moles by molar mass to get grams (always check: grams ÷ g/mol = mol; the units must cancel).
- Forgetting the subscript outside parentheses when computing molar mass: Mg(NO₃)₂ has 2 N and 6 O.
- Rounding mole ratios too eagerly (1.5 is not "about 1" or "about 2"; double it).
- Using the percentage itself as a mole number without dividing by the element's molar mass.
- Reporting the empirical formula when the molecular formula is asked for, or vice versa.
- Treating a mole as a mass: a mole of CO₂ (44 g) and a mole of H₂ (2 g) are the same number of molecules.
