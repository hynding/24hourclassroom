## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Period | A horizontal row; its number is the highest energy level occupied in the ground state. |
| Group (family) | A vertical column; main-group members share a valence configuration and so share chemistry. |
| Alkali metals (group 1) | Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs: one valence electron, soft, very reactive, form 1+ ions. |
| Alkaline earth metals (group 2) | Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba: two valence electrons, reactive, form 2+ ions. |
| Halogens (group 17) | F, Cl, Br, I: seven valence electrons, reactive nonmetals, form 1− ions. |
| Noble gases (group 18) | He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe: full valence shells, almost unreactive. |
| Transition metals | Groups 3–12, the d block; often several ion charges, coloured compounds. |
| Metalloid | B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te: along the staircase; semiconductors with mixed properties. |
| Atomic radius | Half the distance between the nuclei of two bonded atoms of the element. |
| Ionization energy | Energy needed to remove the outermost electron from a gaseous atom; kJ/mol. |
| Electronegativity | How strongly an atom in a bond pulls shared electrons toward itself (Pauling scale, F = 3.98). |
| Effective nuclear charge | The net positive charge felt by a valence electron: nuclear charge minus shielding by inner electrons. |
| Shielding | Inner electrons repelling outer ones and so cancelling part of the nucleus's pull. |

## How the table is built (2.7)

Mendeleev (1869) arranged the known elements by atomic mass and found that properties repeat periodically; he left gaps and predicted the elements that would fill them. The modern table orders elements by **atomic number**, which removes the few places where mass order was wrong, and the periodic law now reads: the properties of the elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers.

**Periods** are rows 1 to 7. The period number is the highest occupied energy level: sodium (3s¹) is in period 3; bromine (4s² 4p⁵) is in period 4. **Groups** are columns 1 to 18. For main-group elements (groups 1, 2 and 13–18), the group number's last digit gives the valence electron count: group 1 has one, group 2 two, group 13 three … group 17 seven, group 18 eight (helium: two).

**Blocks.** The table's shape is the Aufbau order drawn out. Groups 1–2 are the **s block** (ns¹, ns²). Groups 13–18 are the **p block** (ns² np¹ to np⁶). Groups 3–12 are the **d block**, the transition metals, where the (n − 1)d sublevel fills. The two rows below, lanthanides and actinides, are the **f block**.

**Figure description:** an outline of the periodic table. The two left columns are shaded as the s block; the six right columns as the p block; the ten middle columns of periods 4–7 as the d block; the two detached rows beneath as the f block. A stepped line from boron down to astatine separates metals (left, most of the table) from nonmetals (upper right); the elements touching the line are marked as metalloids.

**Metals, nonmetals, metalloids.** Metals (about 80% of elements) are shiny, malleable, ductile, good conductors, and lose electrons to form cations. Nonmetals are dull, brittle as solids, poor conductors, and gain or share electrons. Metalloids sit on the staircase and conduct electricity moderately, which makes silicon and germanium the basis of electronics. Hydrogen sits in group 1 for its single electron but is a nonmetal gas.

**Families to know.**

| Family | Group | Valence config | Typical behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alkali metals | 1 | ns¹ | react violently with water; stored under oil; 1+ ions |
| Alkaline earth metals | 2 | ns² | reactive, less so than group 1; 2+ ions |
| Halogens | 17 | ns² np⁵ | F₂ and Cl₂ gases, Br₂ liquid, I₂ solid; 1− ions; salts with metals |
| Noble gases | 18 | ns² np⁶ (He 1s²) | monatomic gases; almost no reactions |
| Transition metals | 3–12 | (n−1)d filling | hard, high melting, variable charges, coloured compounds |

**Worked example.** An element is in period 4, group 2. Give its configuration, valence count and likely ion.
Period 4 means the outer level is n = 4; group 2 means ns². Configuration [Ar] 4s²: calcium, two valence electrons, Ca²⁺.

**Try it.** Locate the element with configuration [Ne] 3s² 3p⁵: period, group, family, and the ion it forms.
*Answer:* period 3, group 17, a halogen (chlorine), Cl⁻.

## Why properties trend (2.8)

Two ideas explain every trend. **Effective nuclear charge** is the pull a valence electron actually feels: the full nuclear charge minus the **shielding** by inner electrons. Across a period, protons are added one at a time while electrons enter the same level, so shielding barely changes and the effective charge grows: the valence electrons are held more tightly. Down a group, each step adds a whole new level: the valence electrons are farther out and more inner electrons shield them, so they are held less tightly.

### Atomic radius

Across a period, radius **decreases** (greater effective charge pulls the same level inward). Down a group, radius **increases** (a new level is added).

| Period 3 element | Na | Mg | Al | Si | P | S | Cl |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Radius (pm) | 186 | 160 | 143 | 118 | 110 | 104 | 99 |

| Group 1 element | Li | Na | K | Rb |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Radius (pm) | 152 | 186 | 227 | 248 |

### Ionization energy

The energy to remove the outermost electron from a gaseous atom. Across a period it **increases** (tighter hold); down a group it **decreases** (farther, more shielded). Noble gases have the highest in each period; alkali metals the lowest. Small dips (Mg → Al, P → S) come from sublevel changes and are not required at this level.

| Period 3 element | Na | Mg | Al | Si | P | S | Cl | Ar |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| First ionization energy (kJ/mol) | 496 | 738 | 578 | 786 | 1,012 | 1,000 | 1,251 | 1,521 |

Second and later ionization energies are always larger, and there is a huge jump once a noble-gas core is reached: magnesium's first two removals cost 738 and 1,451 kJ/mol, the third 7,733, because the third electron comes from the full 2p shell. The jump tells you the number of valence electrons.

### Electronegativity

The pull an atom exerts on shared electrons in a bond. Across a period it **increases**; down a group it **decreases**. Fluorine (3.98) is the highest, followed by oxygen (3.44), chlorine (3.16) and nitrogen (3.04); cesium (0.79) is the lowest. Noble gases are usually left unassigned because they rarely bond. Unit 3 uses electronegativity differences to classify bonds.

### Metallic character and reactivity

Metallic character (tendency to lose electrons) **decreases** across a period and **increases** down a group, the reverse of ionization energy: cesium is the most metallic natural element. Among metals, reactivity increases down a group (potassium is more violent in water than sodium). Among nonmetals, reactivity increases up a group (fluorine is the most reactive halogen), because gaining an electron is easier for a small, high-effective-charge atom.

### Ionic radius

A cation is **smaller** than its atom: it has lost its outer level, and the remaining electrons are pulled in by the unchanged nucleus (Na 186 pm → Na⁺ 102 pm). An anion is **larger**: extra electrons repel one another and spread the cloud (Cl 99 pm → Cl⁻ 181 pm). Ions with the same configuration (O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺, Mg²⁺, all 1s² 2s² 2p⁶) shrink as nuclear charge grows: O²⁻ is the largest, Mg²⁺ the smallest.

**Worked example.** Arrange S, Cl, K and Mg in order of increasing atomic radius and increasing ionization energy.
Radius: Cl (99) < S (104) < Mg (160) < K (227). Ionization energy is the reverse of this for these elements: K (419) < Mg (738) < S (1,000) < Cl (1,251).

**Worked example.** Which is larger, K⁺ or Cl⁻? Both have 18 electrons (argon's configuration), but potassium has 19 protons pulling them in and chlorine 17, so **Cl⁻ is larger** (181 pm against 138 pm).

**Try it.** Which has the higher electronegativity, O or S? Which has the larger radius, Br or Br⁻? Which is more reactive, Na or Rb?
*Answers:* O (higher in the group); Br⁻ (anions are larger); Rb (metal reactivity rises down the group).

## Common mistakes

- Reading the table's decimal as a mass number or the box's integer as anything but the proton count.
- Reversing the radius trend across a period: more protons means *smaller*, not larger, because the electrons enter the same level.
- Saying ionization energy rises down a group because "there are more electrons"; the extra electrons are shielding, which lowers it.
- Counting d electrons as valence electrons for a main-group element.
- Confusing cation and anion size changes: cations shrink, anions grow.
- Treating hydrogen as an alkali metal because of its column.
