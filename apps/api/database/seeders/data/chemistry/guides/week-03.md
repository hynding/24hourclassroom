## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Dimensional analysis | Solving problems by multiplying by conversion factors so that units cancel, leaving the unit wanted. |
| Conversion factor | A ratio equal to 1 built from an equality: 1,000 mL / 1 L or 1 L / 1,000 mL. |
| Density | Mass per unit volume, D = m ÷ V, usually g/cm³ for solids and g/mL for liquids. |
| Energy | The capacity to do work or transfer heat; measured in joules (J). 1 kJ = 1,000 J. |
| Kinetic energy | Energy of motion; for particles, higher temperature means higher average kinetic energy. |
| Potential energy | Stored energy, in chemistry mostly in the arrangement of particles and the bonds between them. |
| Temperature | A measure of the average kinetic energy of a sample's particles. |
| Heat | Energy transferred from a hotter object to a colder one because of the temperature difference. |
| Absolute zero | 0 K = −273.15 °C, where particle motion is at its minimum. |
| Particle model | Matter is made of tiny particles in constant motion; the state depends on how strongly they attract each other compared with how fast they move. |
| Phase change | A physical change between states: melting, freezing, vaporization, condensation, sublimation, deposition. |

## Dimensional analysis (1.5)

Every equality gives two conversion factors. From 1 in = 2.54 cm come 2.54 cm / 1 in and 1 in / 2.54 cm; both equal 1, so multiplying by either changes units without changing the quantity. Pick the one that cancels the unit you have.

**Worked example.** Convert 5.0 km to inches (1 km = 1,000 m, 1 m = 100 cm, 1 in = 2.54 cm).

5.0 km × (1,000 m / 1 km) × (100 cm / 1 m) × (1 in / 2.54 cm) = 196,850 in → **2.0 × 10⁵ in** (two significant figures, from 5.0 km; the conversion factors are exact).

Write the units at every step and strike them out as they cancel. If a unit does not cancel, the factor is upside down.

**Squared and cubed units.** To convert cm³ to m³ the factor must be cubed: (1 m / 100 cm)³ = 1 m³ / 1,000,000 cm³. So 2,500 cm³ = 2,500 × 10⁻⁶ m³ = 2.5 × 10⁻³ m³.

**Rates.** A car doing 65 mi/h converts to m/s by converting the top and bottom separately: 65 mi/h × (1,609 m / 1 mi) × (1 h / 3,600 s) = 29 m/s.

**Try it.** How many seconds are in 3.5 days? Express 0.045 L in cm³.
*Answers:* 3.5 d × 24 h/d × 60 min/h × 60 s/min = 302,400 s → 3.0 × 10⁵ s. 0.045 L × 1,000 mL/L × 1 cm³/mL = 45 cm³.

## Density (1.5)

Density is mass divided by volume. Water's is 1.00 g/mL at 4 °C (0.998 at 20 °C); anything denser sinks in it, anything less dense floats. Some useful values, rounded from reference data:

| Substance | Density (g/cm³) |
|---|---|
| Air (20 °C, 1 atm) | 0.0012 |
| Ethanol | 0.789 |
| Water (20 °C) | 0.998 |
| Aluminium | 2.70 |
| Iron | 7.87 |
| Copper | 8.96 |
| Lead | 11.3 |
| Gold | 19.3 |

The formula rearranges three ways: D = m / V, m = D × V, V = m / D. Keep the units straight: a density in g/cm³ needs a volume in cm³, so convert a volume in mL (same thing) or L (× 1,000) first.

**Worked example.** A rectangular bar measures 2.00 cm × 3.00 cm × 10.0 cm and has a mass of 162 g. What is it made of?

V = 2.00 × 3.00 × 10.0 = 60.0 cm³. D = 162 g ÷ 60.0 cm³ = 2.70 g/cm³. The table says **aluminium**.

**Worked example.** What volume does 50.0 g of ethanol occupy?

V = m / D = 50.0 g ÷ 0.789 g/mL = 63.4 mL.

**Worked example.** A piece of metal is lowered into a graduated cylinder holding 25.0 mL of water; the level rises to 31.4 mL. The metal's mass is 57.3 g. Find its density.

V = 31.4 − 25.0 = 6.4 mL (one decimal place from the subtraction). D = 57.3 ÷ 6.4 = 8.95 → **9.0 g/cm³** (two significant figures, limited by 6.4). Copper (8.96) fits.

**Graph description:** a straight line through the origin on a plot of mass (g, vertical) against volume (cm³, horizontal). The slope of the line is the density. A steeper line means a denser substance; a lead line rises more steeply than an aluminium line.

**Try it.** A 15.0 mL sample of a liquid has a mass of 11.8 g. Is it more likely ethanol or water? Will a 2.0 cm³ cube of gold float on mercury (13.5 g/cm³)?
*Answers:* 11.8 ÷ 15.0 = 0.787 g/mL, ethanol. Gold (19.3) is denser than mercury, so it sinks.

## Energy, temperature and heat (1.6)

Energy is measured in **joules**. Particles have **kinetic energy** because they move (translate, rotate, vibrate) and **potential energy** because of their positions and the attractions between them. Chemical changes rearrange atoms and so change potential energy; that is where the heat of a burning match comes from, a topic for Unit 7.

**Temperature** measures the *average* kinetic energy of the particles. A cup of tea and a bath can be at the same temperature; the bath holds far more total energy because it has far more particles. **Heat** is energy moving from a hotter body to a colder one, and it stops flowing when the temperatures are equal. "The soup has a lot of heat" is loose; "the soup transfers heat to the spoon" is correct.

The **kelvin** scale starts at absolute zero, where particle motion is minimal. K = °C + 273.15. Because 0 K is a real zero, a sample at 400 K has twice the average kinetic energy of one at 200 K; the same is not true of 40 °C and 20 °C.

Energy units you will meet: 1 kJ = 1,000 J; the food calorie (Cal, actually a kilocalorie) is 4.184 kJ; a small calorie (cal) is 4.184 J.

**Worked example.** A snack label reads 150 Cal. How many kilojoules is that? 150 Cal × 4.184 kJ/Cal = **628 kJ** → 6.3 × 10² kJ to two significant figures.

## States of matter and the particle model (1.6)

The **particle model** (also called kinetic molecular theory, which Unit 5 develops for gases) says that all matter is made of tiny particles in constant motion that attract one another. Which state a substance is in depends on the balance between how fast the particles move (temperature) and how strongly they attract.

| | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shape | fixed | takes container's shape | fills container |
| Volume | fixed | fixed | fills container |
| Compressible? | no | barely | yes, easily |
| Particle arrangement | tightly packed, ordered | close but disordered | far apart |
| Particle motion | vibrate in place | slide past each other | move freely and fast |
| Attractions versus motion | attractions win | roughly balanced | motion wins |

Gases are compressible because their particles are far apart with empty space between; liquids and solids are not because their particles are already touching. Heating a solid makes its particles vibrate harder until they break free of their fixed positions and the solid **melts**; heating a liquid lets particles escape the surface (**evaporation**) and, at the boiling point, form bubbles of vapour throughout (**boiling**).

**The six phase changes.** Melting (solid → liquid) and freezing (liquid → solid); vaporization (liquid → gas; evaporation or boiling) and condensation (gas → liquid); sublimation (solid → gas, as dry ice does) and deposition (gas → solid, as frost forms). The first of each pair absorbs energy; the second releases it. During a phase change the temperature stays constant: the energy goes into separating particles (potential energy), not into speeding them up. Unit 5 draws this as a heating curve.

**Worked example.** Why can you compress air in a bicycle pump but not water in a syringe?

Air is a gas: its particles are far apart, so pushing the piston mostly closes empty space. Water's particles are already in contact; there is almost no space to remove, so the volume barely changes.

**Try it.** Name the phase change and say whether energy is absorbed or released: (a) frost forming on a window overnight; (b) wet pavement drying; (c) a candle's wax hardening.
*Answers:* (a) deposition, released; (b) evaporation (vaporization), absorbed; (c) freezing, released.

## Common mistakes

- Multiplying by a conversion factor upside down. Always write units and check that they cancel.
- Forgetting to cube the factor when converting cm³ to m³ (it is 10⁶, not 10²).
- Using a volume in litres with a density in g/mL.
- Reading a water-displacement volume as the final level instead of the difference.
- Confusing heat and temperature: temperature is average kinetic energy; heat is energy in transit.
- Saying the temperature rises during melting. It stays constant until all the solid has melted.
- Treating evaporation and boiling as the same: evaporation happens at the surface at any temperature, boiling throughout the liquid at the boiling point.
