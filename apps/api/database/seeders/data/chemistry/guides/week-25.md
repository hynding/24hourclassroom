## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| System and surroundings | The reaction (or object) you are studying, and everything else that can exchange energy with it. |
| Heat (q) | Energy transferred because of a temperature difference; in joules. Positive when absorbed by the system, negative when released. |
| Enthalpy change (ΔH) | The heat absorbed or released by a reaction at constant pressure; ΔH = H_products − H_reactants. |
| Exothermic | Releases heat to the surroundings; ΔH < 0; the surroundings warm up. |
| Endothermic | Absorbs heat from the surroundings; ΔH > 0; the surroundings cool down. |
| Thermochemical equation | A balanced equation with its ΔH, e.g. CH₄(g) + 2 O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(l), ΔH = −890 kJ. |
| Activation energy (Ea) | The energy barrier between reactants and products; the hump on an energy diagram. |
| Specific heat (c) | Energy to raise 1 g of a substance by 1 °C; water 4.184 J/(g·°C). |
| Heat capacity (C) | Energy to raise a whole object by 1 °C; C = mc. |
| Calorimeter | An insulated container in which a temperature change measures the heat of a process. |

## Exothermic and endothermic (7.1)

Every chemical reaction breaks some bonds and forms others. Breaking a bond always **costs** energy; forming a bond always **releases** energy. Whether the reaction as a whole releases or absorbs energy depends on the balance:

- If the bonds formed are stronger (release more) than the bonds broken (cost), energy is left over and flows out as heat: **exothermic**, ΔH negative. Combustion, neutralisation, rusting, respiration, and almost every reaction that happens "on its own" at room temperature.
- If the bonds broken cost more than the bonds formed give back, the deficit must come from the surroundings: **endothermic**, ΔH positive. Photosynthesis, baking soda with vinegar, the cold packs that use ammonium nitrate dissolving, and every melting or boiling.

The sign is from the **system's** point of view. An exothermic reaction loses enthalpy (ΔH < 0) and warms the surroundings; the thermometer in the beaker goes up. An endothermic one gains enthalpy (ΔH > 0) and cools the surroundings; the thermometer goes down. The classic confusion is to see a beaker getting cold and call the reaction "exothermic because it is giving off cold". Cold is not a thing that is given off; heat is flowing into the system.

### Thermochemical equations

A thermochemical equation attaches ΔH to a balanced equation, for the number of moles written:

CH₄(g) + 2 O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(l)   ΔH = −890 kJ
N₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2 NO(g)   ΔH = +181 kJ

Three rules follow from ΔH being a property of the amounts written:

1. **Scaling:** burn 2 mol of methane and ΔH is −1,780 kJ; burn 0.25 mol and it is −222.5 kJ.
2. **Reversing:** CO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(l) → CH₄(g) + 2 O₂(g) has ΔH = +890 kJ.
3. **Phases matter:** making water vapour instead of liquid water releases less, −802 kJ, because 88 kJ stays as the heat of vaporisation of 2 mol of water.

**Worked example.** How much heat is released when 10.0 g of methane (16.04 g/mol) burns completely?

10.0 ÷ 16.04 = 0.623 mol CH₄; 0.623 mol × (−890 kJ/mol) = **−555 kJ** (555 kJ released).

**Worked example.** The thermochemical equation 2 H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2 H₂O(l), ΔH = −572 kJ. What is ΔH for forming 1 mol of liquid water? For decomposing 3 mol of water?

Per mole of water: −572/2 = −286 kJ. Decomposing 3 mol: reverse (+286 kJ per mole) and scale: +858 kJ.

**Try it.** Using CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O(l), ΔH = −890 kJ, what mass of CO₂ forms when 445 kJ is released?
*Answer:* 445/890 = 0.500 mol CH₄ → 0.500 mol CO₂ → 22.0 g.

### Energy diagrams

Plot enthalpy (vertical) against the progress of the reaction (horizontal). Reactants sit at one level, products at another, and between them is a hump, the **activation energy** that the colliding molecules must supply before bonds can rearrange.

- Exothermic: products lower than reactants; ΔH is the drop; the hump still exists (that is why a match is needed to light methane, even though burning releases energy).
- Endothermic: products higher than reactants; ΔH is the climb; the hump is at least as tall as ΔH.

Figure description for an exothermic reaction: the curve starts at the reactant level, rises to a peak Ea above it, then falls to the product level a distance ΔH below the start. Week 27 uses Ea to explain reaction rates and catalysts.

## Specific heat (7.2)

Different materials need different amounts of energy to warm up. The **specific heat** c is the energy to raise one gram by one degree Celsius (or one kelvin; the degree is the same size):

q = m × c × ΔT, with ΔT = T_final − T_initial

| Substance | c, J/(g·°C) |
|---|---|
| water (liquid) | 4.184 |
| ethanol | 2.44 |
| ice | 2.09 |
| aluminium | 0.897 |
| iron | 0.449 |
| copper | 0.385 |
| lead | 0.129 |

Water's value is unusually high, which is why coastal climates are mild, why the body uses water to carry heat, and why a pot of water takes so long to boil while its metal handle gets hot in seconds. The sign of q follows ΔT: warming is positive (energy in), cooling is negative (energy out).

**Worked example.** Heat needed to warm 250 g of water from 20.0 °C to 95.0 °C?
q = 250 × 4.184 × 75.0 = 78,450 J = **78.5 kJ**.

**Worked example.** A 50.0 g block of copper cools from 80.0 °C to 25.0 °C. q = 50.0 × 0.385 × (25.0 − 80.0) = 50.0 × 0.385 × (−55.0) = **−1,059 J**: 1.06 kJ released.

**Worked example (finding a final temperature).** 5,000 J is added to 100 g of aluminium at 20.0 °C. Final temperature?
ΔT = q/(mc) = 5,000/(100 × 0.897) = 55.7 °C, so T_final = **75.7 °C**.

## Calorimetry (7.2)

A **coffee-cup calorimeter** is two nested foam cups with a lid and a thermometer, holding a known mass of water (or solution). A process happens inside; the water's temperature change tells you the heat, because the insulation keeps the energy inside:

q_water = m_water × 4.184 × ΔT and q_process = −q_water

The minus sign is the whole idea: whatever the water gained, the process lost, and vice versa. Dilute solutions are treated as if they were water (density 1.00 g/mL, c = 4.184).

**Worked example (heat of neutralisation).** 50.0 mL of 1.00 M HCl and 50.0 mL of 1.00 M NaOH, both at 21.0 °C, are mixed in a coffee-cup calorimeter; the temperature rises to 27.8 °C. Find ΔH per mole of water formed.

1. Mass of solution: 100.0 mL × 1.00 g/mL = 100.0 g.
2. q_solution = 100.0 × 4.184 × (27.8 − 21.0) = 100.0 × 4.184 × 6.8 = 2,845 J.
3. q_reaction = −2,845 J (the reaction released it).
4. Moles of water formed: 0.0500 mol HCl reacts with 0.0500 mol NaOH → 0.0500 mol H₂O.
5. ΔH = −2,845 J / 0.0500 mol = −56,900 J/mol = **−56.9 kJ/mol**, close to the accepted −57 kJ/mol for H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.

**Worked example (heat of solution).** 5.00 g of ammonium nitrate (80.04 g/mol) dissolves in 100.0 g of water in a calorimeter and the temperature falls from 22.0 °C to 18.1 °C.

q_water = 100.0 × 4.184 × (18.1 − 22.0) = −1,632 J (the water lost heat). q_dissolving = +1,632 J. Moles = 5.00/80.04 = 0.0625 mol. ΔH = +1,632/0.0625 = +26,100 J/mol = **+26.1 kJ/mol**, endothermic, which is why it is used in instant cold packs.

### The method of mixtures: specific heat of a metal

Heat a metal in boiling water (so it is at 100.0 °C), drop it into cool water in a calorimeter, and record the final temperature. The heat the metal lost equals the heat the water gained:

−(m_metal × c_metal × ΔT_metal) = m_water × 4.184 × ΔT_water

**Worked example.** 75.0 g of a metal at 100.0 °C is dropped into 50.0 g of water at 22.0 °C; the final temperature is 26.6 °C.

Water gains: 50.0 × 4.184 × 4.6 = 962 J. Metal loses 962 J over a drop of 73.4 °C: c = 962/(75.0 × 73.4) = **0.175 J/(g·°C)**. Comparing with the table, the metal is probably not lead (0.129) or copper (0.385); 0.175 is close to tin (0.228) or an alloy; a real lab would repeat and refine. The method is what matters: equal and opposite heats, each written with its own m, c and ΔT.

**Try it.** 20.0 g of iron at 95.0 °C is dropped into 40.0 g of water at 20.0 °C. Estimate the final temperature.
*Answer:* Let T be the final temperature. 20.0 × 0.449 × (T − 95.0) = −40.0 × 4.184 × (T − 20.0). 8.98T − 853.1 = −167.4T + 3,347; 176.4T = 4,200; T = 23.8 °C. Iron's small specific heat and mass barely warm the water.

## Common mistakes

- Wrong sign. Warming surroundings means an exothermic process and a negative ΔH. Write q_reaction = −q_water every time.
- Using the mass of the solute, or of one solution, instead of the total mass of the solution in the calorimeter.
- Forgetting to convert J to kJ, or dividing by the wrong number of moles (use the limiting reactant, or the moles of water formed).
- Scaling ΔH without scaling by the same factor, or reversing an equation without flipping the sign.
- Mixing up specific heat (per gram) with heat capacity (for the whole object).
- Assuming an exothermic reaction needs no activation energy.

## Self-check

1. A beaker gets cold as two solids are mixed. Exothermic or endothermic? Sign of ΔH?
2. 2 H₂O₂(l) → 2 H₂O(l) + O₂(g), ΔH = −196 kJ. Heat released by 0.500 mol of H₂O₂?
3. q to warm 80.0 g of ethanol (c = 2.44) by 15.0 °C?
4. 100.0 g of water in a calorimeter warms by 3.50 °C when a reaction occurs. q for the reaction?
5. Why does a swimming pool stay cool on a hot afternoon while the concrete around it burns your feet?

*Answers:* (1) Endothermic; ΔH > 0. (2) 0.500/2 × (−196) = −49.0 kJ, so 49.0 kJ released. (3) 80.0 × 2.44 × 15.0 = 2,928 J = 2.93 kJ. (4) q_water = 100.0 × 4.184 × 3.50 = 1,464 J; q_reaction = −1,464 J. (5) Water's specific heat (4.184) is about five times concrete's (around 0.88 J/(g·°C)), so the same sunlight raises its temperature far less.
