## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Voltaic (galvanic) cell | A device in which a spontaneous redox reaction produces an electric current. |
| Half-cell | One electrode in contact with a solution of its ions: Zn in Zn²⁺(aq), Cu in Cu²⁺(aq). |
| Anode | The electrode where oxidation happens. Negative in a voltaic cell; positive in an electrolytic cell. |
| Cathode | The electrode where reduction happens. Positive in a voltaic cell; negative in an electrolytic cell. |
| Salt bridge | A tube of gel containing an inert salt (KNO₃) that connects the two half-cells, lets ions flow and keeps each solution neutral. |
| Standard reduction potential, E° | The voltage of a half-reaction written as a reduction, measured against the hydrogen electrode (E° = 0.00 V) at 1 M and 25 °C. More positive = stronger pull for electrons. |
| Cell potential, E°cell | E°(cathode) − E°(anode), the voltage the cell produces. Positive for a spontaneous reaction. |
| Electrolysis | Using an external power supply to force a non-spontaneous redox reaction to occur. |
| Electrolytic cell | A cell in which electrolysis takes place. |
| Faraday's constant, F | 96,485 C per mole of electrons. |
| Electroplating | Electrolysis that deposits a thin layer of one metal on another; the object is the cathode. |

## 1. Separating the two halves

Last week, zinc in copper(II) sulfate exchanged electrons at the metal surface, and the energy came out as heat. Put the zinc in its own beaker of Zn²⁺ solution and the copper in a beaker of Cu²⁺ solution, join the metals with a wire, and connect the solutions with a salt bridge. The same reaction happens, but now the electrons have to travel through the wire to get from zinc to copper, and a current flows. That is a **voltaic cell**.

What happens where:

- **Anode (zinc):** Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻. Oxidation. Electrons leave this electrode, so it is the negative terminal. The zinc strip slowly dissolves.
- **Cathode (copper):** Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. Reduction. Electrons arrive here, so it is the positive terminal. Copper plates onto the strip and the blue solution fades.
- **External circuit:** electrons flow from anode to cathode through the wire (and through whatever you connect, such as a bulb or a voltmeter).
- **Salt bridge:** as Zn²⁺ builds up in the anode beaker and Cu²⁺ is removed from the cathode beaker, the solutions would become charged and the current would stop. Negative ions from the bridge (NO₃⁻) drift toward the anode, positive ions (K⁺) toward the cathode, keeping both solutions neutral and completing the circuit. No salt bridge, no current.

Chemists abbreviate the cell as Zn | Zn²⁺ || Cu²⁺ | Cu: anode on the left, the double line for the salt bridge, cathode on the right. "An ox, red cat" (anode oxidation, reduction cathode) applies to every cell, voltaic or electrolytic.

## 2. Reduction potentials and the cell voltage

A voltmeter across the Zn/Cu cell reads 1.10 V. The voltage measures how much more strongly the copper half-cell pulls electrons than the zinc half-cell does. To compare any pair, each half-reaction is measured against a hydrogen electrode set to 0.00 V and listed as a reduction:

| Reduction half-reaction | E° (V) |
| --- | --- |
| F₂ + 2e⁻ → 2F⁻ | +2.87 |
| Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻ | +1.36 |
| Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag | +0.80 |
| Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu | +0.34 |
| 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ | 0.00 |
| Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb | −0.13 |
| Ni²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Ni | −0.26 |
| Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Fe | −0.44 |
| Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Zn | −0.76 |
| Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al | −1.66 |
| Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Mg | −2.37 |
| Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na | −2.71 |
| Li⁺ + e⁻ → Li | −3.04 |

Read it this way. A more positive E° means the species on the left is a stronger oxidising agent (F₂ is the strongest). A more negative E° means the species on the right is a stronger reducing agent (lithium is the strongest). The metals from top to bottom are the activity series of unit 4 in reverse order: the most active metals, which displace everything, have the most negative potentials.

To build a cell from two half-reactions: the one with the higher E° runs as a reduction (cathode); the other is reversed and runs as an oxidation (anode). Then

E°cell = E°(cathode) − E°(anode)

using the table values as listed, without changing the sign of the anode's E° (the subtraction does that for you). Do **not** multiply E° by the coefficients used to balance electrons; voltage does not depend on the amount.

**Worked example.** Zinc and copper. Copper has the higher E° (+0.34 V), so Cu²⁺ is reduced at the cathode. E°cell = 0.34 − (−0.76) = **1.10 V**, as measured.

**Worked example.** Magnesium and silver. E°cell = 0.80 − (−2.37) = **3.17 V**. Overall: Mg + 2Ag⁺ → Mg²⁺ + 2Ag. The 2 in front of Ag⁺ balances the electrons but does not change the voltage.

A positive E°cell means the reaction as written is spontaneous and will drive a current. If you compute a negative value you have the electrodes backwards: swap them. A reaction with E°cell = 0 is at equilibrium, which is what a dead battery is.

**Try it.** Calculate E°cell for a cell made of aluminium and nickel half-cells, and write the overall reaction. (Answer: 2Al + 3Ni²⁺ → 2Al³⁺ + 3Ni; E°cell = −0.26 − (−1.66) = 1.40 V.)

## 3. Batteries

A battery is one or more voltaic cells packaged so the chemicals do not mix.

- **Alkaline dry cell (1.5 V).** Zinc powder is oxidised at the anode; manganese dioxide is reduced at the cathode; a paste of potassium hydroxide is the electrolyte. Not rechargeable: the products do not reform cleanly.
- **Lead-acid car battery (12 V).** Six 2 V cells in series. Discharge: Pb + PbO₂ + 2H₂SO₄ → 2PbSO₄ + 2H₂O. Lead is oxidised (0 → +2) at the anode; lead(IV) oxide is reduced (+4 → +2) at the cathode. Sulfuric acid is consumed, so the electrolyte's density falls as the battery discharges, which is how a garage tests it. Rechargeable: the alternator forces the reaction backwards, an electrolysis.
- **Lithium-ion (about 3.7 V per cell).** Lithium ions shuttle between a graphite anode and a metal-oxide cathode through a non-aqueous electrolyte (lithium reacts violently with water). Lithium's very negative E° and tiny mass give the highest energy per kilogram of any common battery. Rechargeable.
- **Hydrogen fuel cell.** Hydrogen is oxidised at the anode and oxygen reduced at the cathode, making only water, and the fuel is fed in continuously rather than stored inside. Overall 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, the same reaction as burning hydrogen, but the energy comes out as electricity instead of heat.

## 4. Corrosion is a voltaic cell you did not want

Rust forms where a drop of water sits on iron. One part of the surface is the anode (Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻), another part the cathode (O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻), and the water is the salt bridge. Salt water conducts better, so cars rust fastest in winter.

Two defences follow from the table. **Coat the iron** (paint, oil, a tin layer, or zinc in galvanised steel) to keep water and oxygen off. Or attach a **sacrificial anode**: a block of zinc or magnesium bolted to a ship's hull or buried beside a pipeline. The more active metal has the more negative E°, so it is oxidised in preference to the iron and feeds it electrons, keeping the iron as the cathode. The block corrodes and is replaced every few years. Galvanising works both ways: the zinc coating keeps water off, and if it is scratched the exposed iron still becomes the cathode of a zinc–iron cell.

## 5. Electrolysis: running redox backwards

Connect a power supply instead of a voltmeter and you can force electrons the wrong way. The reaction is non-spontaneous, so energy must be supplied, and the cell is **electrolytic**. The names stay with the processes, not the signs: the anode is still where oxidation happens and the cathode still where reduction happens. But now the power supply pulls electrons out of the anode, making it **positive**, and pushes them into the cathode, making it **negative**. Positive ions (cations) migrate to the cathode and are reduced; negative ions (anions) migrate to the anode and are oxidised.

- **Molten sodium chloride.** Cathode: Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na(l). Anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂(g) + 2e⁻. This is how sodium metal and chlorine are manufactured. The salt must be molten so the ions can move; nothing happens to the solid.
- **Water (with a little dissolved salt or acid to conduct).** Cathode: 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻. Anode: 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻. Overall 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂: twice as many moles of hydrogen as oxygen, so the gas collected at the cathode has twice the volume of the gas at the anode (5.4). Add a drop of indicator and the cathode turns basic, the anode acidic.
- **Aluminium.** Al₂O₃ dissolved in molten cryolite at about 950 °C; Al³⁺ is reduced at the carbon-lined cathode, and oxygen produced at the carbon anodes burns them away. The three electrons per aluminium make this one of the most electricity-hungry industrial processes, which is why recycling aluminium saves about 95 percent of the energy.
- **Electroplating.** Make the object the cathode, dip it in a solution of the plating metal's ions, and use a piece of the plating metal as the anode. Silver dissolves at the anode (Ag → Ag⁺ + e⁻) at the same rate that Ag⁺ plates onto the object at the cathode, so the solution's concentration stays constant.

## 6. Faraday's law: counting electrons with a clock and an ammeter

Charge is current multiplied by time: Q = I × t, with I in amperes (coulombs per second) and t in seconds. One mole of electrons carries 96,485 C, Faraday's constant. So:

moles of electrons = (I × t) ÷ 96,485
moles of metal = moles of electrons ÷ (electrons per ion)
mass = moles of metal × molar mass

**Worked example.** A current of 2.00 A runs for 30.0 min through a copper(II) sulfate solution. How much copper plates onto the cathode?

Q = 2.00 A × 1,800 s = 3,600 C. Moles e⁻ = 3,600 ÷ 96,485 = 0.0373 mol. Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu needs two electrons per atom: 0.0373 ÷ 2 = 0.01866 mol Cu. Mass = 0.01866 mol × 63.55 g/mol = **1.19 g**.

**Worked example.** The same 2.00 A for 30.0 min in a silver nitrate solution. Ag⁺ needs one electron per atom, so 0.0373 mol of silver forms: 0.0373 × 107.87 = **4.02 g**. The same charge deposits twice as many silver atoms as copper atoms, and silver atoms are heavier too.

**Try it.** How long must 1.50 A run to deposit 2.01 g of silver? (Answer: 2.01 ÷ 107.87 = 0.01863 mol Ag = 0.01863 mol e⁻; Q = 0.01863 × 96,485 = 1,798 C; t = 1,798 ÷ 1.50 = 1,199 s, about 20.0 min.)

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing up the signs of the electrodes.** Anode is negative in a voltaic cell and positive in an electrolytic cell; the name follows the process (oxidation), not the sign.
- **Changing the sign of the anode's E° and then also subtracting.** Use E°(cathode) − E°(anode) with the table values as listed; that is the whole calculation.
- **Multiplying E° by a coefficient.** Voltage is independent of amount.
- **Electrons through the salt bridge.** Electrons travel only in the wire; ions travel in the bridge.
- **Forgetting the electrons per ion in Faraday calculations.** Cu²⁺ needs two, Al³⁺ three, Ag⁺ one.
- **Minutes instead of seconds in Q = It.** Convert first.
- **Thinking electroplating puts the object at the anode.** The object receives metal, so it is where reduction happens: the cathode.
