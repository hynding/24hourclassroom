## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Reactant / product | What you start with (left of the arrow) / what forms (right of the arrow). |
| Coefficient | The whole number in front of a formula: how many of that particle. Changeable. |
| Subscript | The small number inside a formula: how many atoms in that particle. Fixed by the compound's identity. |
| State symbol | (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) dissolved in water. |
| Balanced equation | Equal numbers of every kind of atom on both sides; mass and charge conserved. |
| Diatomic elements | H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂: these seven exist as two-atom molecules when uncombined. |
| Synthesis (combination) | A + B → AB. Two or more substances form one. |
| Decomposition | AB → A + B. One substance breaks into two or more. |
| Single replacement | A + BC → AC + B. One element swaps places with another in a compound. |
| Double replacement | AB + CD → AD + CB. Two ionic compounds trade partners. |
| Combustion | Fuel + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O (for a hydrocarbon), releasing heat and light. |

## Reading and writing equations (4.1)

A chemical equation is a sentence about particles. "Hydrogen gas burns in oxygen to form water" becomes

2 H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2 H₂O(l)

Read it as: *two molecules of hydrogen react with one molecule of oxygen to produce two molecules of water*. The arrow means "produces" or "yields". The state symbols tell you the physical form of each substance at the conditions of the reaction; (aq) means the substance is dissolved in water, as every salt solution is.

Two kinds of numbers appear, and confusing them is the commonest error of the whole unit:

- **Subscripts** are part of the formula. H₂O is water; H₂O₂ is hydrogen peroxide, a different compound that bleaches hair. You never alter a subscript to balance an equation, because that changes what substance you are talking about.
- **Coefficients** count particles. 2 H₂O means two water molecules: 4 H atoms and 2 O atoms in total. A coefficient multiplies everything in the formula that follows it, including everything inside parentheses: 3 Ca(NO₃)₂ contains 3 Ca, 6 N and 18 O.

**Writing formulas from words.** The vocabulary comes from Unit 3. An ionic compound's formula follows from its ions (aluminum chloride is AlCl₃); a molecular compound's follows from its prefixes (dinitrogen tetroxide is N₂O₄). One extra rule: an element on its own is written as a single atom (Na, Fe, C, S, Ar) **except** the seven diatomic gases and liquids, H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂ and I₂. "Chlorine gas" is Cl₂; "a chlorine atom" in a compound carries no subscript of its own.

**Worked example.** Write the unbalanced equation for "solid aluminum reacts with chlorine gas to form solid aluminum chloride".
Al(s) + Cl₂(g) → AlCl₃(s). Chlorine is diatomic as a reactant; the product's formula comes from Al³⁺ and Cl⁻.

**Try it.** Write formulas for: magnesium metal plus oxygen gas giving magnesium oxide; hydrogen gas plus nitrogen gas giving ammonia gas.
*Answers:* Mg(s) + O₂(g) → MgO(s); H₂(g) + N₂(g) → NH₃(g). Neither is balanced yet.

## Balancing (4.1)

Week 4's law of conservation of mass says atoms are neither created nor destroyed, only rearranged. A balanced equation has the same number of each kind of atom on both sides. You balance by placing coefficients, and only coefficients.

A procedure that works for nearly every equation at this level:

1. Write correct formulas (check charges, diatomics, prefixes). Do not touch them again.
2. Count atoms of each element on each side. Treat a polyatomic ion that appears unchanged on both sides (SO₄, NO₃, OH) as one unit.
3. Balance metals first, then nonmetals other than H and O, then hydrogen, then **oxygen last** (it often appears in several places).
4. If an element appears alone (O₂, Cl₂, Fe), balance it last of all; a lone element's coefficient changes only one count.
5. If you end up needing a fraction, double every coefficient.
6. Check every element again, and reduce if all coefficients share a factor.

**Worked example.** Al + O₂ → Al₂O₃.
Al: 1 left, 2 right → 2 Al. O: 2 left, 3 right → need 6 of each: 3 O₂ and 2 Al₂O₃. Now Al: 2 left, 4 right → 4 Al.
**4 Al + 3 O₂ → 2 Al₂O₃.** Check: Al 4 = 4; O 6 = 6.

**Worked example (combustion).** C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O.
C: 3 → 3 CO₂. H: 8 → 4 H₂O. O on the right: 6 + 4 = 10 → 5 O₂.
**C₃H₈ + 5 O₂ → 3 CO₂ + 4 H₂O.** Check: C 3 = 3; H 8 = 8; O 10 = 6 + 4.

**Worked example (the fraction trick).** C₂H₆ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O.
C: 2 CO₂. H: 3 H₂O. O on the right: 4 + 3 = 7, which needs 7/2 O₂. Double everything: **2 C₂H₆ + 7 O₂ → 4 CO₂ + 6 H₂O.** Check: C 4 = 4; H 12 = 12; O 14 = 8 + 6.

**Worked example (polyatomic units).** Ca(OH)₂ + H₃PO₄ → Ca₃(PO₄)₂ + H₂O.
Ca: 3 Ca(OH)₂. PO₄ as a unit: 2 H₃PO₄. H: left 3 × 2 + 2 × 3 = 12 → 6 H₂O. O not in PO₄: left 6 (from OH), right 6 (from H₂O). ✓
**3 Ca(OH)₂ + 2 H₃PO₄ → Ca₃(PO₄)₂ + 6 H₂O.**

**Try it.** Balance: (a) Fe + Cl₂ → FeCl₃; (b) KClO₃ → KCl + O₂; (c) C₄H₁₀ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O.
*Answers:* (a) 2 Fe + 3 Cl₂ → 2 FeCl₃. (b) 2 KClO₃ → 2 KCl + 3 O₂. (c) 2 C₄H₁₀ + 13 O₂ → 8 CO₂ + 10 H₂O (the odd oxygen count forces doubling).

Why the coefficients matter beyond bookkeeping: in week 15 they become the **mole ratio**, the conversion factor that turns a mass of one substance into a mass of another. An unbalanced equation gives wrong ratios and therefore wrong masses.

## Five reaction types (4.2)

Most reactions in a first course fit one of five patterns. Classifying a reaction is the first step toward predicting its products (week 13).

| Type | General form | Fingerprint | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Synthesis | A + B → AB | Two or more reactants, **one** product | 2 Mg + O₂ → 2 MgO |
| Decomposition | AB → A + B | **One** reactant, two or more products | 2 H₂O₂ → 2 H₂O + O₂ |
| Single replacement | A + BC → AC + B | An **element** and a compound in, an element and a compound out | Zn + 2 HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂ |
| Double replacement | AB + CD → AD + CB | Two **compounds** in, two compounds out; ions swap partners | AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO₃ |
| Combustion | CₓHᵧ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O | O₂ is a reactant; CO₂ and H₂O are the products | CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O |

Notes on each:

- **Synthesis** often joins two elements (metal + nonmetal → ionic compound; nonmetal + nonmetal → molecular compound), but can join compounds: CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂; SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄.
- **Decomposition** usually needs energy: heat, electricity or light. Metal carbonates give the metal oxide and CO₂ (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂, the lime kiln); chlorates give the chloride and O₂; water electrolyses to H₂ and O₂ (2 H₂O → 2 H₂ + O₂).
- **Single replacement** is a metal pushing a less reactive metal (or hydrogen) out of a compound, or a halogen pushing out a less reactive halogen: Cu + 2 AgNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2 Ag; Cl₂ + 2 KBr → 2 KCl + Br₂. Whether it happens at all depends on the activity series (week 13).
- **Double replacement** happens in solution and "goes" only when one product leaves: an insoluble solid (a **precipitate**), a gas, or water. Acid + base → salt + water is the double replacement you will meet most: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O.
- **Combustion** of a hydrocarbon (or an alcohol or sugar, anything of C, H and possibly O) in enough oxygen gives CO₂ and H₂O and releases energy. A metal burning in oxygen (2 Mg + O₂ → 2 MgO) is both combustion and synthesis; name it synthesis when the product is a single compound.

**Figure description:** five rows of coloured circles. Synthesis: a red circle and a blue circle on the left, a joined red-blue pair on the right. Decomposition: the reverse. Single replacement: a lone green circle plus a red-blue pair on the left, a green-blue pair plus a lone red circle on the right. Double replacement: a red-blue pair plus a green-yellow pair on the left, a red-yellow pair and a green-blue pair on the right. Combustion: a cluster of black and white circles (a hydrocarbon) plus red pairs (O₂) on the left, black-red-red triples (CO₂) and white-red-white triples (H₂O) on the right.

**Worked example.** Classify: (i) 2 KBr + Cl₂ → 2 KCl + Br₂; (ii) 2 NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂; (iii) Pb(NO₃)₂ + 2 KI → PbI₂ + 2 KNO₃; (iv) C₂H₅OH + 3 O₂ → 2 CO₂ + 3 H₂O; (v) N₂ + 3 H₂ → 2 NH₃.
(i) An element and a compound in, an element and a compound out: **single replacement** (chlorine replaces bromine). (ii) One reactant, several products: **decomposition**. (iii) Two compounds swap ions, and a yellow solid PbI₂ precipitates: **double replacement**. (iv) O₂ in, CO₂ and H₂O out: **combustion**. (v) Two elements, one product: **synthesis**.

**Try it.** Classify and balance: Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu; Al + O₂ → Al₂O₃; HgO → Hg + O₂.
*Answers:* Single replacement, already balanced. Synthesis, 4 Al + 3 O₂ → 2 Al₂O₃. Decomposition, 2 HgO → 2 Hg + O₂.

## Common mistakes

- Changing a subscript to balance (writing H₂O₂ to get the oxygens to match). Only coefficients change.
- Forgetting the diatomic seven: writing O or Cl for the free element.
- Counting atoms inside parentheses without multiplying by both the subscript and the coefficient: 2 Al₂(SO₄)₃ holds 24 O atoms.
- Leaving a balanced equation with a common factor (4 H₂ + 2 O₂ → 4 H₂O should be reduced to 2, 1, 2).
- Calling a reaction decomposition because it has several products, when it also has several reactants (that is replacement or combustion).
- Treating a coefficient as a mass. "2 H₂ + O₂" does not mean 2 grams of hydrogen; it means two molecules (or, in week 14, two moles).
