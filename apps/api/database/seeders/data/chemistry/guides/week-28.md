## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Reversible reaction | One in which products can re-form reactants under the same conditions, written with ⇌. |
| Dynamic equilibrium | The state in which the forward and reverse reactions continue at equal rates, so the amounts of every substance stay constant. |
| Closed system | No matter enters or leaves. Equilibrium can be reached only in a closed system; an open flask lets a gas product escape and the reaction runs to completion. |
| Equilibrium constant, Kc | For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, Kc = [C]^c [D]^d ÷ [A]^a [B]^b, using equilibrium concentrations in mol/L. Solids and pure liquids are omitted. Fixed for a reaction at a given temperature. |
| Position of equilibrium | Which side is favoured at equilibrium: "lies to the right" means mostly products. |
| Le Châtelier's principle | If a change is imposed on a system at equilibrium, the position shifts in the direction that partly counteracts the change. |
| Shift | A temporary imbalance of the forward and reverse rates that carries the system to a new equilibrium with different amounts. |
| Haber process | N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g), ΔH = −92 kJ, run at about 450 °C and 200 atm over an iron catalyst to make ammonia for fertiliser. |

## 1. Reactions that go both ways

Most reactions you have written run to completion: the limiting reactant is used up and the arrow points one way. But many reactions can run backwards under the same conditions. Nitrogen dioxide, a brown gas, pairs up into colourless dinitrogen tetroxide; the N₂O₄ falls apart again into NO₂. In a sealed tube the colour settles at a pale brown that never changes, yet both reactions are still happening. The tube holds a **dynamic equilibrium**:

2NO₂(g) ⇌ N₂O₄(g)

Three facts define it:

1. The forward and reverse reactions are both running, at **equal rates**. That is what "dynamic" means: the system looks still because the two changes cancel.
2. The concentrations are **constant, not equal**. The equilibrium mixture may be mostly product, mostly reactant or anything between; constant means they stop changing.
3. It requires a **closed system**. If a product escapes, the reverse reaction has nothing to work with and the forward reaction runs to completion. Lime is made by heating limestone in an open kiln precisely so that the CO₂ blows away.

How equilibrium is reached is a rates story (7.4). At the start, only reactants are present, so the forward rate is at its highest and the reverse rate is zero. As products accumulate the reverse rate rises; as reactants are consumed the forward rate falls. When they meet, the concentrations stop changing. The same equilibrium is reached whether you start from reactants, from products or from a mixture.

## 2. The equilibrium constant

For the general reaction aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD at equilibrium,

Kc = [C]^c [D]^d ÷ [A]^a [B]^b

Products on top, reactants on the bottom, each concentration raised to its coefficient. Two conventions:

- **Solids and pure liquids are left out.** Their concentration does not change, so it is folded into the constant. For CaCO₃(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO₂(g), Kc = [CO₂].
- **Kc depends only on temperature.** Start with different amounts and the equilibrium concentrations will differ, but the ratio in the expression comes out the same. Change the temperature and you get a different Kc.

The size of Kc tells you where the equilibrium lies. Kc far above 1 (say 10⁵): the reaction goes nearly to completion; products dominate. Kc far below 1 (10⁻⁵): barely any product forms. Kc near 1: substantial amounts of both.

**Worked example.** At 700 K, an equilibrium mixture of H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g) contains [H₂] = 0.020 M, [I₂] = 0.020 M and [HI] = 0.140 M. Find Kc.

Kc = [HI]² ÷ ([H₂][I₂]) = (0.140)² ÷ (0.020 × 0.020) = 0.0196 ÷ 0.00040 = **49**. Products are favoured but not overwhelmingly: a fair amount of H₂ and I₂ remains.

**Worked example.** At 25 °C, [N₂O₄] = 0.040 M and [NO₂] = 0.012 M at equilibrium for N₂O₄(g) ⇌ 2NO₂(g). Kc = [NO₂]² ÷ [N₂O₄] = (0.012)² ÷ 0.040 = 0.000144 ÷ 0.040 = **0.0036**. The equilibrium lies well to the left: the tube is only pale brown.

**Try it.** Write Kc for 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g) and evaluate it for [SO₂] = 0.050 M, [O₂] = 0.025 M, [SO₃] = 0.200 M. (Answer: Kc = [SO₃]² ÷ ([SO₂]²[O₂]) = 0.0400 ÷ (0.0025 × 0.025) = 0.0400 ÷ 0.0000625 = 640.)

## 3. Le Châtelier's principle

Push on a system at equilibrium and it pushes back: the position of equilibrium shifts in whichever direction partly undoes what you did. The rule covers three kinds of change.

**Concentration.** Add a substance, and the equilibrium shifts away from it, consuming some of what you added. Remove a substance, and the equilibrium shifts toward it, replacing some of what you took. In rate terms, adding a reactant raises the forward rate above the reverse rate until the extra product has pushed the reverse rate up to match. Industry removes a product continuously so that the reaction keeps shifting to make more. Kc does not change; the concentrations simply move to a new set that gives the same ratio.

**Pressure (by changing volume), gases only.** Compressing a gas mixture raises its pressure. The equilibrium shifts toward the side with **fewer moles of gas**, which lowers the pressure a little. Expanding does the opposite. If both sides have the same number of gas moles, as in H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI (2 moles each side), changing the volume does nothing. Count only gases: solids and liquids do not count. Adding an unreactive gas such as argon at constant volume raises the total pressure but changes none of the concentrations, so nothing shifts.

**Temperature.** Treat heat as a reactant (endothermic) or a product (exothermic) and apply the concentration rule. For an exothermic reaction, heat is a product; raising the temperature "adds product," so the equilibrium shifts left toward reactants and Kc **decreases**. For an endothermic reaction, raising the temperature shifts right and Kc **increases**. Temperature is the only change that alters Kc, because it is the only one that changes the relative rates of the forward and reverse reactions in a way that does not cancel: both speed up, but the one with the larger activation energy speeds up more.

**Catalyst.** No shift at all. A catalyst lowers the activation energy of the forward and reverse reactions equally, so both speed up by the same factor. Equilibrium is reached sooner, with exactly the same composition.

| Change | Shift | Effect on Kc |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Add a reactant or remove a product | Right | None |
| Add a product or remove a reactant | Left | None |
| Increase pressure (decrease volume) | Toward fewer gas moles | None |
| Decrease pressure (increase volume) | Toward more gas moles | None |
| Raise temperature | Toward the endothermic direction | Changes (up for endothermic forward, down for exothermic forward) |
| Lower temperature | Toward the exothermic direction | Changes the other way |
| Add a catalyst | None; equilibrium reached faster | None |
| Add an inert gas at constant volume | None | None |

## 4. The Haber process: equilibrium in a factory

Ammonia for fertiliser is made from nitrogen and hydrogen:

N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g), ΔH = −92 kJ

Apply the table. Four moles of gas become two, so **high pressure** shifts right and raises the yield; plants run at about 200 atm, the limit of what is economical and safe. The reaction is exothermic, so **low temperature** favours ammonia. But at low temperature the N≡N bond (945 kJ/mol) is almost never broken in a collision, and the rate is hopeless. The compromise is a moderate **450 °C** with an **iron catalyst**, which gets the mixture to equilibrium quickly even though the equilibrium yield at that temperature is only about 15 percent. The ammonia is cooled and liquefied out of the stream (removing a product shifts the equilibrium right), and the unreacted gases are recycled. Everything about the design follows from this week's rules and last week's.

**Worked example.** A mixture of N₂, H₂ and NH₃ is at equilibrium in a cylinder with a piston. Predict the effect of (a) pushing the piston in, (b) heating the cylinder, (c) injecting more hydrogen, (d) adding powdered iron.

(a) Pressure rises; shift right toward 2 moles of gas; more NH₃, Kc unchanged. (b) Heat is a product; shift left; less NH₃, Kc decreases. (c) Shift right, consuming some of the added H₂; more NH₃ and more N₂ consumed; Kc unchanged. (d) Iron is the catalyst; no shift, same amounts, reached faster.

**Try it.** Methanol is made by CO(g) + 2H₂(g) ⇌ CH₃OH(g), ΔH = −90 kJ. State the pressure and temperature that maximise the equilibrium yield, and why a real plant would not use the ideal temperature. (Answer: high pressure, 3 gas moles → 1; low temperature, exothermic; but a low temperature makes the rate too slow, so a moderate temperature with a catalyst is used.)

## 5. Equilibria you have already met

- **A saturated solution** (6.1): solid ⇌ dissolved ions, dissolving and crystallising at equal rates; the concentration stays at the solubility.
- **A weak acid** (6.4): CH₃COOH ⇌ H⁺ + CH₃COO⁻ lies far to the left, which is exactly why a weak acid has a higher pH than a strong acid of the same concentration. Its Kc is the acid dissociation constant, Ka.
- **Water's autoionisation** (6.5): H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻, with Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C. Kw rises with temperature because the autoionisation is endothermic.
- **Vapour pressure** (5.6): liquid ⇌ vapour in a closed container, which is why vapour pressure depends only on temperature.
- **Carbon dioxide in blood and in fizzy drinks:** CO₂(g) ⇌ CO₂(aq); opening the bottle lowers the pressure and the equilibrium shifts to release gas.

## Common mistakes

- **"At equilibrium the concentrations are equal."** They are constant; the ratio Kc decides how unequal.
- **"At equilibrium the reaction has stopped."** Both reactions continue at equal rates.
- **Including solids or water in Kc.** Only gases and dissolved species appear.
- **Forgetting the exponents.** The coefficient 2 on HI makes [HI]², not 2[HI].
- **Saying a catalyst shifts the equilibrium or increases the yield.** It changes only how fast equilibrium is reached.
- **Saying Kc changes when a concentration or pressure changes.** Only temperature changes Kc.
- **Counting all moles for a pressure change.** Count gas moles only, and if they are equal on both sides there is no shift.
- **Reversing the temperature rule.** Write heat into the equation on the correct side (left for endothermic, right for exothermic) and then treat it like any reactant or product.
