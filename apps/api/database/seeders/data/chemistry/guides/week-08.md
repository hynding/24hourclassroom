# Week 08 · Ions, Ionic Bonds and Naming Ionic Compounds

**Topics:** 3.1 Ions and Ionic Bonding · 3.2 Naming Ionic Compounds and Polyatomic Ions
**Practices:** 2 (developing and using models), 6 (constructing explanations), 8 (communicating information)
**Spiral:** Week 7 ended with sodium handing an electron to chlorine. This week makes that the general rule: metals lose electrons to reach a noble-gas configuration, nonmetals gain, and the charges predict the formula. Valence electrons (week 6) and the group numbers (week 7) are the tools; electron configurations of ions (week 6) explain the charges.

## What you need to be able to do

- **3.1** Predict the charge of a main-group ion from its group, write the electron configuration of the ion, and explain why the octet rule describes what atoms do.
- **3.1** Describe an ionic bond as the attraction between oppositely charged ions, describe the crystal lattice, and connect lattice structure to the properties of ionic compounds (high melting point, brittleness, conductivity when molten or dissolved).
- **3.2** Write the formula of an ionic compound from its ions by balancing charge, including compounds with polyatomic ions and with transition metals of variable charge.
- **3.2** Name any ionic compound from its formula and write the formula from its name, using Roman numerals for variable-charge metals and the names of the common polyatomic ions.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Octet rule | Atoms gain, lose or share electrons to end up with eight valence electrons (two for H, Li, Be). |
| Cation | A positive ion, formed when a metal loses its valence electrons. Named like the element: sodium ion. |
| Anion | A negative ion, formed when a nonmetal gains electrons. Named with -ide: chloride, oxide, nitride. |
| Ionic bond | The electrostatic attraction between a cation and an anion. |
| Formula unit | The smallest whole-number ratio of ions in an ionic compound (NaCl, CaCl₂); there are no molecules. |
| Crystal lattice | The repeating three-dimensional arrangement of ions in an ionic solid. |
| Lattice energy | The energy released when gaseous ions come together to form the solid; larger for smaller, more highly charged ions. |
| Polyatomic ion | A charged group of covalently bonded atoms that acts as one unit: NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻, NH₄⁺. |
| Binary ionic compound | Two elements only, a metal and a nonmetal: MgO, AlCl₃. |
| Variable-charge metal | A transition metal that forms more than one cation; the Roman numeral in the name gives the charge: iron(III). |
| Electrolyte | A substance that conducts electricity when dissolved or molten because its ions are free to move. |

## Why atoms form ions (3.1)

Noble gases are unreactive because their outer level is full (ns² np⁶). Other atoms get the same arrangement by transferring electrons. Metals, with low ionization energies and one to three valence electrons, lose them: sodium (3s¹) becomes Na⁺ with neon's configuration; magnesium (3s²) becomes Mg²⁺; aluminium (3s² 3p¹) becomes Al³⁺. Nonmetals, with high electronegativities and five to seven valence electrons, gain: chlorine (3s² 3p⁵) becomes Cl⁻ with argon's configuration; oxygen becomes O²⁻; nitrogen becomes N³⁻. This is the **octet rule**: atoms tend to reach eight valence electrons.

| Group | 1 | 2 | 13 | 15 | 16 | 17 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Valence electrons | 1 | 2 | 3 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
| Ion charge | 1+ | 2+ | 3+ | 3− | 2− | 1− |
| Examples | Na⁺, K⁺ | Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺ | Al³⁺ | N³⁻, P³⁻ | O²⁻, S²⁻ | F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻ |

Groups 14 and 18 rarely form simple ions. Transition metals lose electrons too, but the count varies: iron forms Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺, copper Cu⁺ and Cu²⁺. A few main-group metals also vary (Pb²⁺/Pb⁴⁺, Sn²⁺/Sn⁴⁺). Silver (Ag⁺), zinc (Zn²⁺) and cadmium (Cd²⁺) are fixed.

**Worked example.** Write the electron configurations of Ca and Ca²⁺, and of S and S²⁻, and name the noble gas each ion matches.
Ca: [Ar] 4s² → Ca²⁺: [Ar] (argon). S: [Ne] 3s² 3p⁴ → S²⁻: [Ne] 3s² 3p⁶ (argon). Both ions have 18 electrons.

## The ionic bond and the lattice (3.1)

When sodium meets chlorine, each sodium atom gives one electron to a chlorine atom. The resulting Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions attract one another, and that attraction is the **ionic bond**. But the attraction is not between one pair. Each Na⁺ pulls on every nearby Cl⁻ and vice versa, so the ions pack into a **crystal lattice**: in sodium chloride each ion is surrounded by six of the opposite charge in a cubic pattern that repeats in all directions. There are no molecules; the formula NaCl states only the 1:1 ratio, a **formula unit**.

**Figure description:** a cube of alternating small grey spheres (Na⁺) and larger green spheres (Cl⁻), each sphere touching six neighbours of the other colour along the three axes; the pattern continues beyond the cube's edges.

The energy released when the lattice forms, the **lattice energy**, is large, and it explains the properties of ionic compounds:

- **High melting and boiling points.** Breaking the lattice means separating every ion from its neighbours; NaCl melts at 801 °C, MgO at 2,852 °C (smaller, doubly charged ions, stronger attraction).
- **Hard but brittle.** A blow slides one layer of ions over another, bringing like charges face to face; they repel and the crystal shatters.
- **Conduct electricity when molten or dissolved, not as solids.** Current needs mobile charges; in the solid the ions are locked in place. Dissolved or melted, they move freely: ionic compounds are **electrolytes**.
- **Many dissolve in water**, because water molecules pull the ions apart (Unit 6).

## Writing formulas (3.2)

An ionic compound is neutral, so the total positive charge equals the total negative charge. Find the smallest numbers of each ion that balance.

- Na⁺ and Cl⁻: one each, **NaCl**.
- Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻: one calcium needs two chlorides, **CaCl₂**.
- Al³⁺ and O²⁻: the least common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6, so two Al³⁺ (6+) and three O²⁻ (6−): **Al₂O₃**.
- Mg²⁺ and O²⁻: 2+ and 2− balance one to one, **MgO** (not Mg₂O₂; formula units are reduced to lowest terms).

The **criss-cross** shortcut swaps the charge numbers into subscripts (Al³⁺ O²⁻ → Al₂O₃), then reduces (Mg²⁺ O²⁻ → Mg₂O₂ → MgO). Check by multiplying out the charges.

**Polyatomic ions** stay together as a unit. When more than one is needed, put it in parentheses: Ca²⁺ with NO₃⁻ is **Ca(NO₃)₂**; NH₄⁺ with SO₄²⁻ is **(NH₄)₂SO₄**. No parentheses for a single polyatomic ion: NaNO₃, K₂SO₄.

| Polyatomic ion | Formula | Polyatomic ion | Formula |
|---|---|---|---|
| ammonium | NH₄⁺ | sulfate | SO₄²⁻ |
| hydroxide | OH⁻ | sulfite | SO₃²⁻ |
| nitrate | NO₃⁻ | carbonate | CO₃²⁻ |
| nitrite | NO₂⁻ | phosphate | PO₄³⁻ |
| acetate | C₂H₃O₂⁻ | chromate | CrO₄²⁻ |
| hydrogen carbonate (bicarbonate) | HCO₃⁻ | dichromate | Cr₂O₇²⁻ |
| cyanide | CN⁻ | permanganate | MnO₄⁻ |
| chlorate | ClO₃⁻ | hypochlorite | ClO⁻ |
| perchlorate | ClO₄⁻ | chlorite | ClO₂⁻ |

Patterns worth noticing: -ate has one more oxygen than -ite (nitrate NO₃⁻, nitrite NO₂⁻); per- -ate has one more than -ate and hypo- -ite one fewer than -ite (ClO₄⁻, ClO₃⁻, ClO₂⁻, ClO⁻, all 1−); the charge does not change along such a series. Ammonium is the only common polyatomic cation.

## Naming ionic compounds (3.2)

Name the cation, then the anion. The cation keeps the element's name; a monatomic anion takes the stem plus **-ide**; a polyatomic ion keeps its own name. No prefixes (di-, tri-) are used for ionic compounds, because the charges already fix the ratio.

- NaCl: sodium chloride. MgO: magnesium oxide. Al₂O₃: aluminium oxide. K₂S: potassium sulfide.
- Ca(NO₃)₂: calcium nitrate. (NH₄)₂SO₄: ammonium sulfate. NaHCO₃: sodium hydrogen carbonate.

**Variable-charge metals** need a Roman numeral giving the charge on the cation (the **Stock system**). Work the charge out from the anions: in FeCl₃ three Cl⁻ make 3−, so iron is 3+: **iron(III) chloride**. In FeO oxygen is 2−, so **iron(II) oxide**; in Fe₂O₃ three O²⁻ are 6−, shared by two irons, 3+ each: **iron(III) oxide**. Cu₂O is copper(I) oxide; CuO is copper(II) oxide. The Roman numeral is the charge of one cation, never the subscript.

Metals that need a numeral: most transition metals (Fe, Cu, Co, Ni, Cr, Mn, Hg, Sn, Pb, Au). Metals that never do: groups 1 and 2, Al, Zn, Ag, Cd.

**Worked example.** Name Cr₂(SO₄)₃ and write the formula of lead(IV) oxide.
Three sulfates are 6−; two chromiums share 6+, so each is 3+: **chromium(III) sulfate**. Lead(IV) is Pb⁴⁺; oxide is O²⁻; two oxides balance one lead: **PbO₂**.

**Worked example.** Write the formulas of magnesium phosphate and ammonium carbonate.
Mg²⁺ and PO₄³⁻: LCM 6, three Mg and two PO₄: **Mg₃(PO₄)₂**. NH₄⁺ and CO₃²⁻: **(NH₄)₂CO₃**.

**Try it.** Name: CuBr₂, Na₂SO₃, Zn(OH)₂, Co₂O₃. Write: potassium permanganate, iron(II) phosphate, calcium hydrogen carbonate, aluminium nitrate.
*Answers:* copper(II) bromide; sodium sulfite; zinc hydroxide; cobalt(III) oxide. KMnO₄; Fe₃(PO₄)₂; Ca(HCO₃)₂; Al(NO₃)₃.

## Common mistakes

- Writing Mg₂O₂ instead of MgO, or NaCl₂ for sodium chloride. Balance charges, then reduce.
- Forgetting parentheses around a multiplied polyatomic ion (CaNO₃₂ is meaningless), or adding them around a single one.
- Using prefixes in ionic names ("dichloride"); prefixes belong to molecular compounds (week 9).
- Putting a Roman numeral on a fixed-charge metal ("sodium(I) chloride") or leaving it off iron, copper or lead.
- Taking the Roman numeral from the subscript: Fe₂O₃ is iron(III), not iron(II).
- Confusing -ide (monatomic anion) with -ite/-ate (oxygen-containing polyatomic ions): sulfide S²⁻, sulfite SO₃²⁻, sulfate SO₄²⁻.
- Saying an ionic compound is "made of molecules". It is a lattice of ions.
