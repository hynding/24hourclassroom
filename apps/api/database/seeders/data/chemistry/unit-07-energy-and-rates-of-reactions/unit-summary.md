## The ideas that generate every question

1. **Energy in a reaction comes from bonds.** Breaking bonds costs energy; forming bonds releases it. If the bonds formed release more than the bonds broken cost, ΔH is negative (exothermic) and the surroundings warm; if less, ΔH is positive (endothermic) and the surroundings cool. "Energy stored in bonds" is the slogan to unlearn.
2. **ΔH belongs to the equation as written.** Double the coefficients, double ΔH; reverse the equation, flip the sign. A thermochemical equation is a recipe with a heat term attached.
3. **Heat is measured by what it does to water.** q = m × c × ΔT, with c = 4.184 J/(g·°C). The heat a reaction gives the water is the negative of the heat the reaction lost; divide by the moles that reacted to get ΔH per mole.
4. **Enthalpy is a state function.** Only the start and end matter, so ΔH values add (Hess's law), formation enthalpies can be combined as products minus reactants, and the result is the same no matter which route you imagine.
5. **Rate is about collisions.** Only collisions with energy above the activation energy and the right orientation react. Concentration, pressure and surface area change how often particles collide; temperature changes, above all, the fraction that collide hard enough; a catalyst lowers the barrier.
6. **ΔH and Ea are independent.** How much energy a reaction releases says nothing about how fast it goes. Exothermic wood waits for a match.
7. **Equilibrium is two rates meeting.** When the forward and reverse rates become equal, concentrations stop changing but neither reaction stops. Kc measures where the balance sits; Le Châtelier predicts which way it moves when pushed; only temperature changes Kc; a catalyst changes only how soon you get there.

## Lists to memorise

- **Signs of an exothermic process:** ΔH < 0; surroundings warm; products below reactants on the energy diagram; combustion, neutralisation, freezing, condensing, most dissolving of strong acids and bases. Endothermic: the reverses, plus melting, boiling, photosynthesis, cold packs (ammonium nitrate dissolving), limestone decomposing.
- **Three ways to measure a rate:** gas volume in a syringe against time; mass loss on a balance; a colour change or the disappearance of a cross under a flask.
- **Six rate factors and their collision reason:** concentration (more collisions), pressure of a gas (more collisions), temperature (more collisions and, far more, a larger fraction above Ea), surface area (more particles exposed to collision), catalyst (lower Ea pathway, not consumed, ΔH unchanged), nature of the reactants (ions react instantly; strong covalent bonds react slowly).
- **Le Châtelier in one line each:** add a substance → shift away from it; remove a substance → shift toward it; raise pressure → toward fewer gas moles; raise temperature → toward the endothermic direction (Kc changes); catalyst → no shift; inert gas at constant volume → no shift.
- **Haber process:** N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, ΔH = −92 kJ; about 200 atm (fewer gas moles on the right), about 450 °C (compromise between yield and rate), iron catalyst, ammonia liquefied out, gases recycled.

## Which method for ΔH

| You are given | Do this |
| --- | --- |
| A temperature change of a known mass of water | q = mcΔT, flip the sign for the reaction, divide by moles |
| Two or three equations with ΔH values | Hess's law: arrange so target species are on the correct sides, add |
| A table of ΔH°f and a balanced equation | Products minus reactants with coefficients; elements zero |
| A bond-energy table and Lewis structures | Bonds broken minus bonds formed (gases only, estimate) |
| ΔH for an equation and a mass of reactant | Mass → moles → multiply by ΔH per mole from the equation |

## Common errors

- **Sign slips.** The water warming means the reaction lost heat: q(reaction) is negative. ΔT is final minus initial.
- **Dividing by the wrong moles.** Use the moles of the limiting reactant, or of the species the equation is written per mole of, not the total moles of everything.
- **Not scaling or flipping ΔH** when an equation is doubled, halved or reversed.
- **Reactants minus products.** The order is always products first.
- **A nonzero value for an element.** O₂(g), N₂(g), C(graphite), metals: all zero. Only O₃, diamond and the like have values.
- **Miscounting bonds.** Draw the Lewis structures: CO₂ has two double bonds; water has two O–H bonds.
- **"Concentration gives the particles more energy."** No: more collisions. Energy is temperature's job.
- **"A catalyst shifts the equilibrium" or "makes more product."** It changes neither the position nor Kc, only the time.
- **"At equilibrium the concentrations are equal" or "the reaction stops."** Constant, not equal; both reactions continue.
- **Solids and water in Kc.** Leave them out.
- **Counting all moles for a pressure shift.** Gas moles only.

## Worked example: calorimetry to ΔH per mole

A 50.0 mL volume of 1.00 M HCl is mixed with 50.0 mL of 1.00 M NaOH in a coffee-cup calorimeter. The temperature rises from 21.0 °C to 27.8 °C. Find ΔH per mole of water formed, assuming the solution has the density and specific heat of water.

Mass of solution = 100.0 g. q(water) = 100.0 g × 4.184 J/(g·°C) × 6.8 °C = 2,845 J. The reaction released this heat: q(reaction) = −2,845 J. Moles of HCl = 1.00 mol/L × 0.0500 L = 0.0500 mol, and the same of NaOH, so 0.0500 mol of water forms. ΔH = −2,845 J ÷ 0.0500 mol = −56,900 J/mol = **−56.9 kJ/mol**. The literature value for strong acid–strong base neutralisation is about −57 kJ/mol; heat lost to the cup makes a student's result slightly smaller in magnitude.

## Worked example: one reaction, four questions

Hydrogen and iodine: H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g). Bond energies: H–H 436, I–I 151, H–I 299 kJ/mol. At 700 K an equilibrium mixture has [H₂] = [I₂] = 0.020 M and [HI] = 0.140 M.

1. **ΔH by bond energies:** broken 436 + 151 = 587; formed 2 × 299 = 598; ΔH ≈ −11 kJ. Slightly exothermic.
2. **Rate:** if [HI] rises from 0 to 0.070 M in 35 s, the average rate of HI formation is 0.0020 M/s. Raising the temperature increases the rate because more collisions exceed Ea; adding a catalyst would also, without changing the next two answers.
3. **Kc:** (0.140)² ÷ (0.020 × 0.020) = 49. Products favoured but not overwhelmingly.
4. **Le Châtelier:** compressing the mixture does nothing (2 gas moles each side); adding I₂ shifts right and makes more HI; heating shifts left, because the forward reaction is exothermic, and lowers Kc.

## What to review from earlier units

- **Energy, temperature and the joule (1.6):** temperature measures average kinetic energy; 1 cal = 4.184 J; heat flows from hot to cold.
- **Covalent bonds and Lewis structures (3.3):** bond energies and bond counting both start from a correct Lewis structure.
- **Balancing and stoichiometry (4.1, 4.4, 4.6):** every ΔH is tied to a balanced equation, and mass → moles is the first step of every calorimetry and "heat from a given mass" problem.
- **Kinetic molecular theory and the gas laws (5.1, 5.3):** collisions and the energy distribution are KMT; pressure shifts in equilibrium are PV = nRT thinking.
- **Phase changes (5.5):** heating curves use the same q = mcΔT, and ΔH of vaporisation is the gap between H₂O(l) and H₂O(g) formation values.
- **Solutions and acids (6.1, 6.4, 6.5):** a saturated solution, a weak acid's partial ionisation and Kw are equilibria; the neutralisation you titrated is the reaction whose heat you measured.

## Last-minute checklist

- Can you state the sign of ΔH from a temperature change, an energy diagram, or a description, and give the bond-energy reason?
- Can you run q = mcΔT, flip the sign, and divide by the right number of moles?
- Can you reverse, scale and add three equations to reach a target, and do products minus reactants with the coefficients?
- Can you count bonds from Lewis structures and say why the bond method is an estimate?
- Can you compute an average rate from a table and label an energy diagram with Ea(forward), Ea(reverse) and ΔH?
- Can you explain each rate factor in collision-theory language, including the energy-distribution reason for temperature?
- Can you write Kc for any equation, evaluate it, and predict every Le Châtelier shift with its reason and its effect on Kc?
- Can you explain the Haber process conditions as a compromise between yield and rate?
