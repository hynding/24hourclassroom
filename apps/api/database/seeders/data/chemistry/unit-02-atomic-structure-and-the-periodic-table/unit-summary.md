## The ideas that generate every question

1. **Atoms explain the mass laws.** Conservation of mass and definite proportions are what you see; Dalton's rearranging atoms are why. Multiple proportions (CO versus CO₂) is the fingerprint of whole-number atom ratios.
2. **The atom has parts, and the evidence says where they are.** Thomson's cathode rays gave the electron; the gold foil's back-scattered alpha particles gave the tiny, dense, positive nucleus; Chadwick's neutron filled in the mass.
3. **Protons define, neutrons vary, electrons react.** Z = protons = element; A = protons + neutrons; isotopes share Z; ions change electrons only. The table's decimal is an abundance-weighted average of isotope masses.
4. **Unstable nuclei decay on a fixed schedule.** Alpha (A − 4, Z − 2), beta (Z + 1), gamma (no change). Equations balance mass number and atomic number. Half-life halves the sample each period, whatever the conditions.
5. **Electrons sit in fixed energy levels.** Line spectra come from drops between levels (E = hf, c = λf). The quantum model fills orbitals by Aufbau, Pauli and Hund; valence electrons are the outer s and p.
6. **The periodic table is the Aufbau order drawn out.** Period = highest n; group = valence count; blocks = sublevels. Trends follow effective nuclear charge (up across a period) and shielding (up down a group).

## Key terms and formulas

| Item | What to know |
|---|---|
| Conservation of mass | Products' mass = reactants' mass; gases count. |
| Definite proportions | A compound's mass percent is fixed (water 11.2% H). Use the ratio as a conversion factor. |
| Particles | p⁺ 1 u in nucleus; n⁰ 1 u in nucleus; e⁻ 1/1,836 u outside. |
| Counting | protons = Z; neutrons = A − Z; electrons = Z − charge. |
| Average atomic mass | Σ (fraction × isotope mass). Use fractions, not percents. Nearest the dominant isotope. |
| Unknown abundance | m₁(1 − x) + m₂x = average; solve for x. |
| Alpha / beta / gamma | ⁴He (2+, stopped by paper) / electron (1−, stopped by Al) / photon (0, needs lead). |
| Half-life | Remaining = original × (1/2)ⁿ, n = time ÷ half-life. 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16 … |
| Isotopes to know | C-14 5,730 y (dating); I-131 8.02 d; Tc-99m 6.0 h; Co-60 5.27 y; U-238 4.47 × 10⁹ y. |
| Light | c = λf (c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s); E = hf (h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s); 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m. Short λ = high E. |
| Hydrogen lines | Drops to n = 2: 656 (red), 486, 434, 410 nm. Levels bunch together as n rises. |
| Sublevels | s 2, p 6, d 10, f 14 electrons; level n holds 2n². |
| Filling order | 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p 5s 4d 5p. Fe = [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶. |
| Rules | Aufbau (lowest first); Pauli (2 per orbital, opposite spins); Hund (one per orbital before pairing). |
| Families | Group 1 ns¹ (1+); group 2 ns² (2+); group 17 ns² np⁵ (1−); group 18 full. |
| Radius | Decreases across, increases down. Cations smaller than atoms, anions larger. |
| Ionization energy | Increases across, decreases down. Big jump after valence electrons are gone. |
| Electronegativity | Increases across, decreases down; F 3.98 highest, Cs 0.79 lowest. |
| Reactivity | Metals: more reactive down a group. Nonmetals: more reactive up a group. |

## Common errors

- Reading the table's decimal as a mass number, or an unweighted average of isotope masses.
- Using percents instead of fractions in the average-mass sum (answer 100 times too big).
- Changing proton number when forming an ion; forgetting electrons = Z − charge.
- Beta decay *raises* Z; alpha decay drops A by 4 and Z by 2. Check both numbers balance.
- Dividing by the number of half-lives instead of halving repeatedly.
- Long wavelength is *low* energy; light is emitted when the electron drops, not when it absorbs.
- Filling 3d before 4s; pairing electrons before each orbital of a sublevel has one.
- Reversing the radius trend across a period; counting d electrons as valence electrons.

## Worked example

Chlorine: Z = 17, isotopes Cl-35 (34.969 u, 75.76%) and Cl-37 (36.966 u, 24.24%).

1. **Particles in Cl-37:** 17 p, 20 n, 17 e. In Cl⁻ (from Cl-35): 17 p, 18 n, 18 e.
2. **Average atomic mass:** 0.7576 × 34.969 + 0.2424 × 36.966 = 26.49 + 8.96 = 35.45 u. Closer to 35 because Cl-35 dominates.
3. **Configuration:** 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁵ = [Ne] 3s² 3p⁵. Period 3 (highest n), group 17 (seven valence electrons), halogen, p block. 3p boxes [↑↓][↑↓][↑ ]: one unpaired electron.
4. **Ion:** gains one electron to reach argon's configuration: Cl⁻, [Ne] 3s² 3p⁶, larger than the atom (181 pm versus 99 pm).
5. **Trends:** Chlorine is smaller than sulfur (more protons, same level) and larger than fluorine (extra level). Its ionization energy (1,251 kJ/mol) is above sulfur's and below argon's; its electronegativity (3.16) is below fluorine's and above bromine's. It is a more reactive nonmetal than bromine, less than fluorine.
6. **Nuclear:** Chlorine-36 is radioactive (half-life about 301,000 years) and decays by beta emission to argon-36: 36 = 36 + 0, 17 = 18 + (−1). After three half-lives (about 900,000 years) one eighth of a sample remains.
7. **Spectrum:** the green-yellow glow of a chlorine discharge is a line spectrum; each line is a photon with E = hf emitted as an electron drops between two of chlorine's fixed energy levels.

## What the unit exam looks like

About 44 items in roughly 75 minutes. Two stimulus sets (a mass-spectrum table leading to an average mass and isotope questions; a period-3 trend table), multiple choice spanning all eight topics, select-all, true/false, fill-in-the-blank, four numeric items (average atomic mass, a half-life, a photon frequency or energy, a conservation-of-mass calculation), two short answers and one long answer on atomic models or periodic trends. Bring the constants c and h; the periodic table is provided.
