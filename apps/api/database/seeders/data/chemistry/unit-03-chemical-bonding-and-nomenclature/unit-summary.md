## The ideas that generate every question

1. **Atoms bond to reach a full valence shell.** Metals lose their few valence electrons (cations, noble-gas configuration behind them); nonmetals gain or share to complete an octet. The number of electrons lost or gained is read off the group number.
2. **Metal + nonmetal = transfer = ionic.** Oppositely charged ions stack in a lattice; the formula is the lowest whole-number ratio that cancels charge. Lattice energy, high melting points, brittleness and conduction only when molten or dissolved all follow from a lattice of charged particles.
3. **Nonmetal + nonmetal = sharing = covalent.** A Lewis structure accounts for every valence electron: skeleton, remaining electrons as lone pairs on outer atoms, then the centre, then multiple bonds if the centre is short. Bond order sets bond length and strength.
4. **Names are rules, not memory.** Ionic: cation then anion, Roman numeral for a variable-charge metal, no prefixes. Molecular: prefixes for both elements, "-ide" ending. Acids: hydro-…-ic for binary, -ate → -ic acid, -ite → -ous acid.
5. **Shape comes from electron repulsion.** Count electron domains on the central atom (a multiple bond is one domain): 2 linear, 3 trigonal planar, 4 tetrahedral; then name the shape by the atoms alone. Lone pairs squeeze angles.
6. **Polarity is bond polarity plus shape.** Electronegativity difference makes a bond polar; the molecule is polar only if the bond dipoles fail to cancel (lone pairs on the centre, or different outer atoms).
7. **Forces between molecules set physical properties.** Dispersion (everything, grows with electron count) < dipole-dipole (polar molecules) < hydrogen bonding (H on N, O or F) ≪ ionic, covalent-network and metallic bonding. Stronger forces mean higher melting and boiling points and lower vapour pressure.
8. **Four solid types, four property sets.** Ionic, molecular, covalent-network and metallic solids are told apart by melting point, hardness and when (if ever) they conduct.

| Roman numerals | Charge of the metal, found from the anion: Fe₂O₃ → iron(III) oxide; CuCl → copper(I) chloride. |
| Prefixes | mono (dropped on the first element), di, tri, tetra, penta, hexa: N₂O₄ dinitrogen tetroxide; CO carbon monoxide. |
| Acids | HCl hydrochloric; HNO₃ nitric; HNO₂ nitrous; H₂SO₄ sulfuric; H₂SO₃ sulfurous; H₃PO₄ phosphoric; HC₂H₃O₂ acetic; H₂CO₃ carbonic. |
| Lewis structure count | Sum valence electrons (add one per negative charge, subtract per positive); pairs placed = count ÷ 2. Octet for C, N, O, F; H has 2; B often 6; period 3+ may exceed 8. |

| VSEPR shapes | 2 domains linear 180°; 3/0 trigonal planar 120°; 3/1 bent ~118°; 4/0 tetrahedral 109.5°; 4/1 trigonal pyramidal ~107°; 4/2 bent ~104.5°. |
| Polar molecules | H₂O, NH₃, HCl, SO₂, CHCl₃, CH₃Cl. Nonpolar: CO₂, CH₄, CCl₄, BF₃, SO₃, all diatomic elements. |
| IMF order | Dispersion (all; up with molar mass and surface area) < dipole-dipole < hydrogen bond (N–H, O–H, F–H). |

| Alloys | Substitutional (brass, Cu/Zn) or interstitial (steel, C in Fe); harder than the pure metal because layers cannot slide as freely. |

## Common errors

- Writing Mg₂O₂ or Ca₂Cl₂: always reduce an ionic formula to the lowest ratio (but never reduce inside a polyatomic ion: Na₂O₂ sodium peroxide is a real exception you will not be asked about).
- Using prefixes in ionic names (not "dicopper oxide") or Roman numerals in molecular names.
- Reading the Roman numeral as a subscript; it is the metal's charge.
- Forgetting the charge when counting electrons for a polyatomic ion's Lewis structure (NH₄⁺ has 8, not 9).
- Counting a double bond as two domains; naming water tetrahedral instead of bent.
- Calling CO₂ polar because its bonds are. Shape decides whether dipoles cancel.
- Confusing intramolecular bonds (what breaks in a reaction) with intermolecular forces (what breaks on boiling). Boiling water breaks hydrogen bonds, not O–H bonds.
- Claiming hydrogen bonding wherever hydrogen appears: CH₄ has none.
- Saying ionic solids conduct electricity; they conduct only when the ions are free, molten or dissolved.

## Worked example

Take the compound formed by phosphorus and chlorine, PCl₃, and the salt iron(III) sulfate.

1. **Type of bonding:** P and Cl are both nonmetals, ΔEN = 3.16 − 2.19 = 0.97, so PCl₃ is molecular with polar covalent bonds. Iron(III) sulfate is metal + polyatomic anion: ionic.
2. **Formula of the salt:** Fe³⁺ and SO₄²⁻; lowest common multiple of charge 6 → Fe₂(SO₄)₃. The parentheses say three whole sulfate ions.
3. **Name of the molecule:** phosphorus trichloride (no "mono" on phosphorus).
4. **Lewis structure of PCl₃:** 5 + 3 × 7 = 26 electrons, 13 pairs. Three P–Cl bonds use 3 pairs; nine lone pairs complete the chlorines; the last pair sits on phosphorus. Every atom has an octet.
5. **Shape:** 4 domains (3 bonds + 1 lone pair) → tetrahedral electron geometry, **trigonal pyramidal** shape, angle about 100° (less than 109.5° because the lone pair squeezes).
6. **Polarity:** polar P–Cl bonds plus a lone pair on the central atom: the bond dipoles do not cancel. PCl₃ is **polar**, δ− at the chlorines.
7. **Intermolecular forces:** dipole-dipole and dispersion; no hydrogen bonding (no H). Molar mass 137.3 g/mol gives substantial dispersion forces; PCl₃ is a liquid that boils at 76 °C.
8. **Compare the solids:** Fe₂(SO₄)₃ is an ionic solid: high melting point, conducts only in solution. Frozen PCl₃ is a molecular solid held by weak IMFs: melts at −94 °C, never conducts.

## What the unit exam looks like

About 44 items in roughly 75 minutes. Two stimulus sets (a table of four unknown solids with melting points, hardness and conductivity; a table of three molecules' formulas, electronegativity differences and shapes leading to polarity and boiling-point ranking), multiple choice spanning all seven topics with emphasis on naming and formulas, select-all, true/false, fill-in-the-blank, four numeric items (electrons in a Lewis structure, an electronegativity difference, a mass percent of an alloy, a formula-unit count), two short answers and one long answer on explaining physical properties from bonding and intermolecular forces. The periodic table with electronegativities is provided; the polyatomic ion list is not.
