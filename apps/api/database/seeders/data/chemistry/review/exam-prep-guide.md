# Chemistry · Final Exam Prep Guide

**Exam:** a two-hour practice final (week 34), 64 items in course order · **Built from:** all eight units of this course, weighted as `course.yaml` weights them · **Use this guide:** in week 33, alongside the eight unit night-before sheets.

## The format

| Part | Items | Points | How it is graded |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Multiple choice | 40 | 40 | Automatically; one answer each, four options |
| Select all that apply | 3 | 6 | Automatically with partial credit; 2 points each |
| True/false | 4 | 4 | Automatically |
| Fill in the blank | 4 | 4 | Matched automatically against accepted answers |
| Numeric | 8 | 8 | Automatically, within a stated tolerance; the prompt tells you the units and decimal places |
| Short answer | 3 | 9 | By your teacher against scoring notes; 3 points each, parts (a), (b), (c) |
| Long answer | 2 | 12 | By your teacher against a point-by-point rubric; 6 points each |
| **Total** | **64** | **83** | |

Three stimulus sets (a table of molecules, a gas experiment, an industrial equilibrium) each carry three consecutive items; read the stimulus once and answer all three before moving on. Pace: about 90 seconds per short item leaves 25 minutes for the five written items. Answer every automatically graded item; there is no penalty for a wrong answer.

## Formulas you must know by heart

| | Formula | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Density | d = m ÷ V | g/mL or g/cm³ |
| Temperature | K = °C + 273.15 | Every gas law uses kelvin |
| Average atomic mass | Σ(isotope mass × fractional abundance) | |
| Half-life | remaining = original × (½)ⁿ, n = time ÷ half-life | |
| Moles | n = mass ÷ molar mass; particles = n × 6.022 × 10²³ | |
| Percent composition | (mass of element in formula ÷ molar mass) × 100 | |
| Percent yield | (actual ÷ theoretical) × 100 | |
| Combined gas law | P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂ | Boyle, Charles and Gay-Lussac are special cases |
| Ideal gas law | PV = nRT | R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K) or 8.314 J/(mol·K) |
| Molar volume at STP | 22.4 L/mol | 0 °C, 1 atm |
| Dalton's law | P(total) = P₁ + P₂ + … ; P(gas) = mole fraction × P(total) | |
| Heat | q = m × c × ΔT; q = n × ΔH(fus or vap) at a plateau | |
| Molarity | M = mol ÷ L; M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ for dilution | |
| Freezing-point depression | ΔT = K_f × m × i | m is molality, i the number of particles |
| pH | pH = −log[H⁺]; pOH = −log[OH⁻]; pH + pOH = 14; [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ | |
| Titration | mol acid × (ratio) = mol base; M(acid) × V(acid) = M(base) × V(base) for 1:1 | |
| Enthalpy | ΔH°rxn = Σ n ΔH°f(products) − Σ n ΔH°f(reactants); ΔH ≈ Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed) | |
| Rate | Δ(amount) ÷ Δt; Ea(reverse) = Ea(forward) − ΔH | |
| Equilibrium | Kc = [products]^coefficients ÷ [reactants]^coefficients, solids and liquids omitted | |
| Cell voltage | E°cell = E°(cathode) − E°(anode) | Never scaled by coefficients |
| Faraday | Q = I × t; mol e⁻ = Q ÷ 96,485; mol metal = mol e⁻ ÷ (charge on ion) | Time in seconds |
| Nuclear energy | E = mc²; 1 u = 931.5 MeV | |
| Hydrocarbons | CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ alkanes; CₙH₂ₙ alkenes; CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ alkynes | |

## Constants and data given on the exam

Unless an item states otherwise, use: molar masses H 1.008, C 12.01, N 14.01, O 16.00, Na 22.99, Mg 24.31, Al 26.98, S 32.06, Cl 35.45, K 39.10, Ca 40.08, Fe 55.85, Cu 63.55, Zn 65.38, Ag 107.87 g/mol; R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K); water c = 4.184 J/(g·°C), K_f = 1.86 °C/m; F = 96,485 C/mol; Avogadro's number 6.022 × 10²³; Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C. Standard reduction potentials and formation enthalpies are supplied in the item that needs them. You will not be asked to recall any numerical value that is not in this list or in the item.

## How to show work on calculations

The numeric items are graded on the final number, but every short and long answer that involves a calculation is graded on the work, and a correct method with one slip still earns most of the points. Four habits:

1. **Write the equation first**, in symbols, before substituting: q = mcΔT, then q = (50.0 g)(4.184 J/(g·°C))(6.8 °C). A grader can award method marks only for what is on the page.
2. **Carry units through every step** and cancel them. If the units of the answer come out wrong, the setup is wrong; this catches more errors than any other check.
3. **Convert before you compute.** Celsius to kelvin, millilitres to litres, minutes to seconds, grams to moles. The conversions are where exams are lost.
4. **Round once, at the end**, to the significant figures of the least precise measurement, and state the unit. Keep at least one extra digit in intermediate values.

For a stoichiometry chain, write it as one line of factors: 57.1 g C₈H₁₈ × (1 mol ÷ 114.22 g) × (16 mol CO₂ ÷ 2 mol C₈H₁₈) × (44.01 g ÷ 1 mol) = 176 g CO₂. For a limiting-reactant problem, compute the product from each reactant separately and take the smaller; name the limiting reactant explicitly.

For written explanations, answer each lettered part in order, label it, and use the course's vocabulary: "endothermic, ΔH positive," "London dispersion forces," "shifts toward fewer moles of gas," "the oxidising agent is reduced." A sentence that uses the right term correctly usually earns the point; a paragraph that circles it usually does not.

## A two-week review plan

**Week 33, days 1–3: the arithmetic backbone.** Re-read the unit 4 night-before sheet and rework its two worked examples without looking. Then do every numeric item from the unit 4, 5 and 6 exams again, timed: 90 seconds each. Anything you miss, find the step in the guide and rework it the next morning.

**Days 4–5: structure and naming.** Units 2 and 3. Write the electron configurations of the first 36 elements from the periodic table alone. Name twenty ionic and twenty molecular compounds from formulas, and write formulas from names, including polyatomic ions. Draw Lewis structures, give the VSEPR shape and polarity, and name the strongest intermolecular force for H₂O, CO₂, NH₃, CH₄, HCl, BF₃ and CCl₄.

**Days 6–7: energy, rates, equilibrium.** Unit 7. Redo the calorimetry worked example and the three ΔH methods. Explain each rate factor in one collision-theory sentence. Write Kc for five equations and predict every Le Châtelier shift for the Haber process.

**Week 34, days 1–2: electrochemistry, nuclear, organic.** Unit 8. Assign oxidation numbers to ten species, balance two half-reactions in acid, compute E°cell for three metal pairs from the table, run two Faraday calculations, balance two fission equations, and write the formulas of the first ten alkanes and the five functional groups.

**Day 3: units 1 and 5.** Density, significant figures, dimensional analysis; the gas laws with kelvin; a heating curve and a phase diagram described in words.

**Day 4: the practice final, timed, in one sitting.** Two hours, no notes except the constants above. Then mark it and sort every miss into one of three bins: did not know (reread that topic's guide section), knew but slipped (practise that calculation type), misread the question (slow down on the stem and underline what is asked).

**Day 5: the eight night-before sheets**, one after another, reading only the "ideas that generate every question" and "common errors" sections. That is the last thing to read before the exam.

## The errors that cost the most points, course-wide

- **Celsius in a gas law.** Every T in PV = nRT and the combined law is kelvin.
- **Mass instead of moles in a ratio.** Mole ratios come from coefficients; convert mass to moles first.
- **Wrong sign of ΔH or of q.** Water warming means the reaction released heat: q(reaction) is negative.
- **pH arithmetic.** pH 3 is ten times more acidic than pH 4; a strong acid's [H⁺] equals its molarity only if it is monoprotic.
- **Forgetting the charge check in redox** and the coefficient-free rule for E°cell.
- **Denominators in Kc.** Products on top, solids and liquids omitted, coefficients as exponents.
- **Rounding too early**, or reporting more significant figures than the data allow.
- **Not answering the question asked**: a prompt that says "explain" wants a reason; one that says "calculate" wants a number with a unit.

## The night before

Sleep. Read the eight checklists. Pack a calculator you know how to use, and a pencil. The exam gives you the constants; it does not give you time to relearn the mole. If you have followed this plan, everything on the paper is something you have done at least three times.
