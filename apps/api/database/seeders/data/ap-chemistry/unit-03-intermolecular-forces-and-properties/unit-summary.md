## The ideas that generate every question

1. **Every physical property of a substance is a statement about the forces between its particles.** Dispersion forces exist in everything and grow with the size and polarisability of the electron cloud; dipole–dipole forces need a permanent dipole; hydrogen bonds need H on N, O or F plus a lone pair on N, O or F; ion–dipole forces hold ions in water. Stronger forces mean higher boiling point, lower vapour pressure, higher viscosity and surface tension. Boiling never breaks covalent bonds.
2. **Solids are classified by their particles and the forces between them.** Ionic (ions, Coulombic; hard, brittle, conduct only molten or dissolved), metallic (cations in an electron sea; conduct, malleable), covalent network (atoms bonded throughout; very hard, very high melting, SiO₂, diamond, graphite), molecular (molecules held by IMFs; soft, low melting, non-conducting). The phase is the balance of kinetic energy against attraction: gases are compressible because they are mostly empty space.
3. **PV = nRT, with T in kelvin.** R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K). Rearrangements: n = PV/RT; M = dRT/P; P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂ for a fixed sample. Dalton: P_total = ΣP_i and P_i = X_i P_total; a gas collected over water carries water vapour, so subtract the vapour pressure.
4. **Kinetic molecular theory explains the law.** Point particles in random motion, elastic collisions, no attractions; average kinetic energy depends on temperature alone, so at the same T all gases have the same average KE and the lighter one moves faster: v_rms = √(3RT/M) with M in kg/mol and R = 8.314 J/(mol·K). The Maxwell–Boltzmann curve broadens and shifts to higher speed with higher T or lower M.
5. **Real gases deviate when the assumptions fail.** High pressure makes particle volume matter (measured volume larger than ideal); low temperature lets attractions matter (measured pressure or volume smaller than ideal). Big, polar molecules deviate most; He and H₂ deviate least.
6. **Molarity is moles per litre of solution.** Dilution conserves moles: M₁V₁ = M₂V₂. Mixing: add moles, divide by total volume. Strong electrolytes dissociate completely, so each ion's concentration is its subscript times the compound's. Particulate diagrams show separate hydrated ions in the formula ratio, water oxygens toward cations and hydrogens toward anions; molecular solutes stay intact.
7. **"Like dissolves like" is an energy balance.** A solute dissolves when solute–solvent attractions can replace the solute–solute and solvent–solvent attractions lost. Ionic and hydrogen-bonding solutes dissolve in water; nonpolar solutes in nonpolar solvents; a long hydrocarbon chain overwhelms a single O–H.
8. **Separations exploit differences in IMFs.** Chromatography: components attracted more strongly to the stationary phase lag; R_f = distance of spot ÷ distance of solvent front, between 0 and 1. Distillation: the liquid with weaker IMFs (higher vapour pressure, lower boiling point) distils first.
9. **Light is photons of energy E = hν = hc/λ.** Microwave → rotation; infrared → vibration; visible and ultraviolet → electronic transitions. The photoelectric effect proves it: no electrons below a threshold frequency regardless of intensity; KE_max = hν − φ; intensity sets the number of electrons, not their energy.
10. **A = εbc.** Absorbance (−log T) is proportional to concentration and path length; measure at the wavelength of maximum absorbance; build a calibration curve from standards, read the unknown off it, and dilute by a known factor if the absorbance is above the range.

## Ranking boiling points: the order of operations

1. Any ions? Ionic compounds boil highest of all (unit 2).
2. Hydrogen bonding (H–N, H–O, H–F)? It beats dipole–dipole and usually beats dispersion for molecules of similar size (H₂O boils 160 K above H₂S).
3. Polar? Dipole–dipole adds to dispersion for similar sizes (CH₃Cl above C₂H₆).
4. Otherwise, or when sizes differ a lot, dispersion decides: more electrons and more surface area mean a higher boiling point (I₂ is a solid; HCl is a gas). Branched isomers boil lower than straight chains because they pack less closely.

## Common errors

- Saying boiling or melting breaks covalent bonds. It breaks intermolecular attractions.
- Calling any molecule with hydrogen a hydrogen bonder; the H must be on N, O or F.
- Forgetting that dispersion forces are present in polar molecules too, and often dominate.
- Using °C in a gas law, or forgetting to convert torr to atm or mL to L.
- Treating a gas collected over water as pure; subtract the vapour pressure.
- Saying heavier gas molecules have less kinetic energy at the same temperature; they have the same average KE and lower speed.
- Saying real gases deviate at "high temperature and low pressure"; it is the reverse.
- Dividing by the volume of water instead of the volume of the solution; averaging molarities when mixing.
- Drawing intact "NaCl molecules" in water, or the same water orientation around both ions.
- Saying oil and water repel; water simply will not give up its hydrogen bonds for weak dispersion forces.
- Claiming a brighter light ejects faster electrons; intensity sets the number, frequency sets the energy.
- Treating transmittance as proportional to concentration, or extrapolating a calibration curve above its top standard.

## Worked example: a gas-and-solution FRQ across the unit

A student reacts 0.300 g of magnesium with excess hydrochloric acid and collects the hydrogen over water at 25 °C (vapour pressure of water 23.8 torr) and a barometric pressure of 762 torr, then uses the acid left over to make a solution.

(a) Moles of H₂: 0.300 g ÷ 24.31 g/mol = 0.01234 mol Mg → 0.01234 mol H₂ (Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂).
(b) Partial pressure of H₂: 762 − 23.8 = 738 torr = 0.971 atm.
(c) Volume collected: V = nRT/P = (0.01234)(0.08206)(298) ÷ 0.971 = 0.311 L = 311 mL.
(d) The H₂ molecules have the same average kinetic energy as the water-vapour molecules in the flask but, being nine times lighter, move three times faster (v ∝ 1/√M).
(e) Hydrogen behaves almost ideally here: low pressure, room temperature, tiny nonpolar molecules with negligible attractions and negligible volume.
(f) If 25.0 mL of the 2.00 M HCl is diluted to 250.0 mL, the new concentration is (2.00)(25.0)/250.0 = 0.200 M, with [H⁺] = [Cl⁻] = 0.200 M; a particulate diagram shows equal numbers of separately hydrated H⁺ (as H₃O⁺) and Cl⁻ ions, with water hydrogens pointing at Cl⁻.

## What to review from earlier units

## How the exam will ask it

Expect: ranking boiling points with justification; identifying the IMF in a particulate drawing; a gas-law calculation with a collection-over-water twist; a Maxwell–Boltzmann graph to label or compare; a real-gas question asking which assumption fails; a molarity or dilution calculation feeding a stoichiometry problem; a particulate diagram of a solution to draw or criticise; a chromatography strip with R_f values to interpret; a solubility prediction with an IMF argument; a photon energy calculation and the region of the spectrum; a photoelectric-effect question about intensity versus frequency; and a Beer's-law calibration curve to read. In every justification name the particles, the force, and the consequence, in that order.
