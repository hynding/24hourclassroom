## The ideas that generate every question

1. **The mole is a count you can weigh.** n = m/M converts grams to moles; moles × 6.022 × 10²³ converts to particles. Every quantitative problem in the course starts here, and every "which sample has more atoms" question is a conversion to a common unit.
2. **Position is a property, height is a count.** A mass spectrum sorts atoms by mass (peak = isotope, height = abundance); a photoelectron spectrum sorts electrons by binding energy (peak = subshell, height = electrons). Weighted averages of the first give atomic mass; sums of the second give the atomic number.
3. **Fixed formula, fixed composition.** A pure compound has a definite mass percent of each element (law of definite proportions), which lets you go from formula to percent and from percent back to the empirical formula. A mixture's composition varies and must be measured, usually gravimetrically.
4. **Coulomb's law runs the atom.** Attraction grows with charge and shrinks with distance. Add shielding and you have effective nuclear charge, which explains subshell energies, PES peak positions, every periodic trend and the melting points of ionic solids.
5. **Valence electrons decide chemistry.** Configuration (Aufbau, Pauli, Hund) gives the valence count; the valence count gives the ion's charge; the charges give the formula of the ionic compound; the charges and sizes give its lattice energy.

## Key terms and formulas

| Item | What to remember |
|---|---|
| n = m/M | moles = grams ÷ molar mass (g/mol); molar mass = sum of atomic masses × subscripts |
| N = n × N_A | particles = moles × 6.022 × 10²³; divide to go back |
| Average atomic mass | Σ (fractional abundance × isotope mass); fractions, not percentages |
| Mass percent | (atoms × atomic mass ÷ molar mass) × 100; percentages sum to 100 |
| Empirical formula | % → g (assume 100 g) → mol → divide by smallest → clear fractions |
| Molecular formula | multiplier = molar mass ÷ empirical formula mass |
| Combustion analysis | mol C = mol CO₂; mol H = 2 × mol H₂O; O by subtraction |
| Hydrate | water lost ÷ 18.02 over residue ÷ M(salt) = x; % water = loss ÷ sample × 100 |
| Filling order | 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p; Cr [Ar] 4s¹ 3d⁵; Cu [Ar] 4s¹ 3d¹⁰; cations lose 4s first |
| Z_eff | ≈ protons − core electrons; rises across a period, roughly constant down a group |
| PES | binding energy (MJ/mol) on x; one peak per subshell; height ∝ electrons; 1s always highest |
| MJ/mol → J/electron | × 10⁶ ÷ 6.022 × 10²³ |
| Trends across a period | radius ↓, IE ↑ (dips at Al and S), electronegativity ↑ |
| Trends down a group | radius ↑, IE ↓, electronegativity ↓ |
| Successive IE | always rising; big jump after the last valence electron |
| Isoelectronic radii | more protons, smaller ion: Mg²⁺ < Na⁺ < F⁻ < O²⁻ |
| Ion charges | group 1 → 1+, 2 → 2+, Al 3+, N 3−, O 2−, halogens 1− |
| Lattice energy | ∝ q₁q₂ / r: charge product first, then ion size |

## Common errors

- Multiplying by molar mass to get moles; counting moles of molecules as moles of atoms; using percentages instead of fractions in a weighted average (answer 100 times too large).
- Averaging isotope masses without weighting; putting a peak at the average atomic mass.
- Rounding a mole ratio of 1.5 or 1.33 to a whole number instead of multiplying through; forgetting that each H₂O carries two H in combustion analysis; using the hydrate's mass instead of the residue's for moles of salt.
- Removing 3d before 4s from a transition-metal cation; pairing p electrons before each orbital is singly occupied; forgetting Cr and Cu.
- Reading PES height as energy or position as count; calling the tallest peak the valence shell.
- Saying atoms grow across a period "because they have more electrons"; explaining the group trend without the added shell; treating the Al and S dips as mistakes.
- Drawing a cation larger than its atom; writing Ca(NO₃)₂ without parentheses; ranking melting points by size when charges differ.

## Worked example: an FRQ that crosses the unit

*A 1.277 g sample of a hydrated salt, MCl₂·2H₂O, is heated to constant mass, leaving 0.964 g of anhydrous MCl₂. The metal M has a photoelectron spectrum with peaks of relative heights 2, 2, 6, 2, 6, 2 (from highest to lowest binding energy). (a) Identify M. (b) Confirm the formula of the hydrate from the mass data. (c) Explain why M forms a 2+ ion. (d) Predict whether MCl₂ or NaCl has the higher melting point.*

(a) Heights sum to 20: Z = 20, calcium, 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s². The lowest-energy peak of height 2 is the 4s pair.

(b) Water lost = 1.277 − 0.964 = 0.313 g = 0.313 ÷ 18.02 = 0.01737 mol. CaCl₂ = 0.964 ÷ 110.98 = 0.008686 mol. Ratio 0.01737 ÷ 0.008686 = 2.00. The formula CaCl₂·2H₂O is confirmed.

(c) Calcium's two 4s valence electrons are weakly held (IE₁ 590, IE₂ 1145 kJ/mol) but the third ionization (4912 kJ/mol) breaks into the argon core. Losing exactly two gives the noble-gas configuration, so Ca²⁺ forms and no further.

(d) CaCl₂. Both contain Cl⁻, but Ca²⁺ carries twice the charge of Na⁺ and is similar in size (100 pm against 102 pm), so the Coulombic attraction per ion pair is about twice as strong; CaCl₂ melts at 772 °C against NaCl's 801 °C in practice, which shows that the simple charge argument is complicated by lattice geometry (CaCl₂ has a different structure with a 1:2 ion ratio). On the exam, argue from charge and size and note that the comparison is strongest when the structures are alike.

The point of the example: an FRQ moves from spectrum (1.6) to configuration (1.5) to stoichiometry (1.1, 1.4) to trends (1.7) to ionic bonding (1.8) in one question. Keep each step's units and reasoning explicit.

## What to review from earlier units

This is the first unit, so there is nothing earlier, but re-read week 1 before the exam: n = m/M and the weighted average are used in at least a third of the exam's items, including ones filed under later topics.

## How the exam will ask it

Expect a mass spectrum or PES to read (identify the element, justify a configuration, compare two elements), one empirical-formula or hydrate calculation with data, one set of ionization-energy or radius data to interpret, and one explanation of an ionic compound's formula or melting point. Every explanation question wants Coulomb's law named and applied to both charge and distance.
