## The ideas that generate every question

1. **Bond type follows from the atoms.** Metal + nonmetal with a large electronegativity difference: electrons transfer, ionic lattice. Nonmetal + nonmetal: electrons shared, covalent, polar if the electronegativities differ (δ− on the more electronegative atom). Metal + metal: delocalised electron sea. It is a continuum guided by ΔEN, not three boxes.
2. **A bond is the bottom of a potential-energy well.** The distance at the minimum is the bond length, the depth is the bond energy. Higher bond order and smaller atoms give shorter, stronger bonds. Breaking a bond always costs energy; forming one always releases it.
3. **Structure explains properties.** Ionic lattice: high melting point, brittle (like charges meet when a layer shifts), conducts only when molten or dissolved. Metal: conducts, malleable (identical cations, mobile electrons); alloys are harder because foreign atoms (substitutional or interstitial) pin the slip planes.
4. **The Lewis diagram is the starting model.** Count valence electrons (adjust for charge), bond, complete octets, make multiple bonds if the centre is short, bracket ions. Know the exceptions: B and Be short of eight, odd-electron radicals, expanded octets from period 3 down.
5. **Resonance averages; formal charge ranks.** Equivalent structures give equal, intermediate bonds with fractional order. Non-equivalent structures are weighted by formal charge: fewest nonzero charges, negative charge on the most electronegative atom.
6. **Domains decide shape, shape decides polarity.** VSEPR: count domains (a multiple bond is one), place lone pairs where they fit, read the molecular geometry and angle. A symmetric shape with identical terminal atoms is nonpolar; lone pairs on the centre or mixed terminal atoms usually make it polar. Hybridization is just the domain count: 2 sp, 3 sp², 4 sp³.

## Key terms and formulas

| Item | What to remember |
|---|---|
| ΔEN guide | < 0.4 nonpolar; 0.4–1.7 polar covalent; > 1.7 ionic; but metal + nonmetal is the better test (HF is covalent) |
| Potential-energy curve | x of minimum = bond length; depth = bond energy (positive); steep left wall = nuclear repulsion |
| Bond order rule | order ↑ → length ↓, energy ↑ (C–C 154 pm/348; C=C 134/614; C≡C 120/839 kJ/mol) |
| Size rule | larger atoms → longer, weaker bonds (H–F 92 pm/567 to H–I 161/299) |
| Lattice energy | ∝ q₁q₂/r: MgO ≫ NaF; NaF > NaCl > NaBr |
| Ionic properties | high mp; brittle; non-conducting solid, conducting melt/solution; formula = ion ratio |
| Metal properties | conductive, malleable, ductile, lustrous; electron sea |
| Alloys | substitutional (similar radii: brass Cu/Zn) vs interstitial (small atoms in gaps: steel Fe/C); both harder than the pure metal |
| Valence electron count | Σ group numbers + (negative charge) − (positive charge) |
| Formal charge | valence − lone-pair electrons − ½ bonding electrons; sums to the species charge |
| Bond order (resonance) | total bonds ÷ positions: NO₃⁻ 1.33; NO₂⁻ 1.5; benzene 1.5 |
| VSEPR angles | 2 domains 180°; 3 → 120°; 4 → 109.5° (107° NH₃, 104.5° H₂O); 5 → 90°/120°; 6 → 90° |
| Lone pairs | repel more; take equatorial sites in trigonal bipyramid; opposite sites in octahedron |
| Hybridization | sp (2 domains), sp² (3), sp³ (4); π bonds use leftover p orbitals |
| σ/π count | single 1σ; double 1σ + 1π; triple 1σ + 2π |

## Common errors

- Classifying HF as ionic from ΔEN alone; forgetting the metal/nonmetal test.
- Reporting bond energy as a negative number; saying bonds release energy when broken.
- Treating NaCl as molecules; explaining brittleness as "weak bonds" instead of like-charge repulsion.
- Claiming solid ionic compounds conduct because they contain ions.
- Miscounting electrons for ions; giving hydrogen an octet; leaving a central carbon with six electrons when a double bond would fix it; expanding a period-2 octet.
- Moving atoms between resonance structures; imagining the molecule flips between them.
- Counting a double bond as two domains; giving the electron-domain geometry instead of the molecular geometry.
- Calling a molecule nonpolar because its bonds "balance" without checking lone pairs or terminal-atom identity.
- Assigning hybridization from bonded atoms rather than domains (NH₃ is sp³).

## Worked example: a bonding FRQ across the unit

*The thiocyanate ion, SCN⁻, has carbon as its central atom. (a) Draw the Lewis structure that places the −1 formal charge on nitrogen and the one that places it on sulfur. (b) Use formal charge to decide which contributes more. (c) Give the ion's molecular geometry and the hybridization of carbon. (d) Predict whether the C–N bond in SCN⁻ is shorter or longer than the C–N bond in CH₃NH₂ and justify.*

(a) 16 electrons. Structure I: S=C=N with two lone pairs on S and two on N. Structure II: S–C≡N with three lone pairs on S and one on N. (A third, S≡C–N, has S +1 and N −2.)

(b) Structure I: S 0, C 0, N −1. Structure II: S −1, C 0, N 0. Both carry one −1; nitrogen (3.04) is more electronegative than sulfur (2.58), so structure I is the major contributor, with II significant.

(c) Carbon has two bonding domains in every structure (no lone pairs): linear, 180°, sp hybridised. The C≡N or C=N π bonds use carbon's two unhybridised p orbitals.

(d) Shorter. In SCN⁻ the C–N bond order is between 2 and 3 (an average of I and II); in methylamine C–N is a single bond. Higher bond order, shorter bond (about 117 pm against 147 pm).

## What to review from earlier units

Unit 1: electronegativity and the periodic trends (deciding bond type and the better resonance structure), Coulomb's law (lattice energy, bond strength), valence-electron counts from configurations (Lewis diagrams) and the mole (scaling bond energies to a sample).

## How the exam will ask it

A Lewis-structure FRQ (draw, count, choose by formal charge, give geometry, angle, hybridization and polarity) appears almost every year. Expect a potential-energy diagram to read, a property table to match to bonding types, a comparison of alloys from radii, and resonance bond-length evidence to interpret. Every explanation wants the particulate model named and the chain of reasoning written out.
