## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Titration | Measuring the volume of a solution of known concentration needed to react completely with a measured amount of another substance. |
| Titrant / analyte | The solution in the burette, of known concentration / the solution being analysed, in the flask. |
| Standard solution | A solution whose concentration is accurately known, made from a pure solid or standardised against one. |
| Primary standard | A pure, stable, weighable solid (potassium hydrogen phthalate, KHP) used to standardise a titrant such as NaOH. |
| Equivalence point | The point at which the moles of titrant added exactly match the stoichiometric requirement of the analyte. |
| End point | The point at which the indicator changes colour; chosen to lie as close to the equivalence point as possible. |
| Indicator | A substance that changes colour over a narrow pH range (phenolphthalein: colourless in acid, pink in base). |
| Acid–base reaction | Transfer of a proton (H⁺) from an acid to a base. |
| Oxidation–reduction (redox) reaction | Transfer of electrons: one species is oxidised (loses electrons), another is reduced (gains them). |
| Precipitation reaction | Ions in solution combine to form an insoluble solid. |

## Titration (4.6)

A **titration** measures an unknown amount of a substance by reacting it completely with a solution of known concentration. The standard solution, the **titrant**, is delivered from a burette into a measured volume of the **analyte** in a flask. The reaction's balanced equation then converts the titrant's moles into the analyte's moles; from there, concentration, mass or purity follows exactly as in week 14.

**The equipment and what each piece does.** A **volumetric pipette** delivers a precise volume of analyte (25.00 mL) into the flask. The **burette** measures the titrant volume to ±0.05 mL from an initial and a final reading. A few drops of **indicator** signal the end. The flask may be wet with distilled water (it changes neither the moles of analyte nor the moles of titrant), but the pipette and burette must be rinsed with the solutions they will hold, because water left in them dilutes those solutions.

**Equivalence point versus end point.** The **equivalence point** is the chemical fact: moles of titrant = moles required by the equation. The **end point** is what you see: the indicator's colour change. A good indicator changes colour at a pH very close to the equivalence point, so the two volumes nearly coincide; phenolphthalein (colourless → faint pink) suits a strong-acid–strong-base titration. The end point is taken at the first *permanent* faint colour; a deep colour means overshoot.

**Worked example 1: a strong acid.** 25.00 mL of hydrochloric acid of unknown concentration is titrated with 0.100 M NaOH; 21.50 mL is needed to reach the phenolphthalein end point. n(NaOH) = 0.02150 L × 0.100 M = 0.00215 mol. HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O is 1 : 1, so n(HCl) = 0.00215 mol, and [HCl] = 0.00215 ÷ 0.02500 L = 0.0860 M.

**Worked example 2: a diprotic acid.** 20.00 mL of sulfuric acid needs 30.00 mL of 0.150 M NaOH. n(NaOH) = 0.00450 mol. H₂SO₄ + 2 NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2 H₂O, so n(H₂SO₄) = 0.00450 ÷ 2 = 0.00225 mol and [H₂SO₄] = 0.00225 ÷ 0.02000 = 0.113 M. The 2 : 1 ratio is the step most often missed.

**Worked example 3: standardising the titrant.** Sodium hydroxide absorbs water and CO₂ from the air, so a solution made by weighing NaOH is never exactly the intended concentration. It is **standardised** against a primary standard, usually potassium hydrogen phthalate (KHP, KHC₈H₄O₄, 204.22 g/mol, one acidic proton). A 0.5105 g sample of KHP needs 24.80 mL of the NaOH solution: n(KHP) = 0.5105 ÷ 204.22 = 0.002500 mol = n(NaOH); [NaOH] = 0.002500 ÷ 0.02480 = 0.1008 M. This standardised value is then used in every later titration.

**Worked example 4: an analysis for a real sample.** Vinegar is acetic acid in water. 10.00 mL of vinegar needs 14.20 mL of 0.100 M NaOH: n(CH₃COOH) = 0.001420 mol, so the vinegar is 0.142 M. Mass of acid = 0.001420 × 60.05 = 0.0853 g in 10.00 mL; if the vinegar's density is 1.00 g/mL, that is 0.853 % acetic acid by mass. (Acetic acid is weak, but the titration still consumes one OH⁻ per molecule: at the equivalence point all of it has reacted.)

**Error analysis.** The exam asks how a mistake changes the computed concentration. Reason through moles: (a) **Overshooting the end point** records too much titrant, so the analyte's moles and concentration come out too high. (b) **An air bubble in the burette tip** that disappears during the titration counts as delivered volume: titrant volume reads too high → concentration too high. (c) **Rinsing the burette with water** instead of titrant dilutes the titrant, so more volume is needed → concentration too high. (d) **A wet flask** adds water to the analyte but changes neither its moles nor the titrant required: no effect. (e) **Rinsing the pipette with water** leaves the analyte diluted before it is measured → fewer moles delivered → concentration too low. (f) **Reading the burette from above** (parallax) or misreading the meniscus can go either way, which is why readings are taken at eye level.

**Try it.** 25.00 mL of a Ba(OH)₂ solution needs 18.60 mL of 0.0500 M HCl. Find [Ba(OH)₂]. *(n(HCl) = 9.30 × 10⁻⁴ mol; Ba(OH)₂ + 2 HCl → BaCl₂ + 2 H₂O, so n(Ba(OH)₂) = 4.65 × 10⁻⁴ mol; ÷ 0.02500 L = 0.0186 M.)*

## Types of chemical reactions (4.7)

AP Chemistry sorts reactions by **what is transferred**:

1. **Acid–base reactions** transfer a **proton**. HCl + NH₃ → NH₄Cl; H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O; CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O. Nothing changes oxidation state.
2. **Oxidation–reduction (redox) reactions** transfer **electrons**: one species loses electrons (is oxidised) and another gains them (is reduced). Combustion, single replacement, synthesis of a compound from its elements, and many decompositions are redox; the test is that some element changes oxidation state (week 16 does this properly). Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu: zinc goes from 0 to +2, copper from +2 to 0.
3. **Precipitation reactions** combine ions in solution into an insoluble solid, with no change in oxidation state and no proton transfer: Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl(s).

These overlap with the older pattern names, which are still useful for predicting products:

- **Synthesis (combination):** A + B → AB. 2 Mg + O₂ → 2 MgO; N₂ + 3 H₂ → 2 NH₃. Usually redox when the reactants are elements.
- **Decomposition:** AB → A + B. 2 KClO₃ → 2 KCl + 3 O₂ (redox); CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (not redox: every oxidation state is unchanged).
- **Combustion:** a substance reacts with O₂, releasing heat; hydrocarbons give CO₂ and H₂O. Always redox.
- **Single replacement:** an element displaces another from a compound. Zn + 2 HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂; Cl₂ + 2 KBr → 2 KCl + Br₂. Always redox. Whether it happens depends on the **activity series**: a more reactive metal (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe …) displaces a less reactive one (… Pb, H, Cu, Ag, Au); a more reactive halogen (F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂) displaces a less reactive one.
- **Double replacement (metathesis):** ions swap partners, AB + CD → AD + CB. It "goes" only if a precipitate, a gas or water forms; otherwise there is no reaction. Precipitation, strong acid–strong base neutralisation and carbonate-plus-acid are all double replacements. Never redox.

**Worked example 5.** Classify and predict the products: (a) aluminium metal in copper(II) chloride solution; (b) aqueous nitric acid with aqueous potassium hydroxide; (c) aqueous lead(II) nitrate with aqueous sodium sulfate; (d) butane burning; (e) heating magnesium carbonate. *(a) Single replacement, redox: 2 Al + 3 CuCl₂ → 2 AlCl₃ + 3 Cu. (b) Double replacement, acid–base: HNO₃ + KOH → KNO₃ + H₂O; net H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O. (c) Double replacement, precipitation: Pb²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → PbSO₄(s). (d) Combustion, redox: 2 C₄H₁₀ + 13 O₂ → 8 CO₂ + 10 H₂O. (e) Decomposition, not redox: MgCO₃ → MgO + CO₂.*

**Worked example 6: deciding whether a reaction occurs.** Copper wire placed in zinc sulfate solution: copper is below zinc in the activity series, so it cannot displace zinc; no reaction. Silver nitrate solution on copper wire: copper is above silver, so Cu + 2 Ag⁺ → Cu²⁺ + 2 Ag, and the solution turns blue while silver crystals grow.

**Try it.** Classify by transfer type: (i) 2 H₂O₂ → 2 H₂O + O₂; (ii) Ba(OH)₂ + 2 HCl → BaCl₂ + 2 H₂O; (iii) Na₂S + Cu(NO₃)₂ → CuS(s) + 2 NaNO₃. *(i) Redox (O goes from −1 to −2 and 0); decomposition. (ii) Acid–base; double replacement. (iii) Precipitation; double replacement.*

## Common mistakes

- Using the titrant volume as if it were the analyte volume, or forgetting to convert mL to L.
- Ignoring a 2 : 1 ratio for diprotic acids or dibasic bases.
- Saying a wet flask makes the concentration too low; it has no effect on moles.
- Confusing end point (observed) with equivalence point (stoichiometric).
- Treating a titration of a weak acid as if it consumed less base; at the equivalence point every acid molecule has reacted.
- Calling a precipitation reaction redox because "something changed"; check oxidation states.
- Predicting a single replacement that the activity series forbids (Cu in ZnSO₄; Ag in HCl).

## Self-check

1. 20.00 mL of a nitric acid solution needs 17.40 mL of 0.125 M KOH. Find [HNO₃].
2. A student's burette had an air bubble below the stopcock that disappeared during the titration. How does this affect the calculated concentration of the analyte, and why?
3. 0.4210 g of KHP (204.22 g/mol) needs 20.50 mL of NaOH. Find [NaOH].
4. Classify each reaction by what is transferred and by pattern: Mg + 2 HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂; HF + NaOH → NaF + H₂O; 2 Na + Cl₂ → 2 NaCl.
5. Will iron displace copper from CuSO₄(aq)? Will copper displace iron from FeSO₄(aq)? Explain.

*Answers:* (1) n(KOH) = 0.002175 mol = n(HNO₃); ÷ 0.02000 = 0.109 M. (2) Too high: the bubble's volume is counted as titrant delivered, so the titrant volume and hence the analyte's moles are overestimated. (3) 0.4210 ÷ 204.22 = 0.002062 mol; ÷ 0.02050 = 0.1006 M. (4) Redox, single replacement; acid–base, double replacement; redox, synthesis. (5) Yes: iron is above copper in the activity series. No: copper is below iron and cannot give electrons to Fe²⁺.
