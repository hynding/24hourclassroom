## What you need to be able to do

- **3.1** Identify the intermolecular forces present in a substance (London dispersion, dipole–dipole, hydrogen bonding, ion–dipole), explain each as a Coulombic attraction, and rank substances by the strength of their forces to predict boiling points, vapour pressures and viscosities.
- **3.1** Explain that dispersion forces exist in every substance and grow with the size and polarisability of the electron cloud (roughly, with molar mass and surface area).
- **3.2** Classify a solid as ionic, metallic, covalent network or molecular from its properties, and explain the properties from the particles and the forces between them.
- **3.3** Describe solids, liquids and gases at the particulate level in terms of particle spacing, motion and the balance between kinetic energy and intermolecular attractions; explain why gases are compressible and liquids and solids are not.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Intermolecular force (IMF) | An attraction between molecules (or between ions and molecules); much weaker than the covalent bonds within a molecule. |
| London dispersion force | Attraction from temporary dipoles created by momentary unevenness of electron clouds; present in all substances; stronger for larger, more polarisable molecules. |
| Polarisability | How easily an electron cloud is distorted; increases with the number of electrons and the size of the atom. |
| Dipole–dipole force | Attraction between the δ+ end of one polar molecule and the δ− end of another. |
| Hydrogen bond | An especially strong dipole–dipole attraction between an H bonded to N, O or F and a lone pair on N, O or F of another molecule. |
| Ion–dipole force | Attraction between an ion and the oppositely charged end of a polar molecule; how water dissolves salts. |
| Vapour pressure | The pressure of a vapour in equilibrium with its liquid; higher when IMFs are weaker. |
| Covalent network solid | Atoms joined by covalent bonds in a continuous lattice (diamond, graphite, SiO₂, SiC); very hard, very high melting. |
| Molecular solid | Discrete molecules held by IMFs (ice, sucrose, I₂, dry ice); soft, low melting, non-conducting. |
| Kinetic energy vs attraction | Gas: KE dominates; liquid: comparable; solid: attractions dominate and particles only vibrate. |

## The four intermolecular forces (3.1)

Every intermolecular force is Coulomb's law between partial or full charges. The question is where the charges come from.

**London dispersion.** At any instant the electrons of a molecule are unevenly distributed, giving a momentary dipole that induces a dipole in a neighbour; the two attract. Every substance has dispersion forces, including noble gases and nonpolar molecules, which have nothing else. The force grows with the number of electrons (polarisability): He boils at 4 K, Ne 27 K, Ar 87 K, Kr 120 K, Xe 165 K. Shape matters too: long molecules touch along more surface than compact ones, so n-pentane (309 K) boils above its branched isomer neopentane (283 K).

**Dipole–dipole.** Polar molecules line up δ+ to δ−. For molecules of similar size, polar ones boil higher: CH₃CHO (acetaldehyde, polar, 293 K) above C₃H₈ (propane, nonpolar, 231 K), both 44 g/mol. The dipole–dipole force adds to the dispersion force; it does not replace it.

**Hydrogen bonding.** When hydrogen is bonded to N, O or F, the bond is so polar and the hydrogen so small and exposed that its δ+ interacts strongly with a lone pair on N, O or F of another molecule. The effect is dramatic: H₂O boils at 373 K while H₂S, with more electrons, boils at 213 K; HF boils at 293 K above HCl at 188 K; NH₃ at 240 K above PH₃ at 185 K. Hydrogen bonding is why ice floats (an open hydrogen-bonded lattice), why water has a high specific heat, and why DNA strands pair.

**Ion–dipole.** An ion attracts the opposite end of a polar molecule. Na⁺ is surrounded by water oxygens, Cl⁻ by water hydrogens; this hydration energy pays for pulling the lattice apart. Ion–dipole is the strongest of the forces between neutral molecules and ions and is the basis of solubility in Unit 3's later weeks.

Approximate strength order for similar-sized species: ion–dipole > hydrogen bonding > dipole–dipole > dispersion. But dispersion grows without limit with size: I₂ (nonpolar, 254 g/mol, boils at 457 K) is held together far more strongly than HCl (polar, 36.5 g/mol, 188 K). Always compare sizes before declaring a polar substance higher-boiling.

**Worked example 1.** Rank CH₄, CH₃OH, CH₃Cl and CH₃CH₂CH₃ by boiling point.

- CH₄ (16 g/mol): dispersion only, tiny: 112 K.
- CH₃CH₂CH₃ (44 g/mol): dispersion only, larger: 231 K.
- CH₃Cl (50.5 g/mol): dispersion plus dipole–dipole: 249 K.
- CH₃OH (32 g/mol): dispersion, dipole–dipole and hydrogen bonding: 338 K.

Order: CH₄ < C₃H₈ < CH₃Cl < CH₃OH. Methanol beats the heavier chloromethane because hydrogen bonding is so strong.

**Worked example 2.** Explain why ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH) boils at 351 K but dimethyl ether (CH₃OCH₃), with the same formula C₂H₆O, boils at 249 K.

Same molar mass, so dispersion forces are similar; both are polar. Ethanol has an O–H bond and can hydrogen bond between molecules; dimethyl ether has no H bonded to O (its hydrogens are on carbon) and cannot, though its oxygen can accept a hydrogen bond from water. The hydrogen bonding in ethanol raises its boiling point by about 100 K.

**Vapour pressure, viscosity, surface tension.** Stronger IMFs mean a lower vapour pressure at a given temperature (fewer molecules escape), a higher viscosity (molecules resist flowing past each other) and a higher surface tension. Water's high values of all three come from hydrogen bonding.

**Try it.**
1. Which has the stronger dispersion forces, F₂ or Br₂? Which boils higher?
2. Which of HF, HCl, HBr and HI boils highest, and why is the order among the other three the reverse of what dipole strength alone predicts?
3. Name every intermolecular force present in a solution of NaCl in water.

*Answers:* (1) Br₂ (70 electrons vs 18); Br₂ boils at 332 K, F₂ at 85 K. (2) HF, because of hydrogen bonding (293 K). Among HCl (188 K), HBr (206 K), HI (238 K) the boiling point rises with size even though the dipole falls, because the growth in dispersion forces outweighs the loss of dipole–dipole attraction. (3) Ion–dipole (Na⁺/Cl⁻ with water), hydrogen bonding and dipole–dipole between water molecules, and dispersion among everything.

## Four kinds of solid (3.2)

| Type | Particles | Held by | Properties | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ionic | cations and anions | Coulombic attraction in a lattice | hard, brittle, high mp, conducts only molten or dissolved | NaCl, MgO, CaCO₃ |
| Metallic | cations in an electron sea | delocalised electrons | conducts, malleable, ductile, variable mp | Cu, Fe, brass |
| Covalent network | atoms | covalent bonds throughout | very hard, very high mp, usually non-conducting | diamond, SiO₂ (quartz), SiC; graphite conducts |
| Molecular | molecules | intermolecular forces | soft, low mp, non-conducting, often volatile | ice, I₂, sucrose, CO₂(s) |

The decisive question for a molecular solid versus a covalent network solid is what must break to melt it. Melting ice breaks hydrogen bonds between H₂O molecules, not O–H bonds, so ice melts at 0 °C. Melting quartz means breaking Si–O covalent bonds, so quartz melts above 1600 °C. Diamond (every carbon sp³-bonded to four others) is the hardest natural substance; graphite (sp² sheets with delocalised π electrons, sheets held by dispersion forces) is soft, slippery and conducts along its sheets. Same element, different solid, different properties: structure explains everything.

**Worked example 3.** A solid melts at 1710 °C, does not conduct in any state, and is insoluble in water. Classify it.

Not metallic (no conduction), not ionic (no conduction when molten), not molecular (melting point far too high). Covalent network, consistent with SiO₂.

## Solids, liquids and gases (3.3)

The state of a substance is a contest between the kinetic energy of its particles (which grows with temperature) and the attractions between them.

- **Solid:** attractions win; particles are packed closely in fixed positions and only vibrate. Definite shape and volume; essentially incompressible.
- **Liquid:** kinetic energy and attractions are comparable; particles stay in contact but slide past one another. Definite volume, takes the shape of its container, incompressible, flows.
- **Gas:** kinetic energy wins; particles are far apart (about ten times their diameter at 1 atm), move freely and collide elastically; attractions are negligible between collisions. No definite shape or volume; **compressible** because most of the volume is empty space.

Heating a solid raises vibration until the lattice breaks (melting); further heating raises kinetic energy until molecules escape the liquid (boiling). Substances with stronger attractions need more kinetic energy, so they melt and boil higher. The particulate diagrams on the exam show this directly: a gas box is mostly empty; a liquid box is full but disordered; a solid box is full and ordered.

Density follows: solids and liquids are hundreds of times denser than gases because the particles touch. Ice is the famous exception to "solid denser than liquid" because hydrogen bonding holds water molecules in an open hexagonal lattice that collapses on melting.

**Try it.**
1. Why can a gas be compressed to a fraction of its volume while a liquid cannot?
2. At the particulate level, what changes and what stays the same when water boils?

*Answers:* (1) Gas particles are separated by large empty spaces that can be reduced; liquid particles are already in contact and their electron clouds repel when pushed closer. (2) The molecules move from being in contact (held by hydrogen bonds) to being far apart and independent; the H₂O molecules themselves, and their covalent O–H bonds, are unchanged.

## Common mistakes

- Saying water boils at 100 °C because O–H bonds break; boiling breaks intermolecular hydrogen bonds, not covalent bonds.
- Calling any H-containing molecule a hydrogen bonder; the H must be on N, O or F.
- Assuming a polar molecule always boils higher than a nonpolar one without comparing sizes (I₂ vs HCl).
- Forgetting that dispersion forces are present in polar molecules too, and dominate for large ones.
- Confusing covalent network solids (bonds throughout) with molecular solids (bonds within, IMFs between).
- Saying gas particles are "lighter" than liquid particles; they are the same particles, farther apart.

## Self-check

1. Identify the strongest intermolecular force in each: CO₂, HF, CH₃OH, KBr dissolved in water, Ar.
2. Explain why H₂O boils about 160 K above H₂S.
3. Rank the alkanes CH₄, C₂H₆, C₃H₈ and C₄H₁₀ by vapour pressure at 25 °C and explain.
4. Classify diamond, dry ice, sodium fluoride and tungsten as types of solid and give one property of each that follows from its structure.
5. Describe how the particle spacing and motion differ between a liquid and a gas, and use that to explain compressibility.

*Answers:* (1) CO₂ dispersion (nonpolar, linear); HF hydrogen bonding; CH₃OH hydrogen bonding; KBr in water ion–dipole; Ar dispersion. (2) Water hydrogen bonds (H on O with lone pairs on O); H₂S cannot (S is too large and not electronegative enough), so despite more electrons it has only weak dipole–dipole and dispersion forces. (3) CH₄ > C₂H₆ > C₃H₈ > C₄H₁₀: dispersion forces grow with size, so larger alkanes escape the liquid less readily and have lower vapour pressures. (4) Diamond: covalent network, extremely hard; dry ice: molecular, sublimes at −78 °C; NaF: ionic, conducts when molten; tungsten: metallic, conducts and is ductile. (5) Liquid particles are in contact and slide; gas particles are far apart and move freely; only the gas has empty space to remove, so only the gas compresses easily.
