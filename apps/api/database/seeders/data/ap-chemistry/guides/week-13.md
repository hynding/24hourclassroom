## What you need to be able to do

- **4.1** Identify evidence that a chemical change has occurred (new substance with different properties: gas, precipitate, colour change, large energy change) and explain a chemical reaction as the breaking and forming of bonds that rearranges atoms without creating or destroying them.
- **4.1** Write and balance a chemical equation from a description or from a particulate diagram, conserving atoms of every element and total charge.
- **4.2** Write the molecular, complete ionic and net ionic equation for a reaction in aqueous solution, using the solubility rules and the list of strong acids and bases to decide which species are written as ions; identify spectator ions.
- **4.3** Interpret and draw particulate representations of a reaction, including the correct numbers of each species before and after, and connect them to the balanced equation and to the quantities of reactants and products.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Chemical change | Atoms are rearranged into new substances with new properties; bonds break and form. |
| Physical change | The substance stays the same; only its state, shape or mixing changes (melting, dissolving, cutting). |
| Balanced equation | Same number of atoms of each element, and the same total charge, on both sides. |
| Molecular equation | Every compound written as a complete formula: Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2 KI(aq) → PbI₂(s) + 2 KNO₃(aq). |
| Complete ionic equation | Every strong electrolyte in solution written as separate ions. |
| Net ionic equation | The complete ionic equation with spectator ions removed: Pb²⁺(aq) + 2 I⁻(aq) → PbI₂(s). |
| Spectator ion | An ion present but unchanged on both sides; it stays in solution and is omitted from the net ionic equation. |
| Strong electrolyte | Soluble ionic compound, strong acid or strong base: written as ions in solution. |
| Weak electrolyte / non-electrolyte | Weak acids and bases, molecular compounds, insoluble solids, gases, water: written as complete formulas. |
| Particulate diagram | A drawing of a small sample showing each molecule, ion or atom; before-and-after diagrams of a reaction must conserve atoms. |

## Recognising and writing reactions (4.1)

A **chemical reaction** rearranges atoms: some bonds break, others form, and the products are different substances with different properties. Evidence includes a gas (bubbles from a solid or solution that was not boiling), a **precipitate** (a solid forming when two clear solutions are mixed), a permanent colour change, a large temperature change, or a flame. A change of state, dissolving, or cutting is **physical**: the particles are the same before and after. (Dissolving an ionic solid is treated as physical in this course even though the lattice comes apart; the ions are unchanged.)

Because atoms are neither created nor destroyed, a reaction is written as a **balanced equation**: the same count of every element on both sides, and the same total charge. The equation is balanced with coefficients, never by changing a formula. The method is routine: write correct formulas, balance the element that appears in the fewest places first, leave H and O for last, and clear fractions at the end.

**Worked example 1.** Propane burns in air. C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O. Carbon: 3 CO₂. Hydrogen: 4 H₂O. Oxygen on the right: 6 + 4 = 10, so 5 O₂. C₃H₈ + 5 O₂ → 3 CO₂ + 4 H₂O. Check: C 3/3, H 8/8, O 10/10. The sum of the coefficients is 1 + 5 + 3 + 4 = 13.

**Worked example 2.** Aluminium reacts with oxygen to form aluminium oxide. Al + O₂ → Al₂O₃. Oxygen gives 3 O₂ → 2 Al₂O₃ (six O each side), then 4 Al. 4 Al + 3 O₂ → 2 Al₂O₃.

State symbols matter in this unit: (s), (l), (g), (aq). They tell you what dissolves and what does not, which is the whole of the next section.

**Try it.** Balance: Fe₂O₃ + CO → Fe + CO₂. *(Fe₂O₃ + 3 CO → 2 Fe + 3 CO₂.)*

## Net ionic equations (4.2)

When two solutions are mixed, most of the ions present do nothing. The **net ionic equation** shows only the species that actually change. To write it you need two lists.

**Strong electrolytes (written as separate ions in solution):**
- Soluble ionic compounds. The AP solubility rules: all compounds of Na⁺, K⁺, NH₄⁺ and all nitrates (NO₃⁻) are soluble; most chlorides, bromides and iodides are soluble except those of Ag⁺, Pb²⁺ and Hg₂²⁺; most sulfates are soluble except those of Ba²⁺, Sr²⁺, Pb²⁺ (and Ca²⁺ is only slightly soluble); most carbonates, phosphates, sulfides and hydroxides are insoluble except those of group 1 and NH₄⁺ (and Ba(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂ and Ca(OH)₂ are soluble to moderately soluble).
- Strong acids: HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄ (first proton), HClO₄. Written as H⁺(aq) + anion.
- Strong bases: the hydroxides of group 1 and of Ca, Sr, Ba.

**Everything else is written as a complete formula:** weak acids (CH₃COOH, HF, H₂CO₃, H₃PO₄), weak bases (NH₃), molecular compounds (sugar, ethanol), insoluble solids, gases, and water.

**The procedure.** (1) Write the balanced molecular equation with state symbols, using the rules to decide whether each product is (aq), (s) or (g). (2) Rewrite every (aq) strong electrolyte as its ions, with coefficients multiplied through: the complete ionic equation. (3) Cancel every ion that appears unchanged on both sides (the spectators). What remains is the net ionic equation. Check that atoms and charge still balance.

**Worked example 3: precipitation.** Lead(II) nitrate and potassium iodide. Molecular: Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2 KI(aq) → PbI₂(s) + 2 KNO₃(aq) (PbI₂ is one of the insoluble iodides; KNO₃ is soluble). Complete ionic: Pb²⁺ + 2 NO₃⁻ + 2 K⁺ + 2 I⁻ → PbI₂(s) + 2 K⁺ + 2 NO₃⁻. Spectators: K⁺ and NO₃⁻. Net ionic: Pb²⁺(aq) + 2 I⁻(aq) → PbI₂(s). Charge check: +2 − 2 = 0 on both sides.

**Worked example 4: strong acid and strong base.** HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l). Complete ionic: H⁺ + Cl⁻ + Na⁺ + OH⁻ → Na⁺ + Cl⁻ + H₂O. Net ionic: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l). Every strong acid–strong base neutralisation has this same net ionic equation, which is why they all release the same heat per mole of water.

**Worked example 5: weak acid and strong base.** Acetic acid is a weak acid, so it stays as a molecule: CH₃COOH(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H₂O(l). The Na⁺ is the only spectator. The acid is not split into H⁺ and CH₃COO⁻ because almost all of it is un-ionised in solution.

**Worked example 6: a gas-forming reaction.** Sodium carbonate and hydrochloric acid. Molecular: Na₂CO₃(aq) + 2 HCl(aq) → 2 NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g). Net ionic: CO₃²⁻(aq) + 2 H⁺(aq) → H₂O(l) + CO₂(g). (Carbonic acid, H₂CO₃, decomposes to water and carbon dioxide, which bubbles off.)

**No reaction.** If every possible product is soluble and no gas or water forms, nothing happens: NaCl(aq) + KNO₃(aq) gives a solution of four spectator ions and no net ionic equation.

**Try it.** Write the net ionic equation for mixing barium chloride and sodium sulfate solutions. *(Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s); Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are spectators.)*

## Particulate representations of reactions (4.3)

The exam loves a box of circles. A particulate diagram shows a tiny sample: each molecule as its atoms joined, each ion as a labelled sphere. A "before" and "after" pair must obey conservation of atoms, and the numbers in the boxes are the coefficients (or multiples of them).

**Reading a diagram.** Count each species before and after. Convert the counts to the smallest whole-number ratio to get the balanced equation. Whatever is left over unchanged after the reaction was in excess; the species that ran out was limiting (the quantitative treatment is week 14).

**Worked example 7.** Before: 4 molecules A₂ and 2 molecules B₂. After: 4 molecules A₂B and nothing else. The ratio 4 : 2 : 4 reduces to 2 : 1 : 2, so the equation is 2 A₂ + B₂ → 2 A₂B. Atom check: 8 A and 4 B on each side.

**Worked example 8.** Before: 4 H₂ and 3 O₂. The reaction is 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O. Four H₂ need only 2 O₂, so after: 4 H₂O and 1 O₂ left over. Hydrogen was limiting; oxygen was in excess. A diagram that showed 6 H₂O would have created hydrogen atoms from nothing.

**Drawing a solution reaction.** A particulate diagram of the lead iodide precipitation shows, before mixing, hydrated Pb²⁺ and NO₃⁻ in one beaker (1 : 2) and K⁺ and I⁻ in the other (1 : 1); after mixing, a clump of alternating Pb²⁺ and I⁻ ions (the solid) at the bottom, with K⁺ and NO₃⁻ still dispersed and hydrated. The spectators are the ions whose picture does not change.

**Try it.** A diagram shows 3 N₂ and 9 H₂ before, and 6 NH₃ after. Write the balanced equation and state whether anything is left over. *(N₂ + 3 H₂ → 2 NH₃, scaled by 3; nothing is left over because 3 : 9 is exactly the 1 : 3 ratio.)*

## Common mistakes

- Balancing by changing a subscript (H₂O → H₂O₂) instead of a coefficient.
- Splitting an insoluble solid, a weak acid, water or a gas into ions in the complete ionic equation.
- Writing H₂SO₄ as 2 H⁺ + SO₄²⁻ in a net ionic equation; only the first proton is strong (AP accepts either, but know why).
- Forgetting to multiply the ions by the coefficient of their compound (2 KI gives 2 K⁺ and 2 I⁻).
- Cancelling ions that are not identical on both sides (an ion that changes state, from (aq) to (s), is not a spectator).
- Leaving the net ionic equation with unequal charges; if it does not balance, a spectator or coefficient was mishandled.
- Drawing an "after" box with more atoms than the "before" box.

## Self-check

1. Classify as physical or chemical: ice melting; iron rusting; sugar dissolving; baking soda fizzing in vinegar; glass shattering.
2. Balance: C₄H₁₀ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O.
3. Write the net ionic equation for aqueous silver nitrate mixed with aqueous sodium chloride, and name the spectators.
4. Write the net ionic equation for nitric acid reacting with aqueous ammonia (a weak base).
5. A before box holds 2 CH₄ and 4 O₂. Draw the after box in words for CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O.

*Answers:* (1) physical; chemical; physical; chemical; physical. (2) 2 C₄H₁₀ + 13 O₂ → 8 CO₂ + 10 H₂O. (3) Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s); Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ are spectators. (4) H⁺(aq) + NH₃(aq) → NH₄⁺(aq); NO₃⁻ is the spectator. (5) 2 CO₂ and 4 H₂O, nothing left over (2 : 4 is exactly 1 : 2); atoms: 2 C, 8 H, 8 O on each side.
