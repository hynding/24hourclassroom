## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Brønsted–Lowry acid / base | A proton (H⁺) donor / a proton acceptor. |
| Conjugate pair | Two species that differ by one proton: HF/F⁻, NH₄⁺/NH₃, H₂O/OH⁻, H₃O⁺/H₂O. |
| Strong acid | Ionises completely in water: HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄, HClO₄. Its conjugate base is negligible as a base. |
| Weak acid | Ionises only partly: HF, CH₃COOH, HCN, H₂CO₃, NH₄⁺. Written as the molecule in net ionic equations. |
| Amphoteric | Able to act as an acid or a base: H₂O, HCO₃⁻, HSO₄⁻, H₂PO₄⁻. |
| Oxidation number | The charge an atom would have if every bond were ionic; assigned by rules. |
| Oxidation / reduction | Loss of electrons (oxidation number rises) / gain of electrons (oxidation number falls). "OIL RIG". |
| Oxidising agent | The species that is reduced (it takes electrons). Reducing agent: the species that is oxidised (it gives electrons). |
| Half-reaction | The oxidation or reduction written separately, with electrons shown explicitly. |
| Redox titration | A titration whose reaction is electron transfer; KMnO₄ is its own indicator (purple → colourless). |

## Acid–base reactions (4.8)

In the **Brønsted–Lowry** picture an acid is a **proton donor** and a base is a **proton acceptor**, and every acid–base reaction is a proton moving from one to the other. The acid becomes its **conjugate base** (what is left after the proton goes); the base becomes its **conjugate acid**. A conjugate pair differs by exactly one H⁺.

HF(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + F⁻(aq): HF is the acid, H₂O the base; F⁻ is the conjugate base of HF, H₃O⁺ the conjugate acid of water.
NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq): now water is the acid (donating to NH₃) and OH⁻ is its conjugate base.

Water appears on both sides of that comparison because it is **amphoteric**: it can donate or accept a proton depending on its partner. So can any species that still has a proton to give and room to take one: HCO₃⁻, HSO₄⁻, H₂PO₄⁻.

**Strong versus weak.** A **strong acid** (the six: HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄, HClO₄) transfers its proton to water essentially completely, so a 0.10 M HCl solution contains 0.10 M H₃O⁺, 0.10 M Cl⁻ and no HCl molecules. A **weak acid** (HF, CH₃COOH, HCN, H₂CO₃ and nearly every other acid) transfers only a small fraction; 0.10 M acetic acid contains mostly CH₃COOH molecules and about 0.0013 M H₃O⁺. A particulate drawing of a strong acid shows every acid molecule split into ions; one of a weak acid shows mostly intact molecules with a few ion pairs. The same is true of bases: NaOH, KOH and Ba(OH)₂ are fully dissociated; NH₃ is a weak base and a 0.10 M solution contains mostly NH₃ molecules.

There is a useful corollary: **the stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base.** Cl⁻ has no tendency to take a proton back; F⁻ and CH₃COO⁻ do, which is why their solutions are slightly basic (unit 8 quantifies this).

**Net ionic equations for acid–base reactions.** Strong acids and bases are written as ions; weak ones as molecules.
- Strong + strong: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.
- Weak acid + strong base: CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O.
- Strong acid + weak base: H⁺ + NH₃ → NH₄⁺.
- Weak acid + weak base: CH₃COOH + NH₃ → CH₃COO⁻ + NH₄⁺ (both written as molecules; nothing to cancel).

**Worked example 1.** Identify the conjugate pairs in HSO₄⁻ + NH₃ → SO₄²⁻ + NH₄⁺. *HSO₄⁻ (acid) / SO₄²⁻ (conjugate base); NH₃ (base) / NH₄⁺ (conjugate acid).*

**Worked example 2.** Two beakers hold 0.10 M HCl and 0.10 M HF. Compare their particulate pictures and the number of OH⁻ ions each needs for complete neutralisation. *HCl: all as H₃O⁺ and Cl⁻. HF: mostly HF molecules, a few H₃O⁺ and F⁻. Yet both need the same moles of OH⁻ (0.10 mol per litre): as OH⁻ removes H₃O⁺ from the HF solution, more HF ionises until all of it has reacted. Strength is about the fraction ionised at any moment, not about how much base an acid can ultimately consume.*

**Try it.** Write the net ionic equation for aqueous hydrocyanic acid, HCN (weak), reacting with aqueous KOH, and name the conjugate base formed. *(HCN + OH⁻ → CN⁻ + H₂O; CN⁻ is the conjugate base of HCN.)*

## Oxidation–reduction (4.9)

A redox reaction moves electrons. To track them, every atom is given an **oxidation number** by a short set of rules, applied in order:

1. An atom in a free element is 0 (Na, O₂, P₄, Cu).
2. A monatomic ion has its charge (Na⁺ +1, S²⁻ −2).
3. Fluorine is −1 in compounds. Group 1 metals are +1, group 2 are +2.
4. Hydrogen is +1 (except −1 in metal hydrides such as NaH).
5. Oxygen is −2 (except −1 in peroxides such as H₂O₂, and positive with fluorine).
6. The oxidation numbers in a neutral compound sum to 0; in a polyatomic ion they sum to its charge.

**Worked example 3.** Cr in Cr₂O₇²⁻: 2x + 7(−2) = −2, x = +6. Mn in MnO₄⁻: x + 4(−2) = −1, x = +7. S in SO₄²⁻: +6. N in NH₄⁺: x + 4(+1) = +1, x = −3. Cl in ClO₃⁻: +5. C in CH₄: −4; C in CO₂: +4. Fractional values are allowed as averages (Fe in Fe₃O₄ is +8/3).

**Oxidation** is an increase in oxidation number (loss of electrons); **reduction** is a decrease (gain). The species oxidised is the **reducing agent**; the species reduced is the **oxidising agent**. In Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, zinc is oxidised (0 → +2) and is the reducing agent; Cu²⁺ is reduced (+2 → 0) and is the oxidising agent. Metals with low ionization energies (group 1, group 2, Al, Zn) are good reducing agents; electronegative nonmetals (F₂, Cl₂, O₂) and high-oxidation-state species (MnO₄⁻, Cr₂O₇²⁻, NO₃⁻ in acid) are good oxidising agents.

**Half-reactions.** Each redox reaction can be split into an oxidation half and a reduction half, with electrons shown. Balancing in **acidic solution** (the half-reaction method):

1. Write the two half-reactions.
2. Balance all atoms except O and H.
3. Balance O by adding H₂O; balance H by adding H⁺.
4. Balance charge by adding electrons (e⁻) to the more positive side.
5. Multiply the halves so the electrons cancel; add; simplify.
(In **basic solution**, finish by adding OH⁻ to both sides to neutralise every H⁺, cancelling water.)

**Worked example 4: permanganate and iron(II) in acid.**
Reduction: MnO₄⁻ → Mn²⁺. O: add 4 H₂O on the right. H: add 8 H⁺ on the left. Charge: left −1 + 8 = +7, right +2, so add 5 e⁻ on the left: MnO₄⁻ + 8 H⁺ + 5 e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4 H₂O.
Oxidation: Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻. Multiply by 5.
Overall: MnO₄⁻ + 8 H⁺ + 5 Fe²⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 4 H₂O + 5 Fe³⁺. Check: Mn 1/1, O 4/4, H 8/8, Fe 5/5; charge +17 on both sides.

**Worked example 5: copper and nitric acid.** Cu + NO₃⁻ → Cu²⁺ + NO₂ (brown gas) in acid. Oxidation: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2 e⁻. Reduction: NO₃⁻ + 2 H⁺ + e⁻ → NO₂ + H₂O (N goes +5 → +4). Double the reduction: Cu + 2 NO₃⁻ + 4 H⁺ → Cu²⁺ + 2 NO₂ + 2 H₂O. Charge: +2 on each side.

**Worked example 6: a redox titration.** Potassium permanganate is its own indicator: MnO₄⁻ is intensely purple and Mn²⁺ is nearly colourless, so the end point is the first permanent pale pink. A 25.00 mL sample of an acidified iron(II) solution needs 18.50 mL of 0.0200 M KMnO₄. n(MnO₄⁻) = 0.01850 × 0.0200 = 3.70 × 10⁻⁴ mol; × 5 = 1.85 × 10⁻³ mol Fe²⁺; [Fe²⁺] = 1.85 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.02500 = 0.0740 M. The iron in the sample is 1.85 × 10⁻³ × 55.85 = 0.103 g.

**Electrons as a quantity.** The number of electrons transferred is the oxidation-number change times the number of atoms. When 0.250 mol of Zn reacts with Cu²⁺, 0.250 × 2 = 0.500 mol of electrons pass from zinc to copper; in 2 Al + 3 Cu²⁺ → 2 Al³⁺ + 3 Cu, six electrons move per equation as written. Unit 9 turns this count into current and time.

**Try it.** Balance in acidic solution: Cr₂O₇²⁻ + Fe²⁺ → Cr³⁺ + Fe³⁺. *(Reduction: Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14 H⁺ + 6 e⁻ → 2 Cr³⁺ + 7 H₂O. Oxidation ×6: 6 Fe²⁺ → 6 Fe³⁺ + 6 e⁻. Overall: Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14 H⁺ + 6 Fe²⁺ → 2 Cr³⁺ + 7 H₂O + 6 Fe³⁺; charge +24 both sides.)*

## Common mistakes

- Calling a strong acid "concentrated" or a weak acid "dilute"; strength is the fraction ionised, concentration is the molarity.
- Writing a weak acid as ions in a net ionic equation, or a strong acid as a molecule.
- Pairing the wrong species as conjugates (HF and OH⁻ are not a conjugate pair; HF and F⁻ are).
- Treating oxidation numbers as real charges on atoms in covalent compounds.
- Confusing the oxidising agent with the species oxidised; the oxidising agent is reduced.
- Balancing O and H before the other elements, or forgetting to balance charge with electrons.
- Forgetting the 5 : 1 ratio in a permanganate–iron(II) titration.

1. Identify the acid, base, conjugate acid and conjugate base in HCO₃⁻ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ + OH⁻.
2. Write the net ionic equation for aqueous ammonia reacting with hydrochloric acid.
3. Assign the oxidation number of each element in K₂Cr₂O₇, H₂O₂ and NH₄NO₃.
4. In the reaction 2 Al + 3 Cl₂ → 2 AlCl₃, identify the species oxidised, the species reduced, the oxidising agent and the number of moles of electrons transferred per mole of Al.
5. 20.00 mL of an oxalic acid solution, H₂C₂O₄, is titrated in acid with 0.0150 M KMnO₄; 16.40 mL is required. Given 5 H₂C₂O₄ + 2 MnO₄⁻ + 6 H⁺ → 10 CO₂ + 2 Mn²⁺ + 8 H₂O, find [H₂C₂O₄].

*Answers:* (1) HCO₃⁻ is the base (accepts H⁺) and H₂CO₃ its conjugate acid; H₂O is the acid and OH⁻ its conjugate base. (2) NH₃(aq) + H⁺(aq) → NH₄⁺(aq). (3) K +1, Cr +6, O −2; H +1, O −1; in NH₄⁺ N −3 and H +1, in NO₃⁻ N +5 and O −2. (4) Al is oxidised (0 → +3), Cl₂ is reduced (0 → −1), Cl₂ is the oxidising agent, 3 mol e⁻ per mol Al. (5) n(MnO₄⁻) = 2.46 × 10⁻⁴ mol; × 5/2 = 6.15 × 10⁻⁴ mol H₂C₂O₄; ÷ 0.02000 L = 0.0308 M.
