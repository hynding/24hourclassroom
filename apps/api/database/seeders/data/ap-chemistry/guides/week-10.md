## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Solution | A homogeneous mixture: solute particles dispersed uniformly in a solvent at the molecular or ionic level. |
| Solvent / solute | The component present in the larger amount (usually water) / the dissolved component(s). |
| Molarity, M | Moles of solute per litre of **solution** (not per litre of solvent): M = n/V. |
| Dilution | Adding solvent lowers the concentration while the moles of solute stay the same: M₁V₁ = M₂V₂. |
| Strong electrolyte | A solute that dissociates completely into ions in water (soluble ionic compounds, strong acids, strong bases). |
| Solvation (hydration) | Solvent molecules surrounding a solute particle; in water, the δ− oxygen faces cations and the δ+ hydrogens face anions. |
| Particulate diagram | A drawing of a small region of a solution showing solute particles and solvent molecules in the right proportions and orientations. |
| Chromatography | Separation of a mixture by passing it in a mobile phase over a stationary phase; components with weaker attraction to the stationary phase travel farther. |
| Retention factor, R_f | Distance travelled by a component ÷ distance travelled by the solvent front; between 0 and 1, characteristic of a substance under fixed conditions. |
| Distillation | Separation of liquids by boiling point: the component with the weaker intermolecular forces boils first and is condensed and collected. |

## Solutions and molarity (3.7)

A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture: the solute is dispersed as individual molecules or ions through the solvent, so every drop has the same composition and nothing settles. Salt water, air, brass and vinegar are all solutions; muddy water and salad dressing are heterogeneous mixtures whose composition varies from place to place.

Concentration tells you how much solute is in a given amount of solution. AP Chemistry uses **molarity** almost exclusively:

M = moles of solute ÷ litres of solution

Two details catch people out. The volume is the volume of the **final solution**, not the volume of water added: to make 1.00 L of 1.00 M NaCl you dissolve 58.44 g of NaCl in *less* than a litre of water and then add water up to the 1.00 L mark of a volumetric flask. And the moles are moles of the **formula unit as weighed**, so a hydrate's molar mass includes its water.

**Worked example 1: molarity from a mass.** 5.85 g of NaCl is dissolved in enough water to make 250.0 mL of solution. Molar mass of NaCl = 22.99 + 35.45 = 58.44 g/mol. n = 5.85 ÷ 58.44 = 0.100 mol. M = 0.100 mol ÷ 0.2500 L = 0.400 M.

**Worked example 2: mass needed for a target concentration.** How many grams of KNO₃ (101.11 g/mol) are needed to make 250.0 mL of 0.150 M solution? n = M × V = 0.150 mol/L × 0.2500 L = 0.0375 mol; mass = 0.0375 × 101.11 = 3.79 g.

**Worked example 3: dilution.** A stock of 6.00 M HCl is diluted: 25.0 mL of it is made up to 500.0 mL. Moles are conserved, so M₁V₁ = M₂V₂: (6.00)(25.0) = M₂(500.0), M₂ = 0.300 M. Volumes may stay in mL because the units cancel.

**Worked example 4: mixing two solutions of the same solute.** 100.0 mL of 0.200 M NaCl is mixed with 200.0 mL of 0.100 M NaCl. Moles: 0.0200 + 0.0200 = 0.0400 mol in 0.3000 L → 0.133 M. Never average the molarities; add the moles and divide by the total volume.

**Ion concentrations.** A soluble ionic compound dissociates completely, so each ion's molarity is its subscript times the compound's molarity. In 0.20 M Na₂SO₄, [Na⁺] = 0.40 M and [SO₄²⁻] = 0.20 M. In 0.30 M CaCl₂, [Cl⁻] = 0.60 M. The total ion concentration (0.60 M for the sodium sulfate) is what matters for properties such as conductivity. A molecular solute such as glucose or ethanol does not dissociate: 0.10 M glucose contains 0.10 M glucose molecules and no ions.

**Try it.** 17.1 g of sucrose, C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ (342.30 g/mol), is dissolved to make 250.0 mL of solution. What is the molarity? What volume of this solution contains 0.0100 mol of sucrose? *(n = 0.0500 mol; M = 0.200 M; V = 0.0100 ÷ 0.200 = 0.0500 L = 50.0 mL.)*

## Representations of solutions (3.8)

The exam constantly asks you to **draw or judge particulate diagrams** of solutions. A good diagram shows the right particles, in the right ratio, with the solvent oriented correctly.

**Ionic solutes.** When NaCl dissolves, the lattice comes apart into separate Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions, each surrounded by water molecules. Water is polar: its oxygen carries a partial negative charge and its hydrogens partial positive charges. Around a cation the water molecules point their **oxygen** inward; around an anion they point their **hydrogens** inward. These ion–dipole attractions are what pay for breaking the lattice. A diagram of 1 M NaCl should show equal numbers of Na⁺ and Cl⁻, none of them touching each other, and a hydration shell on each. A diagram of MgCl₂ must show two Cl⁻ for every Mg²⁺; a common error on the exam is drawing "MgCl₂ units" floating intact.

**Molecular solutes.** Sugar, ethanol and urea dissolve as whole molecules. Their diagrams show intact molecules hydrogen-bonded to water, never ions. The distinction is testable: a solution of a molecular solute does not conduct electricity, a solution of an ionic solute does, and the diagram should predict that.

**Matching a concentration.** If a diagram of solution A shows four solute particles in a given box and solution B shows eight in the same box, B is twice as concentrated. Dilution is drawn as the same number of solute particles in a bigger box (or fewer per box after the water is added).

**Worked example 5: judging a diagram.** A student draws 0.5 M CaCl₂ as five Ca²⁺ and five Cl⁻ in a box of water, each ion ringed by water molecules with their oxygens pointing at every ion. Two errors: the ratio must be 1 Ca²⁺ : 2 Cl⁻, and the water around the Cl⁻ ions must point its hydrogens, not its oxygens, toward the anion.

**Try it.** Sketch, in words, a particulate diagram of a dilute aqueous solution of KNO₃. *(Separate K⁺ ions each surrounded by water with O inward; separate NO₃⁻ ions each surrounded by water with H inward; equal numbers of each; many more water molecules than ions; no KNO₃ units intact.)*

## Separating mixtures: chromatography and distillation (3.9)

The components of a mixture keep their own properties, so a difference in some property can be used to separate them. Both of the AP methods rest on **intermolecular forces**.

**Chromatography.** A mixture is applied to a **stationary phase** (paper, a silica-coated plate, or a packed column) and carried along it by a **mobile phase** (a solvent, or a gas). Each component spends part of its time attached to the stationary phase and part moving with the mobile phase. A component that is attracted more strongly to the stationary phase than to the mobile phase lags; one attracted more to the mobile phase runs ahead. Because the attractions differ from substance to substance, the components spread out into separate spots or bands.

On paper or a silica plate the stationary phase is **polar** (cellulose and silica are full of O–H groups). With a nonpolar mobile phase, polar components hydrogen-bond to the stationary phase and barely move while nonpolar components ride the solvent to the top. With a more polar mobile phase, polar components move farther. So a spot's position reports its polarity relative to the two phases, and the same component moves a different distance when the solvent is changed.

The position is reported as the **retention factor**:

R_f = (distance moved by the component) ÷ (distance moved by the solvent front)

Both distances are measured from the starting line. R_f is always between 0 (never moved) and 1 (moved with the solvent). For fixed paper, solvent and temperature it is characteristic of the compound, so an unknown can be identified by matching R_f with a standard run on the same plate.

**Worked example 6.** On a silica plate developed with hexane, the solvent front moves 8.0 cm from the start line. Spot A moves 3.6 cm and spot B moves 6.8 cm. R_f(A) = 3.6 ÷ 8.0 = 0.45; R_f(B) = 6.8 ÷ 8.0 = 0.85. B is attracted less strongly to the polar silica than A is, so B is the less polar compound. Switching to a more polar solvent would raise both R_f values, A's the most.

**Column and gas chromatography** work the same way: in a column the mixture is washed down a packed tube and components emerge at different times (the retention time plays the role of R_f); in gas chromatography a carrier gas sweeps vaporised components through a coated column.

**Distillation.** Two miscible liquids with different boiling points are separated by heating: the liquid with the **weaker intermolecular forces** has the higher vapour pressure, boils first, and its vapour is led into a condenser and collected. Ethanol (b.p. 78 °C, one O–H) distils from water (100 °C, two O–H and stronger hydrogen bonding); hexane (69 °C, dispersion only) distils from octane (126 °C, larger and more polarisable). The separation is cleanest when the boiling points are far apart.

**Filtration** separates an insoluble solid from a liquid (the solid cannot pass through the paper); it does nothing to a true solution, where the solute particles are molecule-sized.

**Try it.** A green food dye is spotted on paper and developed with water; it separates into a blue spot with R_f = 0.70 and a yellow spot with R_f = 0.25. Which component has the stronger attraction to the paper, and what would you expect if the paper were developed with a less polar solvent? *(Yellow: it moved the least, so it binds the polar cellulose most strongly. A less polar mobile phase would carry the polar components less well, so both R_f values fall, the yellow spot's the most.)*

## Common mistakes

- Dividing by the volume of water instead of the volume of the solution; the solution's volume is what the flask measures.
- Forgetting to convert mL to L in M = n/V (M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ alone tolerates mL, because the units cancel).
- Averaging molarities when two solutions are mixed; add moles, then divide by the total volume.
- Reporting [Cl⁻] in CaCl₂(aq) as equal to the salt's molarity; it is twice that.
- Drawing ionic compounds as intact "molecules" in water, or orienting water the same way around cations and anions.
- Saying a component moves far in chromatography because it is "lighter" or "smaller"; it moves far because it is attracted more weakly to the stationary phase relative to the mobile phase.
- Measuring R_f from the bottom of the paper rather than from the start line, or reporting a value greater than 1.

## Self-check

1. What is the molarity of a solution made by dissolving 9.01 g of glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆ (180.16 g/mol), to make 500.0 mL of solution?
2. What volume of 0.500 M NaOH is needed to make 250.0 mL of 0.0400 M NaOH, and how is the dilution carried out?
3. State the concentration of every ion in 0.15 M Al(NO₃)₃.
4. Describe the particulate picture of Na₂SO₄ dissolved in water, including the ratio of ions and the orientation of the water.
5. Two compounds run on a silica plate with a nonpolar solvent give R_f values of 0.20 and 0.75. Which is more polar, and how do you know?

*Answers:* (1) n = 9.01 ÷ 180.16 = 0.0500 mol; M = 0.0500 ÷ 0.5000 = 0.100 M. (2) V₁ = (0.0400)(250.0) ÷ 0.500 = 20.0 mL; pipette 20.0 mL of stock into a 250.0 mL volumetric flask and add water to the mark. (3) [Al³⁺] = 0.15 M, [NO₃⁻] = 0.45 M. (4) Two Na⁺ for every SO₄²⁻, all separate; water oxygens point at Na⁺, water hydrogens point at SO₄²⁻. (5) The one with R_f = 0.20 is more polar: it is held more strongly by the polar silica relative to the nonpolar solvent and so moves the least.
