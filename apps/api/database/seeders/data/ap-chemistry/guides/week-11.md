## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Soluble / miscible | A solid or gas that dissolves to a useful extent / two liquids that mix in all proportions. |
| "Like dissolves like" | A solute dissolves in a solvent whose intermolecular forces resemble its own, so solute–solvent attractions can replace the solute–solute and solvent–solvent attractions lost. |
| Hydrophilic / hydrophobic | A molecular region that attracts water (polar, hydrogen-bonding: O–H, N–H, C=O) / one that does not (hydrocarbon chains and rings). |
| Ion–dipole attraction | The force that lets water dissolve ionic compounds: water's partial charges surround each ion. |
| Electromagnetic spectrum | Radiation ordered by wavelength or frequency: radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma. |
| Photon | A packet of light of energy E = hν = hc/λ, with h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s and c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s. |
| Frequency, ν | Oscillations per second (Hz = s⁻¹); related to wavelength by c = λν. |
| Microwave absorption | Excites molecular **rotation**. |
| Infrared absorption | Excites molecular **vibration** (bond stretching and bending). |
| Ultraviolet–visible absorption | Excites an **electron** to a higher-energy orbital (an electronic transition). |

## Solubility and intermolecular forces (3.10)

Dissolving is a trade. Solute particles must be pulled away from one another (breaking solute–solute attractions), solvent molecules must be pushed apart to make room (breaking solvent–solvent attractions), and new solute–solvent attractions form. A solute dissolves when the new attractions are strong enough to make the trade worthwhile. That is all "**like dissolves like**" means: when solute and solvent interact by the same kind of force, the attractions gained are comparable to the attractions lost.

**Ionic compounds in water.** Water's strong dipole lets it surround each ion with oriented molecules (oxygen toward cations, hydrogen toward anions). These ion–dipole attractions are strong enough to pay for breaking up the lattice for many salts (NaCl, KNO₃, CaCl₂). In hexane there is no dipole, so nothing can compensate for the lost lattice energy and the salt stays solid. Ionic compounds with very high lattice energies (small, highly charged ions: MgO, Al₂O₃) are insoluble even in water; unit 4 gives the solubility rules, but the reason is this balance.

**Polar molecular solutes in water.** Glucose has five O–H groups, ethanol one, acetone a C=O: each hydrogen-bonds with water, so each dissolves. Glucose is enormously soluble because so much of its surface can hydrogen-bond.

**Nonpolar solutes.** Iodine, naphthalene, hexane and vegetable oil interact only by dispersion forces. In hexane they dissolve readily: dispersion attractions to the solvent replace dispersion attractions in the solid. In water they barely dissolve: the water molecules would have to give up strong hydrogen bonds to one another to make room for a solute that offers only weak dispersion forces in return. (Note that it is the *water–water* attraction being sacrificed, not any repulsion between water and the solute, that keeps oil and water apart.)

**The alcohol series.** Methanol, ethanol and propanol mix with water in all proportions. Butanol is only partly soluble; hexanol and longer alcohols are nearly insoluble. Every alcohol has one O–H group that hydrogen-bonds with water, but as the hydrocarbon chain grows, more of the molecule is a hydrophobic region that disrupts water's hydrogen bonding without replacing it. The single O–H can carry about three or four carbons into solution, not more. The same logic explains why long-chain alcohols become more soluble in hexane: the chain's dispersion forces match the solvent's.

**Worked example 1.** Predict which of CH₃OH and CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂OH (hexan-1-ol) is more soluble in water, and which is more soluble in hexane. *Methanol in water: its O–H hydrogen-bonds and the hydrophobic part is a single carbon. Hexanol in hexane: its six-carbon chain has dispersion attractions comparable to the solvent's, and the small O–H costs little.*

**Worked example 2.** Explain why iodine, I₂, dissolves in hexane but not in water, even though I₂ is a large molecule with many electrons. *I₂ is nonpolar: it has only dispersion forces. Hexane is also nonpolar, so I₂–hexane dispersion attractions are comparable to the I₂–I₂ and hexane–hexane attractions lost. Water would have to break strong hydrogen bonds to accommodate I₂ and gain only weak dispersion attractions in return, so I₂ stays out.*

**Gases.** Gases dissolve better in cold water than hot (the solvated gas has lost kinetic energy, and heating gives it back) and better under higher pressure (more gas molecules strike the surface). A polar gas such as NH₃ or HCl, which hydrogen-bonds or ionises in water, is far more soluble than N₂ or O₂, which rely on dispersion forces.

**Try it.** Rank by water solubility: NaBr, C₈H₁₈ (octane), CH₃CH₂OH, CH₃(CH₂)₄CH₃ (hexane). *(NaBr ≈ CH₃CH₂OH (both very soluble: ion–dipole and hydrogen bonding) ≫ hexane ≈ octane (nonpolar, essentially insoluble).)*

## The electromagnetic spectrum and what each region does (3.11)

Light is an electromagnetic wave characterised by its wavelength λ and frequency ν, which are linked by the speed of light:

c = λν,  c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s

It is also a stream of photons, each carrying one quantum of energy:

E = hν = hc/λ,  h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s

Short wavelength means high frequency and high photon energy; long wavelength means low energy. Ranked from least to most energetic, the regions AP Chemistry cares about are **microwave** (wavelengths of millimetres to centimetres), **infrared** (about 1 μm to 1 mm), **visible** (about 400 nm violet to 700 nm red) and **ultraviolet** (about 10 to 400 nm). X-rays and gamma rays lie beyond the ultraviolet; radio waves lie beyond the microwaves.

A molecule absorbs a photon only when the photon's energy exactly matches the gap between two of its allowed energy levels, and different kinds of molecular motion have gaps of very different size. That is why each region of the spectrum reports something different:

- **Microwaves** have tiny photon energies, matching the small gaps between **rotational** levels. A microwave oven works by driving the rotation of polar water molecules, whose dipoles follow the oscillating field.
- **Infrared** photons match the gaps between **vibrational** levels: the stretching and bending of bonds. Each type of bond (O–H, C=O, C–H) absorbs at a characteristic wavelength, so an infrared spectrum fingerprints the bonds in a molecule. Greenhouse gases (CO₂, H₂O, CH₄) absorb infrared because their vibrations change the molecular dipole.
- **Visible and ultraviolet** photons are energetic enough to promote an **electron** from an occupied orbital to a higher empty one. Substances that absorb visible light appear coloured (they transmit the colours they do not absorb); most small molecules absorb only in the ultraviolet and look colourless.
- **X-rays** eject core electrons entirely; week 3's photoelectron spectroscopy uses photons of this energy to measure binding energies.

**Worked example 3: wavelength to energy.** A blue photon has λ = 450 nm. ν = c/λ = 2.998 × 10⁸ ÷ 4.50 × 10⁻⁷ = 6.66 × 10¹⁴ Hz. E = hν = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ × 6.66 × 10¹⁴ = 4.41 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Per mole of photons that is 4.41 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 6.022 × 10²³ = 2.66 × 10⁵ J/mol = 266 kJ/mol, comparable to a chemical bond energy, which is why ultraviolet and visible light can drive reactions and infrared light cannot.

**Worked example 4: energy to wavelength.** The yellow line of a sodium street lamp corresponds to an electronic transition of 3.37 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. λ = hc/E = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)(2.998 × 10⁸) ÷ 3.37 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 5.89 × 10⁻⁷ m = 589 nm, in the visible (yellow).

**Worked example 5: a microwave oven.** Domestic ovens use 2.45 GHz radiation. λ = c/ν = 2.998 × 10⁸ ÷ 2.45 × 10⁹ = 0.122 m = 12.2 cm. E = hν = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ × 2.45 × 10⁹ = 1.62 × 10⁻²⁴ J per photon, about 10⁵ times smaller than a visible photon: enough to change rotation, nowhere near enough to break a bond.

**Comparing regions by orders of magnitude.** An infrared photon at 3.0 μm carries 6.6 × 10⁻²⁰ J; a visible photon at 500 nm about 4 × 10⁻¹⁹ J; an ultraviolet photon at 250 nm about 8 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Covalent bond energies of 300–500 kJ/mol are 5–8 × 10⁻¹⁹ J per bond, so only ultraviolet (and the bluest visible) photons can break bonds in one step. This is the reason sunburn is caused by UV and not by the far larger amount of visible and infrared light in sunlight.

**Try it.** Place in order of increasing photon energy, and name the molecular process each would excite: 10 cm radiation, 5 μm radiation, 300 nm radiation. *(10 cm (microwave, rotation) < 5 μm (infrared, vibration) < 300 nm (ultraviolet, electronic transition).)*

## Common mistakes

- Saying oil and water "repel"; there is no repulsion, only a failure of weak oil–water attractions to compensate for the strong water–water hydrogen bonds given up.
- Treating any molecule with an O–H as water-soluble regardless of size; a long hydrocarbon chain overwhelms a single polar group.
- Confusing miscible (liquids that mix) with soluble (a solid or gas that dissolves); both are "like dissolves like".
- Mixing up the energy order of the spectrum; remember shorter wavelength = higher frequency = higher energy, and ultraviolet is more energetic than visible, which is more energetic than infrared.
- Assigning infrared to electronic transitions or ultraviolet to vibrations; rotation, vibration, electronic in order of increasing energy.
- Forgetting to convert nm to m (× 10⁻⁹) or GHz to Hz (× 10⁹) before using c = λν.
- Using E = hν with a wavelength, or E = hc/λ with a frequency.

1. Explain why potassium iodide dissolves in water but not in hexane.
2. Which is more soluble in water, propan-1-ol or octan-1-ol? Why?
3. Calculate the energy of one photon of 250 nm ultraviolet light and compare it with a C–H bond energy of 413 kJ/mol.
4. Which region of the spectrum would you use to study the vibrations of the C=O bond in acetone, and why does that region work?
5. A radio station broadcasts at 100 MHz. Calculate its wavelength and explain why radio waves cannot cause chemical reactions.

*Answers:* (1) KI is ionic; water's dipoles form ion–dipole attractions that pay for breaking the lattice; hexane has no dipole and offers only weak dispersion attractions. (2) Propan-1-ol: both have one O–H, but octanol's eight-carbon hydrophobic chain disrupts water's hydrogen bonding without replacing it. (3) E = hc/λ = 1.986 × 10⁻²⁵ ÷ 2.50 × 10⁻⁷ = 7.95 × 10⁻¹⁹ J; a C–H bond is 413 000 ÷ 6.022 × 10²³ = 6.86 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, so one UV photon can break it. (4) Infrared; its photon energies match the gaps between vibrational levels, and a C=O stretch absorbs at a characteristic infrared wavelength. (5) λ = 2.998 × 10⁸ ÷ 1.00 × 10⁸ = 3.00 m; E = hν = 6.6 × 10⁻²⁶ J per photon, far below any bond energy or even a vibrational gap.
