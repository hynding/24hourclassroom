# Week 01 · Moles, Molar Mass and Mass Spectra

**Topics:** 1.1 Moles and Molar Mass · 1.2 Mass Spectra of Elements
**Practices:** 1 (Models and Representations), 3 (Representing Data and Phenomena), 5 (Mathematical Routines)
**Spiral:** This is where the course begins. The mole is the unit that every later calculation in the year is written in: stoichiometry, gas laws, molarity, thermochemistry, equilibrium and electrochemistry all start by converting a measured mass to an amount in moles.

## What you need to be able to do

- **1.1** Convert between the mass of a sample, the number of moles it contains and the number of particles in it, using molar mass and Avogadro's number as the two conversion factors.
- **1.1** Explain why a mole is a useful unit: it links the mass we can weigh in the laboratory to the number of atoms or molecules that actually react.
- **1.2** Read a mass spectrum of a pure element: identify each isotope from the mass-to-charge ratio of its peak, read its relative abundance from the peak height, and calculate the average atomic mass as a weighted average.
- **1.2** Explain why the average atomic mass on the periodic table is not a whole number and why it lies closest to the mass of the most abundant isotope.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mole (mol) | The amount of substance containing exactly 6.022 × 10²³ particles (Avogadro's number, N_A). |
| Avogadro's number | 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹, the number of carbon-12 atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12. |
| Molar mass (M) | The mass of one mole of a substance in g/mol; numerically equal to the average atomic or formula mass in u. |
| Atomic mass unit (u, Da) | One twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom; 1 u = 1.661 × 10⁻²⁴ g. |
| Isotope | Atoms of the same element (same proton count) with different neutron counts and so different masses. |
| Mass number (A) | Protons plus neutrons; an integer that labels an isotope, as in ³⁵Cl. |
| Average atomic mass | The abundance-weighted mean of the isotope masses of an element as found on Earth. |
| Mass spectrum | A plot of signal (relative abundance) against mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) for the ions produced from a sample. |
| m/z | Mass-to-charge ratio; for singly charged ions (z = 1) it equals the ion's mass in u. |
| Relative abundance | The fraction or percentage of an element's atoms that are a given isotope. |

## The mole: counting by weighing (1.1)

Atoms are far too small to count one by one, but they can be weighed in bulk. Chemists chose a counting unit so large that one unit of atoms has a mass we can read on a balance. That unit is the **mole**: 6.022 × 10²³ particles. The number was set so that one mole of carbon-12 atoms has a mass of exactly 12 g, and because every other atomic mass is measured relative to carbon-12, the molar mass of any element in g/mol has the same numerical value as its average atomic mass in u.

Two conversion factors do all the work:

- **Molar mass** converts between grams and moles: n = m / M.
- **Avogadro's number** converts between moles and particles: N = n × 6.022 × 10²³.

The CED equation sheet writes the first as n = m/M; the second is simply the definition of the mole. Every problem in this topic is one or both of these steps.

**Molar mass of a compound** is the sum of the atomic masses of every atom in the formula. Use atomic masses to two decimal places (the values printed on the AP periodic table):

- CaCO₃: 40.08 + 12.01 + 3(16.00) = 100.09 g/mol
- C₆H₁₂O₆: 6(12.01) + 12(1.01) + 6(16.00) = 180.18 g/mol
- Fe₂O₃: 2(55.85) + 3(16.00) = 159.70 g/mol

**Worked example 1.** How many moles of calcium carbonate are in a 10.0 g antacid tablet that is pure CaCO₃?

1. Molar mass of CaCO₃ = 100.09 g/mol.
2. n = m / M = 10.0 g ÷ 100.09 g/mol = 0.0999 mol.

Three significant figures, because the mass was given to three.

**Worked example 2.** How many water molecules are in 2.50 mol of water? How many hydrogen atoms?

1. N = 2.50 mol × 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ = 1.51 × 10²⁴ molecules.
2. Each molecule holds 2 H atoms, so 2 × 1.51 × 10²⁴ = 3.01 × 10²⁴ hydrogen atoms.

The second step is the one students skip: a mole of molecules is not a mole of atoms. Always ask "a mole of what?"

**Worked example 3.** What is the mass of a single gold atom?

M(Au) = 196.97 g/mol, so one atom has mass 196.97 g/mol ÷ 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ = 3.271 × 10⁻²² g. Dividing a molar mass by Avogadro's number gives the mass of one particle; multiplying the mass of one particle by Avogadro's number gives the molar mass.

**Try it.**
1. How many moles of glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆, are in 36.0 g?
2. What mass of NaCl is 0.250 mol?
3. How many carbon atoms are in 1.20 g of pure carbon?

*Answers:* (1) 36.0 ÷ 180.18 = 0.200 mol. (2) M(NaCl) = 22.99 + 35.45 = 58.44 g/mol; 0.250 × 58.44 = 14.6 g. (3) 1.20 ÷ 12.01 = 0.0999 mol; × 6.022 × 10²³ = 6.02 × 10²² atoms.

## Why average atomic mass is an average (1.2)

Chlorine's atomic mass on the periodic table is 35.45, yet no chlorine atom has that mass. Natural chlorine is a mixture of two isotopes: ³⁵Cl (mass 34.969 u, 75.76% of atoms) and ³⁷Cl (mass 36.966 u, 24.24%). The table value is the **weighted average**:

average atomic mass = Σ (fractional abundance × isotope mass)

= (0.7576)(34.969) + (0.2424)(36.966) = 26.49 + 8.96 = 35.45 u

Two things to notice. The average lies between the two isotope masses, closer to the heavier one only if the heavier one is more abundant; here it is closer to 35 because ³⁵Cl dominates. And the isotope masses are not integers either (34.969, not 35.000) because protons and neutrons do not have masses of exactly 1 u and because binding energy removes a little mass; on the exam, though, treating each isotope's mass as its mass number is accepted when exact masses are not given.

## Reading a mass spectrum (1.2)

A mass spectrometer vaporises a sample, ionises the atoms (usually to 1+), accelerates the ions and separates them by mass-to-charge ratio. The detector records how many ions arrive at each m/z. For an element, the result is one peak per isotope: the **position** of the peak is the isotope's mass (for z = 1) and the **height** of the peak is proportional to its abundance.

Graph description: a mass spectrum of magnesium shows three vertical lines. The tallest, at m/z = 24, reaches a relative abundance of 79.0%; a short line at m/z = 25 reaches 10.0%; a slightly taller line at m/z = 26 reaches 11.0%.

**Worked example 4.** Use the magnesium spectrum to calculate the average atomic mass of magnesium, taking each isotope's mass as its mass number, and then with the exact masses 23.985, 24.986 and 25.983 u.

Using mass numbers: (0.790)(24) + (0.100)(25) + (0.110)(26) = 18.96 + 2.50 + 2.86 = 24.32 u.
Using exact masses: (0.790)(23.985) + (0.100)(24.986) + (0.110)(25.983) = 18.95 + 2.50 + 2.86 = 24.31 u.

Both round to the periodic-table value of 24.31. The spectrum also tells you which isotope dominates (²⁴Mg) and lets you predict that the average will be "a little above 24".

**Heights given as ratios.** Sometimes a spectrum gives peak heights that are not percentages, for example 100 : 32.5 : 1.0. Convert to fractions by dividing each height by the sum of all heights. For the three Mg peaks drawn with heights 79.0, 10.0 and 11.0, the sum is 100 so nothing changes; for heights 3 : 1 the fractions are 0.75 and 0.25.

**Worked example 5.** An element's spectrum has two peaks: m/z = 63 (height 69.2) and m/z = 65 (height 30.8). Identify the element.

Fractions: 0.692 and 0.308 (the heights sum to 100). Average = (0.692)(62.93) + (0.308)(64.93) = 43.55 + 20.00 = 63.55 u. The element whose atomic mass is 63.55 is copper. Even without exact masses, (0.692)(63) + (0.308)(65) = 63.6 points to copper.

**Working backwards.** If you know the average atomic mass and the two isotope masses, you can solve for the abundances. Let x be the fraction of ¹⁰B (10.013 u) and 1 − x the fraction of ¹¹B (11.009 u); the average is 10.81:

10.013x + 11.009(1 − x) = 10.81
11.009 − 0.996x = 10.81
x = 0.199 ≈ 0.20

So boron is about 20% ¹⁰B and 80% ¹¹B. Check: (0.199)(10.013) + (0.801)(11.009) = 10.81 ✓.

**Try it.**
1. Silver has two isotopes: ¹⁰⁷Ag (106.905 u, 51.84%) and ¹⁰⁹Ag (108.905 u, 48.16%). Calculate the average atomic mass.
2. A spectrum of an unknown element shows one tall peak at m/z = 39 and a very small peak at m/z = 41 (about 7% of the tall peak's height). Is the element potassium or calcium? Explain.
3. Why does a mass spectrum of a pure element never show a peak at the element's average atomic mass?

*Answers:* (1) (0.5184)(106.905) + (0.4816)(108.905) = 55.42 + 52.45 = 107.87 u. (2) Potassium: its average atomic mass, 39.10, is just above 39, exactly what a dominant ³⁹K peak with a small ⁴¹K peak would produce; calcium's average is 40.08, which would need a dominant peak at 40. (3) Every ion detected is an actual atom with an actual isotope mass; the average is a calculated statistic of the whole mixture, not the mass of any one atom.

## Common mistakes

- Treating "moles of H₂O" and "moles of H atoms" as the same thing; count atoms per formula unit.
- Multiplying instead of dividing: grams ÷ molar mass gives moles, never grams × molar mass.
- Using whole-number atomic masses (C = 12, Cl = 35) when the periodic table values are given; this costs accuracy in the third significant figure.
- Forgetting to convert percentages to fractions before taking the weighted average, which gives an answer 100 times too large.
- Averaging isotope masses without weighting them: (35 + 37) ÷ 2 = 36 is wrong for chlorine.
- Reading a mass spectrum peak as a compound's molar mass; in this unit the spectra are of elements and each peak is one isotope.

## Self-check

1. Calculate the molar mass of ammonium sulfate, (NH₄)₂SO₄.
2. How many moles of atoms (of all kinds) are in 0.500 mol of CO₂?
3. A sample of pure iron contains 2.00 × 10²³ atoms. What is its mass?
4. Describe what the x-axis and the y-axis of an element's mass spectrum represent.
5. Gallium is 60.1% ⁶⁹Ga (68.926 u) and 39.9% ⁷¹Ga (70.925 u). Calculate its average atomic mass and state which isotope it is closer to and why.

*Answers:* (1) 2(14.01) + 8(1.01) + 32.06 + 4(16.00) = 132.16 g/mol. (2) Each CO₂ has 3 atoms, so 1.50 mol of atoms. (3) 2.00 × 10²³ ÷ 6.022 × 10²³ = 0.332 mol; × 55.85 = 18.5 g. (4) x: mass-to-charge ratio m/z (the isotope mass for 1+ ions); y: relative abundance, proportional to the number of ions detected. (5) (0.601)(68.926) + (0.399)(70.925) = 41.42 + 28.30 = 69.72 u; closer to ⁶⁹Ga because it is the more abundant isotope.
