## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| System and surroundings | The system is the part of the universe you are studying (the reacting mixture); the surroundings are everything else (the solvent, the beaker, the air, the thermometer). |
| Heat, q | Energy transferred because of a temperature difference. q > 0 when the system absorbs heat; q < 0 when it releases heat. Units J or kJ. |
| Temperature | A measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles; it decides the direction of heat flow. |
| Thermal equilibrium | The state two objects in contact reach when they have the same temperature and net heat flow stops. |
| Endothermic | The system absorbs heat from the surroundings; ΔH > 0; the surroundings cool; the products have higher potential energy than the reactants. |
| Exothermic | The system releases heat to the surroundings; ΔH < 0; the surroundings warm; the products have lower potential energy. |
| Enthalpy change, ΔH | The heat absorbed or released by a process at constant pressure, per mole as written. |
| Energy diagram | A plot of potential energy for reactants and products, with the activation barrier between them. |
| Specific heat capacity, c | The heat needed to raise 1 g of a substance by 1 °C (or 1 K). Water: 4.184 J/(g·°C). |
| First law of thermodynamics | Energy is conserved: the energy lost by one part of the universe is gained by another. |

## Where chemical energy lives (6.1)

Every reaction breaks some bonds and makes others. Breaking a bond always costs energy (you must pull atoms apart against their attraction); forming a bond always releases energy. The net effect decides the sign of ΔH:

- If the bonds formed are stronger (release more) than the bonds broken (cost), energy is left over and leaves the system as heat: **exothermic**, ΔH < 0, the surroundings get warmer. Combustion, neutralisation, the reaction of sodium with water, most precipitations.
- If the bonds broken cost more than the bonds formed release, the system must take in heat to make up the difference: **endothermic**, ΔH > 0, the surroundings get cooler. Thermal decomposition of CaCO₃, photosynthesis, dissolving ammonium nitrate (the cold pack).

Physical changes follow the same logic with intermolecular attractions in place of bonds. Melting, boiling and sublimation pull particles apart against their attractions: endothermic. Freezing, condensing and depositing let attractions form: exothermic. Dissolving can go either way, because it both separates solute particles and solvent molecules (costs) and forms solute–solvent attractions (releases); NaOH dissolving warms the water, NH₄NO₃ dissolving cools it.

The sign convention is from the system's point of view. "Exothermic" means heat leaves the system; what you feel with your hand is the surroundings. When a cold pack feels cold, the pack's contents are absorbing heat from your hand: endothermic for the system.

**Try it.** Classify: (a) water vapour condensing on a cold window; (b) baking soda reacting with vinegar, which cools the solution; (c) a match burning.
*Answers:* (a) exothermic (attractions form); (b) endothermic (surroundings cool); (c) exothermic.

## Energy diagrams (6.2)

An energy diagram shows the potential energy of the reactants and of the products as two horizontal levels. The vertical distance between them is ΔH. For an exothermic reaction the product level is lower; for an endothermic one it is higher. Draw the barrier between the levels too: the activation energy is the climb from reactants to the top. Two reactions with the same ΔH can have very different barriers, which is why thermodynamics (ΔH) and kinetics (Ea) are separate questions. Methane and oxygen are strongly exothermic together, ΔH = −890 kJ/mol, and sit mixed forever at room temperature until a spark supplies the activation energy.

Reading a diagram described in words: "Reactants at 0 kJ/mol, products at −120 kJ/mol, transition state at +60 kJ/mol." ΔH = −120 kJ/mol (exothermic); Ea = 60 kJ/mol; reverse Ea = 180 kJ/mol.

A diagram can also show a physical change: liquid water at a higher level than ice, with the gap the enthalpy of fusion, 6.01 kJ/mol. There is no barrier worth drawing for melting; the diagram just makes the point that the solid is the lower-energy state.

## Heat transfer and thermal equilibrium (6.3)

Put a hot piece of metal into cool water. The metal's atoms vibrate harder than the water molecules move; at the interface, collisions transfer kinetic energy from the faster particles to the slower ones. The metal cools, the water warms, and the transfers stop when the average kinetic energies, that is the temperatures, are equal. Heat flows spontaneously only from hot to cold. The final temperature is not the average of the two starting temperatures unless the two objects have the same heat capacity.

How much each temperature changes depends on three things, collected in one equation:

q = m c ΔT

where m is mass (g), c is specific heat capacity (J/(g·°C)) and ΔT = T_final − T_initial. A large mass or a large specific heat means a small temperature change for a given quantity of heat. Water's specific heat, 4.184 J/(g·°C), is unusually high (hydrogen bonding), which is why a lake moderates the local climate and why the body is hard to overheat.

Energy conservation links the two objects: q_metal + q_water = 0, or −q_metal = q_water. The metal's q is negative (it loses heat) and the water's is positive.

**Worked example.** A 50.0 g block of copper (c = 0.385 J/(g·°C)) at 95.0 °C is dropped into 100.0 g of water at 20.0 °C. Find the final temperature.
Let T_f be the final temperature. q_copper = 50.0 × 0.385 × (T_f − 95.0); q_water = 100.0 × 4.184 × (T_f − 20.0).
Conservation: 50.0 × 0.385 × (T_f − 95.0) + 100.0 × 4.184 × (T_f − 20.0) = 0
19.25 (T_f − 95.0) + 418.4 (T_f − 20.0) = 0
19.25 T_f − 1,828.75 + 418.4 T_f − 8,368 = 0
437.65 T_f = 10,196.75
T_f = **23.3 °C**.
The water barely warms because its heat capacity (418 J/°C) is twenty times the copper's (19 J/°C).

**Worked example.** 25.0 g of water at 80.0 °C is mixed with 75.0 g of water at 10.0 °C. Final temperature?
Same substance, so c cancels: 25.0 (T_f − 80.0) + 75.0 (T_f − 10.0) = 0 → 100 T_f = 2,000 + 750 = 2,750 → T_f = **27.5 °C**. Equivalently, a mass-weighted average: (25 × 80 + 75 × 10)/100.

**Try it.** How much heat is needed to warm 250 g of water from 20.0 °C to 100.0 °C?
*Answer:* q = 250 × 4.184 × 80.0 = 8.37 × 10⁴ J = 83.7 kJ.

**Try it.** A 20.0 g piece of metal at 100.0 °C is placed in 50.0 g of water at 25.0 °C; the final temperature is 27.4 °C. Find the metal's specific heat.
*Answer:* q_water = 50.0 × 4.184 × 2.4 = 502 J. q_metal = −502 J = 20.0 × c × (27.4 − 100.0) = 20.0 × c × (−72.6), so c = 502/1,452 = 0.346 J/(g·°C).

## Particle-level explanations the exam wants

- **Why heat flows from hot to cold:** on average the hotter object's particles have more kinetic energy; collisions at the boundary transfer energy from faster to slower particles more often than the reverse, so there is a net flow until the averages match.
- **Why a large specific heat means a small ΔT:** the substance has more ways to store energy (in water, hydrogen bonds must be stretched and bent before molecules speed up), so the same heat produces a smaller rise in average kinetic energy.
- **Why the final temperature is nearer the object with the larger heat capacity:** the product m × c sets how much heat changes its temperature by one degree; the larger that product, the less it moves.
- **Why an endothermic reaction makes the beaker cold:** the system takes kinetic energy from the surroundings (the solvent and the glass) to pay for the net bond-breaking, lowering their average kinetic energy.

## Common mistakes

- Getting the sign of q backwards. From the system's view: absorbs heat → q > 0 → endothermic.
- Confusing heat and temperature. Heat is energy transferred; temperature is average kinetic energy. A bathtub of warm water holds far more heat than a cup of boiling water.
- Writing ΔT as T_initial − T_final. It is final minus initial, so a cooling object has a negative ΔT and a negative q.
- Forgetting that in q_lost + q_gained = 0 the two q values have opposite signs; setting them equal without the sign gives nonsense.
- Treating the final temperature of a mixture as the plain average of the two starting temperatures when the masses or specific heats differ.
- Saying bond breaking releases energy. It never does; forming bonds releases energy.
- Assuming an exothermic reaction must be fast. ΔH and Ea are independent.

## Self-check

1. When ammonium chloride dissolves in water the solution cools. Is the process endothermic or exothermic, and what is the sign of ΔH?
2. A reaction's energy diagram has reactants at +40 kJ/mol and products at −20 kJ/mol. State ΔH and classify the reaction.
3. How much heat is released when 500.0 g of water cools from 60.0 °C to 25.0 °C?
4. 40.0 g of water at 90.0 °C is mixed with 60.0 g of water at 15.0 °C. Final temperature?
5. Why does a hot iron pan cool faster in water than in air of the same temperature?

*Answers:* (1) Endothermic, ΔH > 0. (2) ΔH = −60 kJ/mol, exothermic. (3) q = 500.0 × 4.184 × (−35.0) = −7.32 × 10⁴ J; 73.2 kJ released. (4) 40 (T − 90) + 60 (T − 15) = 0 → 100 T = 3,600 + 900 → T = 45.0 °C. (5) Water has a far higher heat capacity per litre and far more particles in contact with the pan per second, so it removes heat much faster for the same temperature difference.
