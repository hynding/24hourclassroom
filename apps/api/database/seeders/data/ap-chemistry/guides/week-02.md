# Week 02 · Composition of Pure Substances and Mixtures

**Topics:** 1.3 Elemental Composition of Pure Substances · 1.4 Composition of Mixtures
**Practices:** 1 (Models and Representations), 2 (Question and Method), 5 (Mathematical Routines), 6 (Argumentation)
**Spiral:** Week 1's mole conversions (n = m/M) and molar masses are used in every calculation this week. A mass percentage is a molar-mass calculation; an empirical formula is a mole-ratio calculation.

## What you need to be able to do

- **1.3** Explain that a pure compound has a fixed composition by mass (the law of definite proportions) because it has a fixed ratio of atoms, and calculate the mass percent of each element from a formula.
- **1.3** Determine an empirical formula from percent composition or from combustion data, and a molecular formula from the empirical formula and the molar mass.
- **1.4** Explain that a mixture has a variable composition, and determine the mass percent of a component of a mixture from gravimetric data such as the mass lost on heating a hydrate or the mass of a precipitate.
- **1.4** Distinguish a pure substance from a mixture at the particulate level and from the data that describe it.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Pure substance | An element or compound with a fixed composition and fixed properties. |
| Law of definite proportions | A given compound always contains its elements in the same proportion by mass. |
| Mass percent of an element | (mass of the element in one mole of compound ÷ molar mass of the compound) × 100. |
| Empirical formula | The simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in a compound (CH₂O for glucose). |
| Molecular formula | The actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule (C₆H₁₂O₆); a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula. |
| Combustion analysis | Burning a known mass of a C/H (or C/H/O) compound and weighing the CO₂ and H₂O produced to find its formula. |
| Mixture | Two or more substances physically combined in variable proportions; each keeps its own properties. |
| Homogeneous vs heterogeneous | Uniform at every scale we can sample (a solution, air) versus visibly or regionally non-uniform (sand and salt). |
| Gravimetric analysis | Finding the amount of a component by converting it to a weighable, pure solid (a precipitate, an anhydrous salt). |
| Hydrate | An ionic solid with a fixed number of water molecules per formula unit, written CuSO₄·5H₂O. |

## A pure compound has a fixed composition (1.3)

Water from a glacier, a tap or a laboratory still is always 11.19% hydrogen and 88.81% oxygen by mass. That is the **law of definite proportions**, and the reason is particulate: every water molecule is H₂O, so the mass ratio is locked to the atom ratio. Compare that to lemonade, which can be as sweet as you like: a mixture has no fixed formula, so its composition can vary.

Mass percent comes straight from the formula and the molar masses:

mass % of element = (atoms of the element × atomic mass) ÷ molar mass × 100

**Worked example 1.** Find the mass percent of each element in glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆ (180.18 g/mol).

- C: 6(12.01) = 72.06 g per mole of glucose; 72.06 ÷ 180.18 × 100 = 40.0%
- H: 12(1.01) = 12.12; 12.12 ÷ 180.18 × 100 = 6.7%
- O: 6(16.00) = 96.00; 96.00 ÷ 180.18 × 100 = 53.3%

Check: 40.0 + 6.7 + 53.3 = 100.0. The percentages always sum to 100 (within rounding); if they do not, something was counted twice or missed.

**Worked example 2.** Which iron ore contains the higher mass percent of iron, hematite (Fe₂O₃) or magnetite (Fe₃O₄)?

- Fe₂O₃: 2(55.85) ÷ 159.70 × 100 = 69.9%
- Fe₃O₄: 3(55.85) ÷ 231.55 × 100 = 72.4%

Magnetite, slightly. The question is really asking you to compare the fraction of each formula's mass that comes from iron.

**Try it.**
1. What is the mass percent of nitrogen in ammonium sulfate, (NH₄)₂SO₄ (132.16 g/mol)?
2. What is the mass percent of water in CuSO₄·5H₂O (249.71 g/mol)?

*Answers:* (1) 2(14.01) ÷ 132.16 × 100 = 21.2%. (2) 5(18.02) = 90.10; 90.10 ÷ 249.71 × 100 = 36.1%.

## From composition to formula (1.3)

The reverse problem is more common on the exam: you are told the composition and asked for the formula. The method is always the same.

1. Assume 100 g of compound, so each percentage becomes a mass in grams.
2. Convert each mass to moles with the element's molar mass.
3. Divide every mole amount by the smallest one.
4. If the ratios are not whole numbers, multiply all of them by the same small integer (×2 for a .5, ×3 for a .33 or .67).
5. Write the empirical formula. If the molar mass is known, divide it by the empirical formula mass to find the multiplier that gives the molecular formula.

**Worked example 3.** A compound is 40.0% C, 6.7% H and 53.3% O by mass, and its molar mass is 180 g/mol. Find its empirical and molecular formulas.

| Element | Mass in 100 g | Moles | ÷ smallest |
|---|---|---|---|
| C | 40.0 g | 40.0 ÷ 12.01 = 3.33 | 1.00 |
| H | 6.7 g | 6.7 ÷ 1.01 = 6.63 | 1.99 ≈ 2 |
| O | 53.3 g | 53.3 ÷ 16.00 = 3.33 | 1.00 |

Empirical formula CH₂O, empirical formula mass 30.03 g/mol. 180 ÷ 30.03 = 5.99 ≈ 6, so the molecular formula is C₆H₁₂O₆.

**Worked example 4 (combustion analysis).** A 0.500 g sample of a hydrocarbon burns completely to give 1.571 g of CO₂ and 0.643 g of H₂O. The compound's molar mass is 56.1 g/mol. Find its molecular formula.

All the carbon ends up in CO₂ and all the hydrogen in H₂O.

- mol C = mol CO₂ = 1.571 ÷ 44.01 = 0.0357 mol
- mol H = 2 × mol H₂O = 2 × (0.643 ÷ 18.02) = 0.0714 mol
- Ratio H : C = 0.0714 ÷ 0.0357 = 2.00, so the empirical formula is CH₂ (14.03 g/mol).
- 56.1 ÷ 14.03 = 4.00, so the molecular formula is C₄H₈.

Mass check: 0.0357 mol C × 12.01 = 0.429 g; 0.0714 mol H × 1.01 = 0.072 g; total 0.501 g ≈ 0.500 g, so the sample contained no oxygen. When a compound does contain oxygen, find its mass by subtraction from the sample mass before converting to moles.

**Try it.**
1. A compound of sulfur and oxygen is 40.0% S by mass. Find its empirical formula.
2. Benzene is 92.3% C and 7.7% H with molar mass 78.1 g/mol. Find its molecular formula.

*Answers:* (1) In 100 g: S 40.0 ÷ 32.06 = 1.25 mol; O 60.0 ÷ 16.00 = 3.75 mol; ratio 1 : 3, SO₃. (2) C 92.3 ÷ 12.01 = 7.69; H 7.7 ÷ 1.01 = 7.62; ratio 1 : 1, CH (13.02); 78.1 ÷ 13.02 = 6, C₆H₆.

## Mixtures have variable composition (1.4)

A mixture holds two or more substances that keep their own identities and can be present in any proportion. Because nothing locks the ratio, a mixture has no formula, only a **composition**, usually expressed as a mass percent of each component. Seawater, brass, air and a sample of impure ore are mixtures; their composition is something you measure, not something you can look up.

The CED asks for one skill: use quantitative data to find how much of a component a mixture contains. Three laboratory routes come up repeatedly.

**Route 1: drive off a volatile component and weigh what is left.** Heating a hydrate removes its water.

**Worked example 5.** A 2.500 g sample of hydrated copper(II) sulfate, CuSO₄·xH₂O, is heated until its mass stops changing at 1.598 g. Find x.

- Mass of water lost: 2.500 − 1.598 = 0.902 g, which is 0.902 ÷ 18.02 = 0.0501 mol H₂O.
- Mass of CuSO₄ left: 1.598 g, which is 1.598 ÷ 159.61 = 0.01001 mol.
- Ratio: 0.0501 ÷ 0.01001 = 5.00, so x = 5 and the hydrate is CuSO₄·5H₂O.

The same data give the mass percent of water in the sample: 0.902 ÷ 2.500 × 100 = 36.1%, which matches the theoretical 36.1% for the pure pentahydrate. If a sample gave 30%, it would be partly dehydrated or contaminated with something that does not lose water, which is exactly the kind of argument an FRQ asks for.

**Route 2: separate a component physically and weigh it.** Dissolve a salt-and-sand mixture in water, filter, dry and weigh the sand.

**Worked example 6.** A 5.00 g mixture of NaCl and sand is stirred with water and filtered. The dried sand weighs 3.20 g. What is the mass percent of NaCl?

NaCl dissolved = 5.00 − 3.20 = 1.80 g; 1.80 ÷ 5.00 × 100 = 36.0% NaCl. The method assumes all the salt dissolved and no sand passed through the filter; an FRQ may ask you to say which assumption would make the result too high or too low.

**Route 3: convert a component into a weighable precipitate.** All the chloride in a sample can be precipitated as AgCl, weighed, and converted back to moles of Cl⁻; Unit 4 does the stoichiometry, but the logic belongs here: a known pure compound with a fixed composition lets you count the component you care about.

**Worked example 7.** An iron ore sample of mass 25.0 g is 40.2% Fe₂O₃ by mass and contains no other iron compound. What mass of iron does it contain?

Mass of Fe₂O₃ = 0.402 × 25.0 = 10.05 g. Iron is 69.9% of Fe₂O₃ (worked example 2), so mass of Fe = 0.699 × 10.05 = 7.03 g. Two percentages in series: the mixture's composition, then the compound's fixed composition.

**Try it.**
1. A 3.00 g sample of impure CaCO₃ is 85.0% CaCO₃ by mass. What mass of calcium does it contain? (Ca is 40.0% of CaCO₃.)
2. A 1.200 g sample of a hydrate of MgSO₄ loses 0.614 g on heating. What percent of the sample was water, and what is x in MgSO₄·xH₂O? (MgSO₄ 120.37 g/mol)

*Answers:* (1) 0.850 × 3.00 = 2.55 g CaCO₃; 0.400 × 2.55 = 1.02 g Ca. (2) 0.614 ÷ 1.200 × 100 = 51.2% water. Water: 0.614 ÷ 18.02 = 0.0341 mol; MgSO₄: 0.586 ÷ 120.37 = 0.00487 mol; ratio 7.0, so MgSO₄·7H₂O.

## Pure substance or mixture? Reading the evidence (1.3, 1.4)

Particulate diagrams on the exam show boxes of circles. One kind of molecule everywhere is a pure substance; two or more kinds is a mixture. In data, a pure compound has a composition that is the same in every sample and matches a formula; a mixture gives different percentages from sample to sample. A melting point is a third clue: a pure substance melts sharply at one temperature, a mixture over a range.

## Common mistakes

- Dividing by the atom count instead of multiplying by it when computing mass percent (hydrogen in water is 2 × 1.01, not 1.01 ÷ 2).
- Rounding a mole ratio of 1.33 or 1.5 to 1; multiply through by 3 or 2 instead.
- Forgetting that each water molecule contributes two hydrogens in combustion analysis.
- Treating a hydrate's water as an impurity: it is part of the pure compound's formula and has a fixed percentage.
- Reporting an empirical formula when the molar mass was given and a molecular formula was asked for.
- Using the mass of the hydrate (before heating) instead of the mass of the anhydrous salt (after) when finding moles of salt.

## Self-check

1. State the law of definite proportions and explain it at the particulate level.
2. Calculate the mass percent of oxygen in KClO₃ (122.55 g/mol).
3. A compound is 52.1% C, 13.1% H and 34.7% O. Find its empirical formula.
4. Explain how heating a hydrate to constant mass lets you find its formula, and name one error that would make x come out too small.
5. Give two kinds of evidence that would show a white powder is a mixture rather than a pure compound.

*Answers:* (1) A compound always has the same elements in the same mass proportions, because every formula unit has the same fixed number of each kind of atom. (2) 3(16.00) ÷ 122.55 × 100 = 39.2%. (3) C 52.1 ÷ 12.01 = 4.34; H 13.1 ÷ 1.01 = 13.0; O 34.7 ÷ 16.00 = 2.17; ÷ 2.17 → C 2, H 6, O 1: C₂H₆O. (4) The mass lost is water, converted to moles; the residue is anhydrous salt, converted to moles; x is their ratio. Not heating long enough (water left behind) makes the loss too small and x too small. (5) Composition that varies between samples; melting over a range rather than at one temperature; a particulate diagram showing two kinds of particles; a separation (filtering, dissolving) that splits it into parts with different properties.
