## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Atomic radius | Half the distance between the nuclei of two bonded identical atoms; decreases across a period, increases down a group. |
| Ionic radius | The radius of an ion; cations are smaller than their atoms, anions are larger. |
| First ionization energy (IE₁) | Energy to remove the most loosely held electron from a gaseous atom: X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻. |
| Successive ionization energies | IE₂, IE₃, ...: removing further electrons; each is larger than the last, with a big jump when a core electron is removed. |
| Electron affinity | The energy change when a gaseous atom gains an electron; most negative (most energy released) for the halogens. |
| Electronegativity | The tendency of a bonded atom to attract shared electrons; fluorine highest (3.98 on the Pauling scale), francium lowest. |
| Valence electrons | Outer-shell electrons; the group number (1, 2, 13–18) gives 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 for main-group elements. |
| Cation / anion | A positive ion formed by losing electrons / a negative ion formed by gaining them. |
| Ionic compound | A neutral lattice of cations and anions in the ratio that cancels their charges. |
| Lattice energy | The energy released when gaseous ions form the solid lattice; larger for higher charges and smaller ions. |

## The trends, and the one law behind them (1.7)

Every periodic trend is Coulomb's law applied to the valence electrons. Two questions settle any comparison: **how large is the effective nuclear charge** the valence electrons feel, and **how far** are they from the nucleus?

**Across a period (left to right):** electrons are added to the same shell while protons are added to the nucleus. The core, and so the shielding, stays the same, so Z_eff rises steadily (about +1 for Na to +7 for Cl). Valence electrons are pulled in harder.

- Atomic radius **decreases** (Na 186 pm, Mg 160, Al 143, Si 118, P 110, S 104, Cl 99).
- First ionization energy **increases** (Na 496, Mg 738, Al 578, Si 786, P 1012, S 1000, Cl 1251, Ar 1521 kJ/mol).
- Electronegativity **increases** (Na 0.93 to Cl 3.16).
- Electron affinity becomes **more negative** (more energy released on gaining an electron) toward the halogens.

**Down a group:** each row adds a shell. The valence electrons are farther out and shielded by one more full core, and the extra protons are mostly cancelled, so Z_eff stays roughly constant while distance grows.

- Atomic radius **increases** (Li 152 pm, Na 186, K 227).
- First ionization energy **decreases** (Li 520, Na 496, K 419 kJ/mol).
- Electronegativity **decreases** (F 3.98, Cl 3.16, Br 2.96, I 2.66).

**The two exceptions in ionization energy.** Across period 3 the ionization energy dips at aluminum (578, below magnesium's 738) and at sulfur (1000, below phosphorus's 1012). Aluminum's electron comes from a 3p orbital, which is higher in energy and better shielded than magnesium's 3s. Sulfur's fourth 3p electron is paired, and the repulsion in the pair makes it easier to remove than one of phosphorus's three unpaired electrons. The same dips appear in period 2 at boron and oxygen, and they are exactly what the PES data of week 3 showed.

**Worked example 1.** Rank Mg, Ca and Al in order of increasing atomic radius and explain.

Al < Mg < Ca. Al and Mg are in the same period, and Al has one more proton acting on the same n = 3 shell, so Al is smaller than Mg. Ca is below Mg with an n = 4 valence shell and an extra core of shielding, so Ca is the largest.

**Worked example 2.** The successive ionization energies of magnesium are IE₁ = 738, IE₂ = 1451 and IE₃ = 7733 kJ/mol. What do they show?

Each electron removed leaves a more positive ion holding fewer electrons, so each ionization costs more than the last; IE₂ is about twice IE₁. The jump from IE₂ to IE₃ is more than five-fold, because the third electron comes from the n = 2 core: it is much closer to the nucleus and no longer shielded by the 3s electrons. A large jump after the nth ionization energy means the element has n valence electrons. Magnesium has two.

**Worked example 3.** Which has the larger radius, S²⁻ or Cl⁻? Which is larger, Na or Na⁺?

S²⁻ and Cl⁻ are isoelectronic (18 electrons each); S²⁻ has only 16 protons to Cl⁻'s 17, so its electrons are held less tightly and S²⁻ is larger (184 pm against 181 pm). Na is larger than Na⁺: the atom has a 3s electron in the third shell; the ion has lost it and its outer shell is now n = 2, so the radius falls from 186 pm to 102 pm. Cations are always smaller than their parent atoms; anions, which add electrons and repulsion to the same shell, are always larger.

**Try it.**
1. Which has the higher first ionization energy, O or N? Explain the surprise.
2. Arrange F⁻, O²⁻ and Na⁺ in order of increasing radius.
3. An element has successive ionization energies (kJ/mol) 578, 1817, 2745, 11577. How many valence electrons does it have, and which group is it in?

*Answers:* (1) N (1402) is higher than O (1314): oxygen's fourth 2p electron is paired, and the repulsion makes it easier to remove. (2) Na⁺ < F⁻ < O²⁻; all have 10 electrons, and the fewer protons, the larger the ion. (3) Three; the jump comes after IE₃ (2745 to 11577), so group 13; the data are aluminum's.

## Valence electrons and the ions atoms form (1.8)

Elements in a group have the same number of valence electrons and so the same chemistry: that is why the periodic table is arranged as it is. A main-group atom tends to lose or gain enough electrons to reach a noble-gas configuration (eight valence electrons, or two for the elements near helium).

| Group | Valence electrons | Ion formed | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | 1+ | Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺ |
| 2 | 2 | 2+ | Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Ba²⁺ |
| 13 | 3 | 3+ | Al³⁺ |
| 15 | 5 | 3− | N³⁻, P³⁻ |
| 16 | 6 | 2− | O²⁻, S²⁻ |
| 17 | 7 | 1− | F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻ |

Metals (low ionization energy) lose electrons; nonmetals (high electron affinity, high electronegativity) gain them. Transition metals form cations too, often with more than one possible charge (Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺; Cu⁺, Cu²⁺), because their 4s and 3d electrons are close in energy.

**Writing formulas.** An ionic compound is neutral, so the ions combine in the smallest ratio that cancels the charges. Cross the charges as subscripts and simplify.

**Worked example 4.** Aluminum oxide and calcium phosphate.

Al³⁺ and O²⁻: 2 Al³⁺ (6+) balance 3 O²⁻ (6−): Al₂O₃. Ca²⁺ and PO₄³⁻: 3 Ca²⁺ (6+) balance 2 PO₄³⁻ (6−): Ca₃(PO₄)₂. Polyatomic ions stay in parentheses when there is more than one.

**Worked example 5.** Explain why magnesium oxide (MgO, melting point 2825 °C) melts far above sodium fluoride (NaF, 993 °C), although both have the same lattice structure.

Coulomb's law for ions: the attraction is proportional to the product of the charges divided by the distance between the ion centres. MgO has 2+ and 2− ions, so the charge product is four times that of NaF's 1+ and 1−, and the ions are smaller (Mg²⁺ 72 pm, O²⁻ 140 pm against Na⁺ 102 pm, F⁻ 133 pm), so the distance is shorter. Both factors make the lattice energy of MgO much larger, and far more thermal energy is needed to break it apart.

**Worked example 6.** Rank NaCl, NaBr and NaI by melting point.

Same charges (1+, 1−), so compare sizes. Iodide is the largest anion, so Na⁺ and I⁻ are farthest apart and attract least: NaI (661 °C) < NaBr (747 °C) < NaCl (801 °C). Distance matters, but charge matters more: when charges differ, charge decides.

**Try it.**
1. Write the formulas of strontium nitride, potassium sulfide and iron(III) sulfate.
2. Which has the higher melting point, CaO or KCl? Give two reasons.
3. Explain why sodium and potassium form compounds with the same formulas (NaCl and KCl, Na₂O and K₂O).

*Answers:* (1) Sr₃N₂, K₂S, Fe₂(SO₄)₃. (2) CaO: its ions carry 2+ and 2− (charge product four times KCl's), and Ca²⁺ and O²⁻ are smaller than K⁺ and Cl⁻, so the attraction is stronger on both counts. (3) Both are in group 1 with one valence electron; each forms a 1+ ion, so the charge balance with any anion is identical.

## Common mistakes

- Saying radius increases across a period because "more electrons take more room"; the extra electrons go into the same shell while Z_eff rises, so the atom shrinks.
- Explaining the group trend with "more electrons" or "more protons" alone; it is the added shell (distance and shielding) that matters.
- Treating the Al and S dips in ionization energy as errors in the data; they are real and have specific explanations (subshell change; electron pairing).
- Confusing ionization energy (removing an electron, always endothermic) with electron affinity (adding one, usually exothermic).
- Writing a cation as larger than its atom. Losing electrons always shrinks the species.
- Forgetting parentheses around a polyatomic ion that appears more than once: Ca(NO₃)₂, not CaNO₃₂.
- Ranking melting points of ionic solids by size when the charges differ; charge dominates.

## Self-check

1. Explain, using effective nuclear charge, why fluorine has a smaller atomic radius than lithium.
2. Why is the first ionization energy of potassium lower than that of sodium?
3. The successive ionization energies of an element are 1012, 1907, 2914, 4964, 6274 and 21267 kJ/mol. How many valence electrons does it have?
4. Rank Al³⁺, Mg²⁺ and Na⁺ by radius and explain.
5. Predict which has the higher melting point, LiF or CsI, and justify with Coulomb's law.

*Answers:* (1) Both have n = 2 valence electrons with a two-electron core, but fluorine has nine protons to lithium's three, so its Z_eff is about +7 against +1 and its electrons are pulled much closer. (2) Potassium's valence electron is in n = 4, farther from the nucleus and shielded by 18 core electrons, so it is held more weakly than sodium's n = 3 electron. (3) Five; the jump comes after the fifth ionization (6274 to 21267): a group 15 element (phosphorus). (4) Al³⁺ < Mg²⁺ < Na⁺; all have 10 electrons, and the more protons, the more tightly the cloud is pulled in. (5) LiF: same 1+/1− charges, but Li⁺ and F⁻ are much smaller than Cs⁺ and I⁻, so the ions are closer and the attraction stronger (LiF melts at 845 °C, CsI at 632 °C).
