## What you need to be able to do

- **4.4** Classify a process as physical or chemical by what changes at the particle level: a physical change alters intermolecular interactions only (melting, boiling, dissolving a molecular solid), while a chemical change breaks and forms chemical bonds (combustion, precipitation, acid ionisation); explain the ambiguous cases (dissolving an ionic solid, dissolving HCl in water).
- **4.5** Use a balanced equation to convert between amounts of reactants and products: mass to mass, mass to gas volume, molarity and volume of a solution to mass of precipitate, and back.
- **4.5** Identify the limiting reactant, calculate the theoretical yield and the amount of excess reactant left, and compute percent yield from an actual yield.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Physical change | Intermolecular (interparticle) interactions change; the substance's identity does not. Melting, boiling, dissolving sugar. |
| Chemical change | Intramolecular bonds break and form; new substances appear. Burning, rusting, precipitating, ionising. |
| Mole ratio | The ratio of coefficients in the balanced equation; the only bridge between amounts of two different substances. |
| Stoichiometry | The quantitative use of the balanced equation: measured quantity → moles → mole ratio → moles → desired quantity. |
| Limiting reactant | The reactant that is used up first and so decides how much product can form. |
| Excess reactant | A reactant left over when the limiting reactant is gone. |
| Theoretical yield | The maximum amount of product, calculated from the limiting reactant. |
| Actual yield | The amount of product actually obtained. |
| Percent yield | (actual ÷ theoretical) × 100 %. |

## Physical and chemical changes at the particle level (4.4)

The useful test is not "can you reverse it?" but **which interactions change**.

- In a **physical change** only intermolecular or interparticle forces are overcome or formed. Ice melting breaks some hydrogen bonds between water molecules; boiling breaks the rest; every H–O bond inside each molecule survives. Dissolving sugar in water replaces sugar–sugar hydrogen bonds with sugar–water hydrogen bonds; the sucrose molecules are intact and can be recovered by evaporating the water.
- In a **chemical change** covalent or ionic bonds are broken and new ones form. Electrolysing water breaks O–H bonds and forms H–H and O=O bonds; the products are different substances. Burning methane, rusting iron, and precipitating silver chloride (ions leaving their hydration shells to form a new ionic lattice) are all chemical.

Two cases sit on the line, and the AP exam asks about both. **Dissolving an ionic solid** such as NaCl breaks ionic attractions in the lattice and forms ion–dipole attractions with water; because the ions themselves are unchanged and the salt is recovered on evaporation, it is usually treated as a physical process, though it involves breaking strong interparticle forces. **Dissolving HCl gas in water** is chemical: the H–Cl covalent bond breaks and a new O–H bond forms in H₃O⁺, so the solution contains new species (hydronium and chloride ions) that were not in the gas.

**Worked example 1.** Classify, with a particle-level reason: (a) dry ice subliming; (b) baking soda reacting with vinegar; (c) sugar caramelising when heated; (d) iodine dissolving in hexane. *(a) Physical: CO₂ molecules escape the dispersion forces holding them in the solid, unchanged. (b) Chemical: HCO₃⁻ and CH₃COOH exchange a proton and C–O bonds break to release CO₂. (c) Chemical: the brown products and the smell are new substances; C–O and C–C bonds break and form. (d) Physical: I₂ molecules leave the solid and are surrounded by hexane; dispersion forces replace dispersion forces.*

**Try it.** A student says that boiling water is a chemical change because "the water turns into hydrogen and oxygen gas". What is wrong, and what evidence would settle it? *(The vapour is still H₂O; condensing it gives water back. Hydrogen and oxygen form only when O–H bonds break, which needs electrolysis or very high temperature; a glowing splint in the vapour would not relight, and a lit splint would not pop.)*

## Stoichiometry: the mole road (4.5)

Every stoichiometry problem has the same spine:

**measured quantity of A → moles of A → (mole ratio from the equation) → moles of B → quantity of B asked for.**

There are three ways into and out of moles, all from earlier weeks: mass ÷ molar mass; PV = nRT (or 22.4 L/mol at STP) for a gas; molarity × volume for a solution. The mole ratio is the only step that links two different substances; it is always the ratio of coefficients.

**Worked example 2: mass to mass.** Fe₂O₃ + 3 CO → 2 Fe + 3 CO₂. What mass of iron forms from 25.0 g of Fe₂O₃ with excess CO? 25.0 ÷ 159.70 = 0.1566 mol Fe₂O₃ × (2 mol Fe / 1 mol Fe₂O₃) = 0.3131 mol Fe × 55.85 = 17.5 g Fe.

**Worked example 3: mass to gas volume.** 2 KClO₃ → 2 KCl + 3 O₂. What volume of O₂ at 298 K and 1.00 atm comes from 5.00 g of KClO₃? 5.00 ÷ 122.55 = 0.0408 mol KClO₃ × (3/2) = 0.0612 mol O₂; V = nRT/P = (0.0612)(0.08206)(298) ÷ 1.00 = 1.50 L.

**Worked example 4: solution to precipitate.** AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq). What mass of AgCl forms when 25.0 mL of 0.150 M AgNO₃ is treated with excess NaCl? 0.0250 L × 0.150 M = 0.00375 mol Ag⁺ → 0.00375 mol AgCl × 143.32 = 0.538 g.

**Worked example 5: gas from a carbonate.** CaCO₃ + 2 HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂. 2.50 g of CaCO₃ with excess acid: 2.50 ÷ 100.09 = 0.0250 mol CO₂; at 298 K and 1.00 atm, V = (0.0250)(0.08206)(298) = 0.611 L.

## Limiting reactant, theoretical and percent yield (4.5)

When the amounts of two reactants are both given, one of them runs out first and stops the reaction. To find which, convert each to moles and compare with the equation. The quickest reliable method: **divide each reactant's moles by its coefficient; the smallest quotient is the limiting reactant.** Everything after that (theoretical yield, leftover excess) is computed from the limiting reactant.

**Worked example 6.** 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O with 4.00 g H₂ and 24.0 g O₂. Moles: 4.00 ÷ 2.016 = 1.98 mol H₂; 24.0 ÷ 32.00 = 0.750 mol O₂. Quotients: 1.98 ÷ 2 = 0.99; 0.750 ÷ 1 = 0.750. Oxygen is limiting. Water formed: 0.750 mol O₂ × 2 = 1.50 mol = 27.0 g. Hydrogen used: 1.50 mol; left over: 1.98 − 1.50 = 0.48 mol = 0.98 g. The intuition "hydrogen is lighter so it runs out first" is wrong; moles, not grams, decide.

**Worked example 7: limiting reactant in solution.** 20.0 mL of 0.100 M Pb(NO₃)₂ is mixed with 30.0 mL of 0.100 M KI. Pb²⁺: 0.00200 mol; I⁻: 0.00300 mol. The equation Pb²⁺ + 2 I⁻ → PbI₂ needs 0.00400 mol I⁻ for all the lead; only 0.00300 is present, so iodide is limiting: 0.00150 mol PbI₂ × 461.0 g/mol = 0.692 g. Lead left in solution: 0.00200 − 0.00150 = 0.00050 mol.

**Percent yield.** Real reactions give less than the theoretical amount: side reactions, incomplete reaction, losses in transfer and filtering. Percent yield = actual ÷ theoretical × 100 %. If the hydrogen and oxygen above gave 24.3 g of water, the yield is 24.3 ÷ 27.0 = 90.0 %. A yield above 100 % means the product is impure or wet, not that the law of conservation of mass has failed.

**Worked example 8: a metal displacement lab.** Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s). A student adds 2.00 g of iron filings (0.0358 mol) to 50.0 mL of 0.500 M CuSO₄ (0.0250 mol). The 1 : 1 ratio makes CuSO₄ limiting. Theoretical copper: 0.0250 mol × 63.55 = 1.59 g. The student recovers 1.42 g, a yield of 89.4 %. Iron left: 0.0358 − 0.0250 = 0.0108 mol (0.60 g), which must be separated from the copper before weighing, one reason the yield is below 100 %.

**Try it.** 0.540 g of aluminium is added to 100.0 mL of 0.250 M CuCl₂: 2 Al + 3 CuCl₂ → 2 AlCl₃ + 3 Cu. Find the limiting reactant and the theoretical mass of copper. *(Al: 0.0200 mol ÷ 2 = 0.0100; CuCl₂: 0.0250 mol ÷ 3 = 0.00833. CuCl₂ limits. Cu: 0.0250 mol × 63.55 = 1.59 g.)*

## Common mistakes

- Comparing reactant masses, or moles without dividing by coefficients, to decide the limiting reactant.
- Using the excess reactant to compute the theoretical yield.
- Skipping the mole ratio and converting grams of A straight to grams of B.
- Forgetting that molarity × volume needs the volume in litres.
- Using 22.4 L/mol at any temperature; it holds only at STP (273 K, 1 atm). Use PV = nRT otherwise.
- Reporting a percent yield above 100 % without questioning the product's purity.
- Calling the dissolving of HCl in water "physical" because it is just dissolving; the H–Cl bond breaks.

## Self-check

1. Classify at the particle level: nitrogen liquefying; a candle burning; salt dissolving in water; silver tarnishing.
2. What mass of CO₂ forms when 10.0 g of propane, C₃H₈, burns completely? (C₃H₈ + 5 O₂ → 3 CO₂ + 4 H₂O)
3. What volume of 0.200 M BaCl₂ is needed to precipitate all the sulfate from 50.0 mL of 0.150 M Na₂SO₄?
4. 10.0 g of N₂ and 2.00 g of H₂ react: N₂ + 3 H₂ → 2 NH₃. Which is limiting, and what mass of NH₃ forms?
5. A reaction with a theoretical yield of 4.20 g gives 3.57 g. What is the percent yield, and name two reasons it is below 100 %?

*Answers:* (1) physical (dispersion forces form); chemical (C–H and O=O bonds break, C=O and O–H form); physical in the AP classification (ionic attractions replaced by ion–dipole, ions unchanged); chemical (Ag₂S forms). (2) 10.0 ÷ 44.09 = 0.227 mol C₃H₈ × 3 = 0.680 mol CO₂ × 44.01 = 29.9 g. (3) 0.0500 L × 0.150 = 0.00750 mol SO₄²⁻ → 0.00750 mol Ba²⁺ ÷ 0.200 M = 0.0375 L = 37.5 mL. (4) N₂: 0.357 mol ÷ 1 = 0.357; H₂: 0.992 mol ÷ 3 = 0.331 → H₂ limiting; NH₃ = 0.992 × 2/3 = 0.661 mol × 17.03 = 11.3 g. (5) 85.0 %; incomplete reaction, side reactions, product lost in transfer or filtration, product still wet when weighed would make it higher, not lower.
