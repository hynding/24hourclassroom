## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Crystal lattice | A regular, repeating three-dimensional arrangement of particles. |
| Ionic solid | A lattice of alternating cations and anions; each ion is surrounded by ions of opposite charge (6 in NaCl). |
| Brittleness | Ionic solids shatter when struck because shifting one layer brings like charges next to each other, which repel. |
| Electron-sea model | Metal cations in fixed positions with valence electrons delocalised over the whole solid. |
| Malleability / ductility | The ability to be hammered into sheets / drawn into wires; layers of cations slide while the electron sea holds them. |
| Alloy | A mixture of a metal with one or more other elements, with metallic properties. |
| Substitutional alloy | Atoms of similar size replace host atoms in the lattice (brass: Zn replaces some Cu). |
| Interstitial alloy | Small atoms fit into the gaps between host atoms (steel: C in Fe). |
| Lewis diagram | A drawing showing all valence electrons of a molecule or ion as bonding pairs (lines) and lone pairs (dots). |
| Octet rule | Main-group atoms tend to be surrounded by eight valence electrons; hydrogen by two. |
| Lone pair | A pair of valence electrons on one atom not involved in bonding. |
| Expanded octet | More than eight electrons around a central atom of period 3 or lower (PCl₅, SF₆). |

## Ionic solids: a lattice, not molecules (2.3)

Sodium chloride is not a collection of NaCl molecules. It is a lattice in which every Na⁺ is surrounded by six Cl⁻ and every Cl⁻ by six Na⁺, extending in all directions. The formula NaCl is the ratio of ions, an empirical formula. The same is true of every ionic compound: CaF₂ means two fluoride ions for each calcium ion throughout the crystal.

The lattice model explains the properties that every AP question about ionic solids asks for.

- **High melting points.** Melting means giving ions enough energy to leave their lattice positions and move. Every ion is attracted to several neighbours, and by Coulomb's law those attractions are strong, so a lot of energy is needed: NaCl melts at 801 °C, MgO at 2825 °C. The lattice energy ranking of week 4 (charge first, then size) is the melting-point ranking.
- **Brittle.** Strike the crystal and a layer of ions shifts by one position. Suddenly cations sit beside cations and anions beside anions; the repulsion splits the crystal along that plane. Metals do not do this because their cations are all alike and the electron sea moves with them.
- **Do not conduct as solids; conduct when molten or dissolved.** Conduction needs mobile charge carriers. In the solid the ions are fixed and the electrons are localised on the ions. In the melt or in solution the ions themselves move and carry charge.
- **Hard but not malleable.** Strong attractions resist deformation; any deformation large enough to move ions leads to fracture.

**Worked example 1.** Explain why a crystal of NaCl shatters when struck with a hammer while a piece of copper simply dents.

In NaCl the blow slides a layer of ions by one ion spacing, putting Na⁺ next to Na⁺ and Cl⁻ next to Cl⁻; the like charges repel strongly and the crystal cleaves. In copper every lattice site holds an identical Cu²⁺-like cation, and the delocalised electrons re-adjust instantly, so the shifted layer is attracted exactly as before and the metal just changes shape.

**Worked example 2.** Which has the higher melting point, KCl or CaS? Both have the same lattice type.

CaS: 2+ and 2− ions give a charge product four times that of KCl's 1+ and 1−, and the ions are not larger (Ca²⁺ 100 pm, S²⁻ 184 pm against K⁺ 138 pm, Cl⁻ 181 pm). Much larger lattice energy, much higher melting point (CaS about 2525 °C; KCl 771 °C).

**Try it.**
1. Why does solid MgCl₂ not conduct electricity but molten MgCl₂ does?
2. Rank by melting point: NaF, NaBr, MgO.

*Answers:* (1) In the solid the Mg²⁺ and Cl⁻ ions are fixed in the lattice; in the melt they are free to move and carry charge. (2) NaBr < NaF < MgO: same charges for the sodium salts, with the smaller F⁻ making NaF stronger; MgO's doubled charges put it far above both.

## Metals and alloys (2.4)

A metal is a lattice of cations with the valence electrons delocalised over all of them. Three consequences:

- **Conductivity** (electrical and thermal): the electrons are free to move and to carry energy.
- **Malleability and ductility:** cation layers slide without breaking the bonding, because every site is equivalent and the electron sea follows.
- **Lustre:** the mobile electrons absorb and re-emit light across the visible range.

An **alloy** is a solid mixture of a metal with other elements. Alloying changes properties on purpose.

**Substitutional alloys** form when the added atoms are similar in size to the host and take some of its lattice positions. Brass is copper with up to about 40% zinc (atomic radii 128 pm and 134 pm); bronze is copper with tin; sterling silver is silver with 7.5% copper. The substituted atoms disrupt the regularity of the lattice, which makes it harder for layers to slide: the alloy is harder and stronger than the pure metal, often at the cost of some conductivity.

**Interstitial alloys** form when much smaller atoms fit into the spaces between host atoms. Steel is iron with carbon (radii 126 pm and 77 pm) in the interstices. The small atoms pin the layers in place, so steel is far harder and less malleable than pure iron. Many alloys are both: stainless steel has substitutional chromium and nickel plus interstitial carbon.

**Worked example 3.** Predict whether an alloy of copper and nickel (radii 128 and 124 pm) is substitutional or interstitial, and how its hardness compares with pure copper.

The radii are nearly equal, so nickel atoms replace copper atoms: substitutional (cupronickel, used in coins). The differently sized atoms and the different nuclear charges disturb the lattice, so layers slide less easily: harder than pure copper.

**Try it.**
1. Why is an alloy usually harder than the pure metal?
2. Classify the alloy of iron with 1% carbon.

*Answers:* (1) Foreign atoms of a different size interrupt the regular planes of cations, so layers cannot slide past each other as freely. (2) Interstitial: carbon atoms are small enough to sit in the gaps of the iron lattice.

## Lewis diagrams (2.5)

A Lewis diagram shows every valence electron of a molecule or ion. The routine:

1. **Count valence electrons**: add the group-number count for every atom; add one for each negative charge, subtract one for each positive charge.
2. **Choose the central atom**: usually the least electronegative atom that is not hydrogen (carbon is almost always central; hydrogen and the halogens are usually terminal).
3. **Connect** the terminal atoms to the centre with single bonds (2 electrons each).
4. **Complete the octets of the terminal atoms** with lone pairs (hydrogen needs only 2).
5. **Put any remaining electrons on the central atom.**
6. **If the central atom lacks an octet,** move lone pairs from terminal atoms in to make double or triple bonds.
7. **For an ion,** enclose the diagram in brackets with the charge outside.

**Worked example 4.** CO₂.

Valence electrons: 4 + 2(6) = 16. C central, two C–O single bonds use 4; three lone pairs on each O use 12; total 16, none left for carbon, which has only 4. Move one lone pair from each oxygen into a bond: O=C=O, each oxygen with two lone pairs. Carbon now has 8, each oxygen 8, total still 16.

**Worked example 5.** NH₄⁺ and SO₄²⁻.

NH₄⁺: 5 + 4(1) − 1 = 8 electrons: four N–H bonds use all 8; nitrogen has an octet, each hydrogen a duet. Brackets with a + charge.

SO₄²⁻: 6 + 4(6) + 2 = 32 electrons: four S–O single bonds use 8; three lone pairs on each O use 24; total 32. Every atom has an octet. (Some textbooks draw S=O double bonds to lower formal charges; week 7 discusses that choice. The all-single-bond diagram obeys the octet rule and is accepted.)

**Worked example 6.** HCN.

1 + 4 + 5 = 10 electrons. C central: H–C–N uses 4; three lone pairs on N use 6; total 10, carbon has only 4. Move two lone pairs from N into the bond: H–C≡N with one lone pair on N. Carbon 8, nitrogen 8, hydrogen 2.

**Exceptions you must recognise.**
- **Fewer than eight:** BF₃ (boron has 6) and BeCl₂ (beryllium has 4) are accepted as written; boron and beryllium do not need octets.
- **Odd electrons:** NO (11 electrons) and NO₂ (17) cannot give everyone an octet; one atom keeps an unpaired electron (a radical).
- **Expanded octets:** central atoms from period 3 downward can hold more than eight because they have d orbitals available: PCl₅ (10 around P), SF₆ (12 around S), XeF₄ (12 around Xe). Only use an expanded octet when the electron count forces it or when extra electrons must go on a period-3 centre.

**Try it.** Draw Lewis diagrams for (1) H₂O, (2) N₂, (3) CO₃²⁻ (one diagram), (4) PCl₅.

*Answers:* (1) 8 electrons: H–O–H with two lone pairs on O. (2) 10 electrons: N≡N with one lone pair on each N. (3) 24 electrons: C central with one C=O and two C–O; the double-bonded O has two lone pairs, each single-bonded O has three; brackets, 2− (resonance next week). (4) 40 electrons: five P–Cl bonds (10 electrons), three lone pairs on each Cl (30); phosphorus has 10 electrons, an expanded octet.

## Common mistakes

- Treating NaCl as a molecule; it is a lattice and its formula is a ratio.
- Explaining brittleness with "ionic bonds are weak"; they are strong, and the cause is like-charge repulsion after a shift.
- Saying solid ionic compounds conduct because they contain ions; the ions must be free to move.
- Forgetting to add electrons for a negative charge or subtract for a positive charge when counting.
- Giving hydrogen more than two electrons, or putting hydrogen in the centre.
- Leaving the central atom with fewer than eight electrons when a multiple bond could fix it (CO₂, HCN).
- Expanding the octet of a period-2 atom (N, O, F can never have more than eight).

## Self-check

1. Explain two properties of ionic solids using the lattice model.
2. Compare the electrical conductivity of copper, solid NaCl and molten NaCl, with reasons.
3. Distinguish substitutional and interstitial alloys and give an example of each.
4. Count the valence electrons in PO₄³⁻ and in NO₂⁺.
5. Draw the Lewis diagram of SO₂ in text, and state which atom, if any, has an expanded octet.

*Answers:* (1) High melting point: many strong Coulombic attractions must be overcome; brittleness: a shifted layer brings like charges together. (2) Copper conducts (mobile delocalised electrons); solid NaCl does not (ions fixed, electrons localised); molten NaCl conducts (mobile ions). (3) Substitutional: similar-sized atoms replace host atoms (brass, Cu/Zn). Interstitial: small atoms occupy gaps (steel, Fe/C). (4) PO₄³⁻: 5 + 24 + 3 = 32. NO₂⁺: 5 + 12 − 1 = 16. (5) 18 electrons: O=S–O with one lone pair on S, two lone pairs on the double-bonded O and three on the single-bonded O (or the resonance partner); no atom exceeds an octet in this form, though S can.
