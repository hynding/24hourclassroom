## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Resonance structures | Two or more Lewis diagrams that differ only in the placement of electrons (not atoms); the molecule is their average. |
| Resonance hybrid | The actual structure: delocalised electrons, intermediate bond lengths, fractional bond orders. |
| Formal charge | (valence electrons of the free atom) − (lone-pair electrons) − ½(bonding electrons); a bookkeeping charge for one Lewis diagram. |
| Electron domain | A region of electron density around a central atom: a lone pair, or a single, double or triple bond (each bond counts once). |
| VSEPR | Valence-shell electron-pair repulsion: domains arrange themselves as far apart as possible. |
| Electron-domain geometry | The arrangement of all domains (linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal bipyramidal, octahedral). |
| Molecular geometry | The arrangement of the atoms only, after lone pairs are hidden (bent, trigonal pyramidal, see-saw, ...). |
| Bond dipole | The polarity of one bond, from δ+ to δ−. |
| Molecular polarity | Whether the bond dipoles add to a net dipole; depends on geometry. |
| Hybridization | Mixing of s and p orbitals on the central atom to match the domain geometry: sp (2 domains), sp² (3), sp³ (4). |
| σ bond / π bond | A bond with electron density along the internuclear axis (every single bond; the first bond of a multiple bond) / a bond with density above and below the axis (the second and third bonds of a double or triple bond). |

## Resonance (2.6)

Draw the Lewis diagram of ozone, O₃: 18 electrons, O central, O=O–O with lone pairs. But which oxygen gets the double bond? Either. The two diagrams are **resonance structures**, and experiment shows the truth is neither: both O–O bonds in ozone are the same length, 128 pm, between a single (148 pm) and a double (121 pm). The real molecule is the **resonance hybrid**, with the π electrons delocalised over all three atoms.

Rules of resonance:

- Resonance structures differ only in electron placement; the atoms never move.
- The molecule does not flip between structures; it is a single average structure at all times.
- Equivalent resonance structures contribute equally and give identical bonds. The bond order is (total bonds across all structures) ÷ (number of equivalent bond positions).

**Worked example 1.** Nitrate, NO₃⁻.

24 electrons: N central with one N=O and two N–O, bracketed 1−. Three equivalent structures, one with the double bond on each oxygen. Bond order = (2 + 1 + 1) ÷ 3 = 1.33 for each N–O bond. All three bonds are 124 pm, between N–O (136 pm) and N=O (115 pm).

**Worked example 2.** Carbonate, CO₃²⁻ and benzene, C₆H₆.

Carbonate is the nitrate case again: three structures, bond order 1.33. Benzene's ring has alternating double bonds in two equivalent structures, so every C–C bond has bond order 1.5 and length 140 pm, between 154 and 134 pm.

**Try it.**
1. How many equivalent resonance structures does SO₃ have (octet form), and what is each S–O bond order?
2. Explain why the two C–O bond lengths in acetate, CH₃COO⁻, are equal, while those in acetic acid, CH₃COOH, are not.

*Answers:* (1) Three; bond order 1.33. (2) Acetate's two oxygens are equivalent, with two resonance structures placing the double bond on either, so the hybrid has two identical bonds (bond order 1.5). In acetic acid one oxygen carries a hydrogen, so the structures are not equivalent and the C=O (123 pm) and C–OH (136 pm) bonds stay distinct.

## Formal charge (2.6)

When resonance structures are **not** equivalent, formal charge tells you which one matters most.

formal charge = valence electrons − lone-pair electrons − ½(shared electrons)

(or: valence electrons − dots − lines, since each line is one bond counted once). The formal charges in any structure sum to the overall charge.

Choosing the best structure:
1. Fewest nonzero formal charges.
2. Negative formal charge on the most electronegative atom.
3. Avoid like charges on adjacent atoms.

**Worked example 3.** Thiocyanate, SCN⁻ (16 electrons, C central). Three structures:

- S=C=N: S 6 − 4 − 2 = 0; C 4 − 0 − 4 = 0; N 5 − 4 − 2 = −1.
- S≡C–N: S 6 − 2 − 3 = +1; C 0; N 5 − 6 − 1 = −2.
- S–C≡N: S 6 − 6 − 1 = −1; C 0; N 5 − 2 − 3 = 0.

Structure 2 has large separated charges and is negligible. Structures 1 and 3 both have a single −1; structure 1 puts it on nitrogen (3.04) and structure 3 on sulfur (2.58). Nitrogen is more electronegative, so structure 1 is the major contributor, though structure 3 contributes too; the real ion has a C–N bond order between 2 and 3 and a C–S bond order between 1 and 2.

**Worked example 4.** Which Lewis diagram of CO₂ is best: O=C=O, or O≡C–O?

O=C=O: every formal charge 0. O≡C–O: triple-bonded O is +1, single-bonded O is −1. The first wins on rule 1; the second is a minor contributor.

**Worked example 5.** Sulfate: octet structure versus expanded-octet structure.

With four S–O single bonds, sulfur is 6 − 0 − 4 = +2 and each oxygen 6 − 6 − 1 = −1 (sum −2 ✓). With two S=O and two S–O (12 electrons on S), sulfur is 0, the double-bonded oxygens 0, the single-bonded ones −1 (sum −2 ✓). The expanded-octet form has fewer formal charges and is often drawn; both are accepted on the exam. If asked to justify a choice, cite formal charge.

**Try it.** Calculate the formal charges in the Lewis diagram of NH₄⁺ and in the diagram of ozone (O=O–O).

*Answers:* NH₄⁺: N 5 − 0 − 4 = +1, each H 1 − 0 − 1 = 0, sum +1. Ozone: terminal double-bonded O 6 − 4 − 2 = 0; central O 6 − 2 − 3 = +1; terminal single-bonded O 6 − 6 − 1 = −1; sum 0.

## VSEPR: from Lewis diagram to shape (2.7)

Electron domains around a central atom repel one another and spread out as far as possible. Count the domains (lone pairs plus bonded atoms, each multiple bond counting once), then:

| Domains | Electron-domain geometry | Lone pairs | Molecular geometry | Angle | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | linear | 0 | linear | 180° | CO₂, BeCl₂ |
| 3 | trigonal planar | 0 | trigonal planar | 120° | BF₃, CO₃²⁻ |
| 3 | trigonal planar | 1 | bent | < 120° | SO₂, O₃ |
| 4 | tetrahedral | 0 | tetrahedral | 109.5° | CH₄, NH₄⁺ |
| 4 | tetrahedral | 1 | trigonal pyramidal | 107° | NH₃ |
| 4 | tetrahedral | 2 | bent | 104.5° | H₂O |
| 5 | trigonal bipyramidal | 0 | trigonal bipyramidal | 90°, 120° | PCl₅ |
| 5 | trigonal bipyramidal | 1 | see-saw | | SF₄ |
| 5 | trigonal bipyramidal | 2 | T-shaped | | ClF₃ |
| 5 | trigonal bipyramidal | 3 | linear | 180° | XeF₂, I₃⁻ |
| 6 | octahedral | 0 | octahedral | 90° | SF₆ |
| 6 | octahedral | 1 | square pyramidal | | BrF₅ |
| 6 | octahedral | 2 | square planar | 90° | XeF₄ |

Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs (they are held by one nucleus and spread wider), so they squeeze bond angles below the ideal: 109.5° → 107° in NH₃ → 104.5° in H₂O. In the trigonal bipyramid, lone pairs always take the roomier equatorial positions.

**Worked example 6.** Predict the shapes of SO₂, ClF₃ and XeF₄.

- SO₂: 3 domains (two bonds, one lone pair): bent, about 119°.
- ClF₃: 28 electrons; Cl has three bonds and two lone pairs, 5 domains: T-shaped (lone pairs equatorial).
- XeF₄: 36 electrons; four bonds and two lone pairs, 6 domains: square planar (lone pairs opposite each other).

## Polarity (2.7)

A molecule is polar when its bond dipoles do not cancel. Two questions: are the bonds polar, and does the geometry cancel them?

- CO₂: polar bonds, linear, dipoles opposite and equal: **nonpolar**.
- H₂O: polar bonds, bent: dipoles add: **polar**.
- CCl₄: polar bonds, tetrahedral with four identical atoms: cancel: **nonpolar**. CHCl₃: same shape, but one bond differs: **polar**.
- NH₃: trigonal pyramidal: **polar**. BF₃: trigonal planar, identical atoms: **nonpolar**.
- XeF₄ square planar: nonpolar; SF₄ see-saw: polar.

Rule of thumb: a symmetric shape (linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal bipyramidal, octahedral, square planar) with all terminal atoms identical and no lone pairs on the centre (square planar excepted) is nonpolar; anything else with polar bonds is polar.

## Hybridization and σ/π bonds (2.7)

The AP model: the central atom mixes its s and p orbitals to make a set of equivalent hybrid orbitals, one per electron domain.

| Domains | Hybridization | Geometry |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | sp | linear |
| 3 | sp² | trigonal planar |
| 4 | sp³ | tetrahedral |

(The CED does not require d-orbital hybridizations for 5 and 6 domains.) Every bond contains one σ bond; a double bond is one σ plus one π; a triple bond is one σ plus two π. The π bonds form from the unhybridised p orbitals left over: sp² leaves one p for one π bond, sp leaves two p orbitals for two π bonds.

**Worked example 7.** Count σ and π bonds and assign hybridization in ethene (H₂C=CH₂) and in HCN.

Ethene: four C–H σ bonds plus one C–C σ and one C–C π: 5 σ, 1 π; each carbon has 3 domains: sp². HCN: H–C σ, C–N σ plus two π: 2 σ, 2 π; carbon has 2 domains: sp; nitrogen (one bond, one lone pair) also sp.

**Try it.**
1. Give the geometry, bond angle, polarity and hybridization of the central atom in NH₃, BF₃ and H₂O.
2. How many σ and π bonds are in CO₂? What is carbon's hybridization?

*Answers:* (1) NH₃: trigonal pyramidal, 107°, polar, sp³. BF₃: trigonal planar, 120°, nonpolar, sp². H₂O: bent, 104.5°, polar, sp³. (2) 2 σ and 2 π; sp.

## Common mistakes

- Moving atoms between resonance structures; only electrons move.
- Treating a resonance hybrid as a molecule that switches back and forth.
- Confusing formal charge with oxidation number, or forgetting that formal charges must sum to the ion's charge.
- Counting a double bond as two electron domains; it is one.
- Giving the electron-domain geometry when the molecular geometry is asked (H₂O is bent, not tetrahedral).
- Calling a molecule nonpolar because its bonds are "balanced" without checking for lone pairs on the central atom.
- Assigning hybridization from the number of bonded atoms instead of the number of domains (NH₃ is sp³, not sp²).

2. Calculate the formal charges in O≡C–O (a minor structure of CO₂) and explain why it is minor.
3. Predict the molecular geometry and approximate bond angle of PCl₃ and of SF₄.
4. Which of CH₂Cl₂, CS₂ and PF₃ are polar?
5. State the hybridization of each carbon in propyne, HC≡C–CH₃, and count the π bonds.
