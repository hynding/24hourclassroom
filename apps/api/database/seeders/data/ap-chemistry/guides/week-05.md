## What you need to be able to do

- **2.1** Classify a bond as ionic, polar covalent, nonpolar covalent or metallic from the electronegativities and positions of the atoms, and explain that bonding is a continuum rather than three boxes.
- **2.1** Explain that electrons in a polar covalent bond are shared unequally, producing partial charges, and that the polarity grows with the electronegativity difference.
- **2.2** Read a potential-energy diagram of two atoms: identify the bond length as the distance at the energy minimum and the bond energy as the depth of the well; explain the shape in terms of attraction and repulsion.
- **2.2** Relate bond order, bond length and bond energy: more shared pairs mean a shorter, stronger bond; larger atoms mean a longer, weaker bond.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ionic bond | Electrostatic attraction between cations and anions, formed by electron transfer between a metal and a nonmetal (large electronegativity difference). |
| Covalent bond | A shared pair of electrons between two nonmetal atoms. |
| Nonpolar covalent | Shared equally (electronegativity difference near zero): H₂, Cl₂, C–H to a good approximation. |
| Polar covalent | Shared unequally; the more electronegative atom carries a partial negative charge (δ−), the other δ+. |
| Metallic bond | Cations in a sea of delocalised valence electrons; explains conductivity and malleability. |
| Electronegativity difference (ΔEN) | The guide to bond type: near 0 nonpolar; up to about 1.7 polar covalent; above about 1.7 mostly ionic (with exceptions). |
| Bond length | The internuclear distance at the minimum of the potential-energy curve. |
| Bond energy | The energy needed to separate two bonded atoms to infinite distance; the depth of the well, in kJ/mol. |
| Bond order | The number of shared electron pairs: 1 for single, 2 for double, 3 for triple. |
| Potential-energy diagram | A plot of the energy of two atoms against the distance between their nuclei. |

## Three kinds of bond, one continuum (2.1)

Every bond is an electrostatic interaction. What differs is where the electrons end up.

**Ionic.** When a metal of low ionization energy meets a nonmetal of high electronegativity, electrons transfer, and the resulting ions attract in a lattice. NaCl: ΔEN = 3.16 − 0.93 = 2.23. The bond is the Coulombic attraction among many ions, not a pair of atoms.

**Covalent.** Two nonmetals, both wanting electrons, share pairs. If the two atoms are identical (H₂, Cl₂, O₂) or nearly so, the sharing is equal: **nonpolar**. If one atom is more electronegative, the pair spends more time near it: **polar**, with partial charges δ− and δ+. HCl: ΔEN = 3.16 − 2.20 = 0.96, polar; H–Cl has a dipole with the chlorine end negative.

**Metallic.** Metal atoms have few valence electrons and low ionization energies; in a solid those electrons are delocalised over the whole lattice of cations. The "electron sea" model explains why metals conduct electricity, conduct heat and bend without shattering (the cations can slide past one another while the sea keeps them bound).

The CED stresses that these are regions on a continuum, and electronegativity difference is the guide, not a law. A good rule: ΔEN below about 0.4 is nonpolar, 0.4 to about 1.7 polar covalent, above 1.7 ionic, with the important caution that metal-plus-nonmetal is the better test for ionic bonding than any cutoff. HF has ΔEN = 1.78 and is covalent (both nonmetals); it is just very polar.

**Worked example 1.** Classify the bonding in Cl₂, BrCl, NaBr, CaO and brass (Cu–Zn).

- Cl₂: two identical nonmetals, ΔEN = 0: nonpolar covalent.
- BrCl: ΔEN = 3.16 − 2.96 = 0.20, two nonmetals: essentially nonpolar covalent (very weakly polar).
- NaBr: metal and nonmetal, ΔEN = 2.03: ionic.
- CaO: metal and nonmetal, ΔEN = 2.44: ionic.
- Brass: two metals: metallic bonding (an alloy; more in week 6).

**Worked example 2.** Arrange the bonds C–H, O–H, N–H and C–F in order of increasing polarity.

ΔEN: C–H 2.55 − 2.20 = 0.35; N–H 3.04 − 2.20 = 0.84; O–H 3.44 − 2.20 = 1.24; C–F 3.98 − 2.55 = 1.43. Order: C–H < N–H < O–H < C–F. The partial negative charge sits on the more electronegative atom in each case (H is δ+ in all three X–H bonds; F is δ− in C–F).

**Try it.**
1. Which is more polar, H–Br or H–I? Where is the δ− end?
2. Explain why MgCl₂ is ionic but PCl₃ is covalent, even though both contain chlorine.
3. Why does a lump of copper conduct electricity while a lump of NaCl does not?

*Answers:* (1) H–Br (ΔEN 0.76 against 0.46); the δ− end is on Br. (2) Mg is a metal with a low ionization energy and ΔEN with Cl of 1.85, so electrons transfer; P is a nonmetal close to Cl in electronegativity (ΔEN 0.97) and shares electrons. (3) Copper's valence electrons are delocalised and free to move through the lattice; NaCl's electrons are localised on ions fixed in place (molten NaCl conducts because the ions can move).

## The potential-energy curve (2.2)

Imagine two hydrogen atoms far apart; define their energy as zero. Bring them together:

- At large distances the electron of each atom is weakly attracted to the other nucleus, and the energy falls slowly below zero.
- As they approach, the attraction (electrons to both nuclei) grows and the energy drops steeply.
- Below a certain distance the two nuclei repel each other strongly, and the two electron clouds repel too; the energy shoots upward.
- The balance point, the **minimum** of the curve, is the bond: the distance there is the **bond length** and the depth of the well below zero is the **bond energy**.

Graph description: potential energy (kJ/mol) on the y-axis, internuclear distance (pm) on the x-axis. The curve starts at zero at far right, dips to a minimum of −436 kJ/mol at 74 pm, then rises steeply toward positive values as the distance approaches zero.

For H₂ the minimum is at 74 pm and −436 kJ/mol. Breaking the bond means climbing out of the well: it takes 436 kJ to separate one mole of H₂ into atoms. Forming the bond releases the same amount. That is why bond breaking is always endothermic and bond forming always exothermic, a fact you will use in Unit 6.

**Worked example 3.** A potential-energy diagram for Cl₂ has its minimum at 199 pm and −243 kJ/mol. Compare Cl₂ with H₂.

Cl₂ has a longer bond (199 against 74 pm) because chlorine atoms are much larger, and a weaker bond (243 against 436 kJ/mol) because the shared pair is farther from each nucleus and the many inner electrons repel. Longer usually means weaker for bonds of the same order.

**Worked example 4.** Which diagram belongs to N₂ and which to O₂, given minima at (110 pm, −945 kJ/mol) and (121 pm, −498 kJ/mol)?

N₂ has a triple bond; O₂ a double. More shared pairs pull the nuclei closer and bind them more strongly, so the deeper, closer well (110 pm, −945 kJ/mol) is N₂. Bond order, bond length and bond energy move together: order up, length down, energy up.

| Bond | Order | Length (pm) | Energy (kJ/mol) |
|---|---|---|---|
| C–C | 1 | 154 | 348 |
| C=C | 2 | 134 | 614 |
| C≡C | 3 | 120 | 839 |
| H–F | 1 | 92 | 567 |
| H–Cl | 1 | 127 | 432 |
| H–Br | 1 | 141 | 366 |
| H–I | 1 | 161 | 299 |

Notice that a double bond is less than twice as strong as a single bond: the second shared pair (a π bond, week 7) is weaker than the first.

**Worked example 5.** How much energy is needed to break every bond in 0.250 mol of H₂?

436 kJ/mol × 0.250 mol = 109 kJ. (Week 1's mole doing its usual job.)

**Why the curve has that shape: Coulomb again.** The attraction term (electrons to nuclei) and the repulsion terms (nucleus–nucleus, electron–electron) depend on distance differently; the well is where the sum is lowest. Atoms with high effective nuclear charge hold shared electrons close (short, strong bonds: H–F); large atoms with diffuse valence shells form long, weak bonds (H–I). Higher bond order means more attracting electron density between the nuclei.

**Try it.**
1. On a potential-energy diagram, what does a point to the left of the minimum represent physically?
2. Predict which has the longer bond and which the larger bond energy: F₂ or I₂.
3. Rank by bond length: C–O, C=O, C≡O.

*Answers:* (1) The atoms are compressed closer than the bond length; nuclear repulsion dominates and the energy is high, so the atoms spring apart. (2) I₂ has the longer bond (iodine atoms are far larger); F₂ has the larger bond energy in principle (small atoms, short bond), though F₂'s bond (159 kJ/mol) is anomalously weak because lone-pair repulsion between the small, crowded fluorine atoms partly cancels the gain; I₂ is 151 kJ/mol. The reliable trend is Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂. (3) C≡O < C=O < C–O; higher order, shorter bond.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a bond ionic from ΔEN alone when both atoms are nonmetals (HF is covalent).
- Putting the δ− on the less electronegative atom; the partial negative charge sits on the atom that pulls harder.
- Reading the bond energy as the y-value at the minimum with its sign: the bond energy is the positive depth of the well (436 kJ/mol), and the minimum is drawn at −436.
- Saying bonds "store energy that is released when broken"; breaking a bond always costs energy.
- Assuming a double bond is exactly twice as strong as a single bond.
- Forgetting that bond length is set by atom size as well as bond order: C–I is longer than C–Cl even though both are single.

## Self-check

1. Classify the bonding in KF, NO, O₂ and iron, giving a reason for each.
2. Which bond is more polar, Si–O or C–O? (Si 1.90, C 2.55, O 3.44)
3. Describe the shape of a potential-energy diagram and label what the minimum represents.
4. The minimum for HF is at 92 pm and −567 kJ/mol; for HI at 161 pm and −299 kJ/mol. Explain both differences.
5. Calculate the energy needed to break the bonds in 2.00 g of H₂ (2.02 g/mol; 436 kJ/mol).

*Answers:* (1) KF ionic (metal + nonmetal, ΔEN 3.16); NO polar covalent (two nonmetals, ΔEN 0.40); O₂ nonpolar covalent (identical atoms); iron metallic (delocalised electrons among cations). (2) Si–O (ΔEN 1.54 against 0.89). (3) Energy falls from zero as atoms approach, reaches a minimum (bond length on x, −bond energy on y), then rises steeply from nuclear repulsion. (4) Iodine is much larger than fluorine, so the H–I bond is longer; the shared electrons are farther from the iodine nucleus and the bond is weaker. (5) 2.00 ÷ 2.02 = 0.990 mol; × 436 = 432 kJ.
