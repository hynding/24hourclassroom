## The ideas that generate every question

1. **Proton transfer.** A Brønsted–Lowry acid donates H⁺, a base accepts it; every reaction has two conjugate pairs that differ by one proton. Water does both: Kw = [H₃O⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C, rising with temperature (neutral means equal, not pH 7).
2. **Strong means complete; weak means equilibrium.** Six strong acids and the group 1 and heavy group 2 hydroxides: [H₃O⁺] or [OH⁻] from the formula (count hydroxides). Everything else is an ICE table with Ka or Kb and x = √(Kc), checked by the 5% rule, with the quadratic as fallback. Ka × Kb = Kw for a conjugate pair; weaker acid, stronger conjugate base.
3. **The pH of a mixture is decided by what is left after the stoichiometry.** Strong + strong: water and spectators (pH 7 at equivalence). Weak acid + strong base: conjugate base (basic). Weak base + strong acid: conjugate acid (acidic). Partial neutralisation of a weak species: a buffer.
4. **Titration curves are those cases in sequence.** Start (weak acid ICE or strong acid), buffer region (Henderson–Hasselbalch; pH = pKa at half-equivalence), equivalence (salt hydrolysis at the diluted concentration), excess titrant (strong reagent sets pH). Indicator: pKa(HIn) ± 1 on the steep part.
5. **Structure sets strength.** Binary acids: down a group weaker bond wins (HI > HF); across a period polarity wins (HF > H₂O > NH₃). Oxyacids: more oxygens or a more electronegative central atom, stronger. Carboxylates are resonance-stabilised; bases need a lone pair.
6. **pH relative to pKa says which form dominates.** log([A⁻]/[HA]) = pH − pKa: one unit is a factor of 10. That one line is the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation and the rule for indicators, drugs and titration species.
7. **A buffer is the common-ion effect with two reservoirs.** Base absorbs added acid, acid absorbs added base; capacity grows with concentration and is greatest at pH = pKa, useful within pKa ± 1.

## Reading the three kinds of picture

- **Titration curve:** the start tells strong (pH 1) from weak (pH 3); a flat shoulder is a buffer region and its midpoint is pKa; the jump's centre is the equivalence point (7 for strong–strong, above for weak acid, below for weak base); two jumps mean a diprotic acid; the indicator's range must sit inside the jump.
- **Particle diagram of an acid solution:** a strong acid shows only ions; a weak acid shows mostly intact molecules with a few ion pairs; a buffer shows both HA and A⁻ in comparable numbers with few H⁺.
- **Table of Ka values:** rank acids (larger Ka, stronger), rank conjugate bases the other way, pick a buffer pair by pKa near the target pH.

## Common errors

- Treating a weak acid as strong (0.10 M acetic acid is pH 2.87, not 1.00), or forgetting the two hydroxides of Ba(OH)₂.
- Using Ka in a base problem, or stopping at pOH.
- Dropping the x in the numerator, or never checking the 5% rule.
- Assuming equivalence is pH 7 for every titration, or using the undiluted concentration at equivalence.
- Reading pKa at equivalence instead of half-equivalence.
- Inverting the Henderson–Hasselbalch ratio; using initial amounts after a strong reagent has been added; applying it when one component is gone.
- Calling HF the strongest hydrogen halide acid, or ranking oxyacids by hydrogens instead of oxygens.
- Saying a buffer keeps pH constant or has infinite capacity; capacity is the moles of the reacting component.
- Confusing pKa (property of the acid) with pH (property of the solution).

## Worked example: one problem, every method

**The acid alone.** 0.100 M HNO₂ (Ka = 4.5 × 10⁻⁴, pKa 3.35): the approximation gives x = 6.7 × 10⁻³ (6.7%, too large); quadratic x² + 4.5 × 10⁻⁴x − 4.5 × 10⁻⁵ = 0 gives x = 6.5 × 10⁻³ M, pH **2.19**, 6.5% ionised.

**Its salt.** 0.100 M KNO₂: Kb = 2.2 × 10⁻¹¹; x = √(2.2 × 10⁻¹²) = 1.5 × 10⁻⁶ M OH⁻; pH **8.17**.

**Its buffer.** 0.100 M HNO₂ with 0.150 M KNO₂: pH = 3.35 + log(1.50) = **3.53**. Add 0.020 mol HCl per litre: NO₂⁻ 0.130, HNO₂ 0.120; pH = 3.35 + log(0.130/0.120) = **3.38**.

**Its titration.** 25.0 mL of 0.100 M HNO₂ with 0.100 M NaOH: equivalence at 25.0 mL; half-equivalence pH = **3.35**; at equivalence [NO₂⁻] = 0.0500 M, x = √(2.2 × 10⁻¹¹ × 0.0500) = 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ M, pH **8.02**; at 30.0 mL, excess OH⁻ = 0.50 × 10⁻³ mol/0.0550 L = 9.1 × 10⁻³ M, pH **11.96**. Indicator: phenolphthalein.

**Its structure.** HNO₂ is weaker than HNO₃ because nitric acid's extra oxygen withdraws more electron density from the O–H bond and spreads the anion's charge over three oxygens.

## The unit exam

Forty-two items, about 85 minutes, every topic from 8.1 to 8.10 plus review. Three stimulus sets (a table of Ka values, a weak base–strong acid titration curve, and a buffer being prepared and tested), stand-alone multiple choice, select-all, true/false, fill-in-the-blank and five numeric calculations (a strong-base pH, a weak-acid pH, a salt pH, an equivalence-point volume and a buffer pH after an addition), two short-answer questions (a weak-acid ICE calculation with Ka from pH; a buffer-capacity comparison) and one long free-response question that walks a weak acid through its titration curve. The equations sheet carries Kw, pH and pOH, Ka × Kb = Kw and the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation; Ka and Kb values will be given in the question.
