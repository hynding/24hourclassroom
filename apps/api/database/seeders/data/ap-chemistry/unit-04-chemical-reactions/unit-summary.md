## The ideas that generate every question

1. **A reaction rearranges atoms; it never creates or destroys them.** Balance with coefficients, never subscripts; check every element and the total charge. Evidence of a chemical change is a new substance: gas, precipitate, colour, large energy change.
2. **Physical versus chemical is about which interactions change.** Physical: only intermolecular or interparticle forces (melting, boiling, dissolving sugar, dissolving NaCl in the AP treatment). Chemical: bonds within particles break and form (combustion, precipitation, HCl dissolving to give H₃O⁺).
3. **Net ionic equations show only what changes.** Strong electrolytes (soluble ionic compounds, the six strong acids, the strong bases) are written as ions; weak acids and bases, molecular compounds, solids, gases and water as formulas. Cancel spectators; check charge balance. Strong + strong acid–base is always H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.
4. **The solubility rules decide precipitates.** Na⁺, K⁺, NH₄⁺, NO₃⁻ compounds always dissolve; halides except Ag⁺, Pb²⁺, Hg₂²⁺; sulfates except Ba²⁺, Sr²⁺, Pb²⁺; carbonates, phosphates, sulfides and hydroxides mostly do not, except with group 1 and NH₄⁺.
5. **Particulate diagrams are coefficients.** Count species before and after, reduce to the smallest ratio, conserve atoms; whatever is left unchanged was in excess.
6. **Stoichiometry is one road:** quantity → moles → mole ratio → moles → quantity. Into moles by mass ÷ M, by PV = nRT, or by molarity × litres. The limiting reactant has the smallest (moles ÷ coefficient); theoretical yield comes from it; percent yield = actual ÷ theoretical × 100 %.
7. **A titration is solution stoichiometry with a burette.** Moles of titrant = M × V; apply the mole ratio (2 : 1 for a diprotic acid); divide by the analyte volume. Equivalence point is stoichiometric; end point is the indicator. Error analysis: anything that inflates the recorded titrant volume (overshoot, air bubble, water-rinsed burette) raises the result; a water-rinsed pipette lowers it; a wet flask does nothing. NaOH is standardised against KHP.
8. **Three kinds of reaction by what is transferred:** proton (acid–base), electrons (redox), ions into a solid (precipitation). Pattern names still predict products: synthesis, decomposition, combustion, single replacement (needs the activity series; always redox), double replacement (needs a precipitate, gas or water; never redox).
9. **Brønsted–Lowry: acid donates, base accepts.** Conjugate pairs differ by one H⁺; water and HCO₃⁻ are amphoteric. Strong acids ionise completely (particulate: all ions); weak acids barely (mostly molecules). Strength is the fraction ionised, not the concentration, and it does not change how much base an acid consumes.
10. **Redox is tracked by oxidation numbers.** Element 0; ion = charge; F −1; group 1 +1; group 2 +2; H +1; O −2 (−1 in peroxides); sums to 0 or the ion charge. Oxidation raises the number (loss of e⁻); the species oxidised is the reducing agent, the species reduced is the oxidising agent. Half-reaction method in acid: atoms, then O with H₂O, H with H⁺, charge with e⁻, scale and add. Permanganate titrations are self-indicating and use the 5 : 1 ratio.

## Key terms and formulas

| Item | Use |
|---|---|
| Strong acids | HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄, HClO₄ — written as H⁺ + anion. |
| Strong bases | Group 1 hydroxides; Ca(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂, Ba(OH)₂ — written as ions. |
| n = m/M; n = PV/RT; n = M × V(L) | The three doors into moles. |
| Limiting reactant test | Smallest value of (moles ÷ coefficient). |
| Percent yield | actual ÷ theoretical × 100 %. |
| Titration | n(titrant) = M × V(L) → mole ratio → n(analyte) → ÷ V(analyte). |
| KHP | KHC₈H₄O₄, 204.22 g/mol, one acidic proton; the primary standard for NaOH. |
| Conjugate pair | Acid − H⁺ = conjugate base; base + H⁺ = conjugate acid. |
| MnO₄⁻ + 8 H⁺ + 5 e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4 H₂O | The reduction half-reaction every redox titration uses. |
| Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14 H⁺ + 6 e⁻ → 2 Cr³⁺ + 7 H₂O | Chromium +6 → +3; six electrons per dichromate. |

## Common errors

- Balancing by changing a formula.
- Splitting a weak acid, water, a solid or a gas into ions; or writing a strong acid as a molecule.
- Cancelling an ion that changes state (aq → s) as if it were a spectator.
- Deciding the limiting reactant from grams, or from moles without dividing by coefficients.
- Computing theoretical yield from the excess reactant.
- Using 22.4 L/mol away from STP; forgetting mL → L.
- Treating a wet flask as an error, or saying overshooting makes the result too low.
- Forgetting the 2 : 1 ratio for H₂SO₄ or Ba(OH)₂, and the 5 : 1 ratio for MnO₄⁻/Fe²⁺.
- Confusing acid strength with concentration.
- Naming the species oxidised as the oxidising agent.
- Balancing O and H before the other elements, or omitting the electrons.

## Worked example: a reaction FRQ across the unit

A student drops 0.243 g of magnesium ribbon into 50.0 mL of 0.500 M hydrochloric acid and collects the gas.

(a) Equation and type: Mg(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g); single replacement, redox (Mg 0 → +2, H +1 → 0; Mg is the reducing agent, H⁺ the oxidising agent).
(b) Net ionic: Mg(s) + 2 H⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + H₂(g); Cl⁻ is the spectator.
(c) Limiting reactant: Mg 0.0100 mol ÷ 1 = 0.0100; HCl 0.0250 mol ÷ 2 = 0.0125. Magnesium limits.
(d) Gas: 0.0100 mol H₂; at 298 K and 1.00 atm, V = (0.0100)(0.08206)(298) = 0.245 L.
(e) Solution afterwards: 0.0250 − 0.0200 = 0.0050 mol H⁺ left in 0.0500 L → 0.100 M H⁺; [Mg²⁺] = 0.200 M; [Cl⁻] = 0.500 M (spectator, unchanged).
(f) The leftover acid is titrated with 0.100 M NaOH: 0.0050 mol H⁺ needs 50.0 mL. Net ionic: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O; at the equivalence point the flask holds Na⁺, Cl⁻ and Mg²⁺ with essentially no H₃O⁺ or OH⁻.
(g) Particulate diagram before reaction: Mg atoms in a metallic lattice, separate hydrated H₃O⁺ and Cl⁻ (1 : 1); after: hydrated Mg²⁺ (water oxygens inward), Cl⁻ unchanged, fewer H₃O⁺, H₂ molecules escaping.

## What to review from earlier units

Molarity, dilution and ion concentrations (3.7) run through every solution calculation; solubility (3.10) is the reason behind the precipitation rules; PV = nRT (3.4) converts gas volumes; the mole and empirical formulas (1.1–1.3) are the first step of every stoichiometry chain; electronegativity (2.1) sets oxidation numbers and ionization energy (1.7) explains which metals reduce.

## How the exam will ask it

Expect: a net ionic equation to write with spectators named; a particulate diagram to judge or draw; a limiting-reactant calculation with percent yield; a titration calculation with one procedural error to analyse; a classification of three reactions with justification; a conjugate-pair question; oxidation numbers and the agents; a half-reaction to balance in acid; and a redox titration. Every justification should name the species, the transfer (proton, electrons, ions) and the consequence.
