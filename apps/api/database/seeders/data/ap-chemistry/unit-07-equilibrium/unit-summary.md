## The ideas that generate every question

1. **Equilibrium is dynamic.** Forward and reverse rates are equal, so concentrations are constant while both reactions continue. Flat lines on a concentration–time graph mark it; equal, not zero, rates define it. The same equilibrium is reached from either side.
2. **Q compared with K decides everything.** Q uses the concentrations you have now; K is Q at equilibrium. Q < K: forward. Q > K: reverse. Q = K: no net change. Every Le Châtelier prediction is a special case: concentration and volume changes move Q, a temperature change moves K.
3. **K comes from the balanced equation.** Products over reactants, coefficients as exponents, solids and pure liquids left out, water omitted as the solvent. Kp = Kc(RT)^Δn. Reverse → 1/K; multiply by n → Kⁿ; add → multiply. Only temperature changes K.
4. **The ICE table is one tool used two ways.** From data to K: fill in what was measured, use stoichiometry for the rest. From K to concentrations: define x, write K in terms of x, then either drop x against a large initial concentration (small K; check the 5% rule) or solve the quadratic (K not small). Coefficient 2 means 2x and a square.
5. **Size of K means composition, never speed.** K ≫ 1 products, K ≪ 1 reactants; neither says anything about how fast.
6. **Ksp is K for dissolving.** Ksp = s² (1:1), 4s³ (1:2 or 2:1), 27s⁴ (1:3). Precipitate if Q > Ksp. A common ion lowers solubility (fix its concentration, keep its exponent); acid dissolves salts of basic anions by removing the anion; Ksp itself changes only with temperature.
7. **ΔG° = −RT ln K.** A tiny Ksp means a large positive ΔG° of dissolution; the sign comes from lattice-versus-hydration enthalpy and the entropy of ions against the ordering of water.

|---|---|---|
| Equilibrium constant | Kc = [C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ/([A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ) | solids, pure liquids and solvent omitted |
| Reaction quotient | Q, same form, any concentrations | compare with K for direction |
| Kp and Kc | Kp = Kc(RT)^Δn | Δn = gas moles (products − reactants); R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K) |
| Reverse, scale, add | 1/K; Kⁿ; K₁ × K₂ | ΔH adds, K multiplies |
| Small-x approximation | [A]₀ − x ≈ [A]₀ | valid if x/[A]₀ < 5% |
| Quadratic | x = [−b ± √(b² − 4ac)]/(2a) | keep the root with positive concentrations |
| Volume change | halving V doubles every concentration | Q × 2^(Δn); no change if Δn = 0 |
| Ksp, 1:1 salt | Ksp = s² | AgCl 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰, s = 1.3 × 10⁻⁵ M |
| Ksp, 1:2 or 2:1 salt | Ksp = 4s³ | Ca(OH)₂, PbI₂, Ag₂CrO₄ |
| Common ion | Ksp = s(c)ⁿ with c fixed | exponent n kept; s dropped from (c + ns) |
| Hydroxide at buffered pH | [OH⁻] = 10^(−(14 − pH)); s = Ksp/[OH⁻]ⁿ | only the metal ion is s |
| Precipitation | Q > Ksp → precipitate | dilute each ion on mixing first |
| Free energy of dissolution | ΔG° = −RT ln Ksp | R = 8.314 J/(mol·K); T in K; J → kJ |
| Temperature and K | ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS° | endothermic: K rises with T |

## Reading the three kinds of picture

- **Concentration–time graph:** curves flatten at equilibrium; the ratio of plateaus (coefficients as exponents) is K; the changes from start to plateau are in the stoichiometric ratio; a vertical jump in one curve is a disturbance, and the new plateaus that follow show the shift.
- **Rate–time graph:** forward rate falls, reverse rate rises, they meet and run together at equilibrium.
- **Particle boxes:** count, treat counts as concentrations, compute Q for each box; the box with Q = K is the one at equilibrium, whatever it looks like.

## Common errors

- Writing solids or water into K; leaving coefficients out of the exponents; using rate-law orders as exponents.
- Saying equilibrium means equal concentrations, or that the reactions stop.
- Dropping x from the product side, or dropping the exponent along with the x in a common-ion problem.
- Using the approximation when K is not small, or not checking the 5% rule.
- Reversing without reciprocating; halving without the square root; adding K values instead of multiplying.
- Claiming a catalyst or an inert gas at constant volume shifts an equilibrium, or that a temperature change changes Q rather than K.
- Shifting toward "fewer moles" when Δn(gas) = 0, or counting solids as gas moles.
- Comparing Ksp values of salts of different formula types as if they were solubilities.
- Setting [OH⁻] = 2s in a buffered solution; forgetting to halve concentrations when equal volumes are mixed.
- Reading a positive ΔG° as "cannot happen at all" rather than K < 1.

## Worked example: one problem, every method

**From data to K.** For N₂O₄(g) ⇌ 2 NO₂(g) at 298 K, 0.100 M N₂O₄ reaches equilibrium with [N₂O₄] = 0.071 M. Change: −0.029 M, so [NO₂] = 2(0.029) = 0.057 M. Kc = (0.057)²/0.071 = **0.046**. Kp = Kc(RT)^Δn with Δn = +1: 0.046 × 0.08206 × 298 = **1.1**.

**From K to concentrations.** Start with 0.400 M N₂O₄. (2x)²/(0.400 − x) = 0.046 → 4x² + 0.046x − 0.0184 = 0 → x = 0.0623 M (the approximation would give x = 0.068, 17% of 0.400, so the quadratic was required). [NO₂] = **0.125 M**, [N₂O₄] = 0.338 M.

**Disturbance.** Halve the volume of the first mixture: concentrations double, Q = (0.114)²/0.142 = 0.092 > 0.046, so NO₂ recombines (toward fewer gas moles); the gas darkens then lightens. Warm it: K rises (endothermic), the forward reaction proceeds, the gas darkens and stays dark.

**Solubility.** Mg(OH)₂, Ksp = 5.6 × 10⁻¹². Pure water: s = ∛(Ksp/4) = 1.1 × 10⁻⁴ M, pH 10.35. At pH 13.00: s = 5.6 × 10⁻¹²/(0.10)² = 5.6 × 10⁻¹⁰ M. In HCl: OH⁻ is removed, Q < Ksp, it dissolves. ΔG° = −(8.314)(298) ln(5.6 × 10⁻¹²) = **+64.2 kJ/mol**.

## The unit exam

Forty-two items, about 85 minutes, every topic from 7.1 to 7.14 plus review. Three stimulus sets (a concentration–time graph for the water-gas shift reaction, a set of particle boxes for a dimerisation, and a table of Ksp values), stand-alone multiple choice, select-all, true/false, fill-in-the-blank and five numeric calculations (K from equilibrium data, Kp from Kc, an equilibrium concentration by the small-x approximation, a common-ion solubility and a free energy of dissolution), two short-answer questions (a disturbance analysed with Q and K; a precipitation decision) and one long free-response question built around an ICE calculation, a disturbance and a temperature change for the same gas-phase system. The equations sheet carries the K expression, Kp = Kc(RT)^Δn, ΔG° = −RT ln K and the gas constant in both sets of units; Ksp values will be given in the question.
