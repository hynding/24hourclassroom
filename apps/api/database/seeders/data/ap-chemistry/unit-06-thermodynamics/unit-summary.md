## The ideas that generate every question

1. **Sign from the system's side.** Exothermic: the system releases heat, q < 0, ΔH < 0, surroundings warm, products lower in energy. Endothermic: the opposite. Breaking bonds or attractions always costs energy; forming them always releases it; ΔH is the net.
2. **Heat is not temperature.** Heat is energy transferred (J); temperature measures average kinetic energy. Heat flows hot to cold until thermal equilibrium; q = mcΔT says how far each temperature moves, and q_lost + q_gained = 0 ties the two objects together.
3. **Calorimetry in three lines.** q_solution = mcΔT (whole solution mass); q_reaction = −q_solution; ΔH = q_reaction ÷ moles of limiting reactant. Bomb: q = C_cal ΔT.
4. **Phase changes are potential-energy events.** Temperature is flat while q = nΔH_fus or nΔH_vap is absorbed; ΔH_vap ≫ ΔH_fus because boiling separates molecules completely.
5. **ΔH belongs to the equation as written.** Scale it with the equation; reverse the sign for the reverse reaction; use the limiting reactant's moles.
6. **Three routes to ΔH without a thermometer.** Bond enthalpies (estimate, gas phase, Σ broken − Σ formed); enthalpies of formation (exact, Σ n ΔH°f products − Σ n ΔH°f reactants, elements zero); Hess's law (scale, reverse, add, because enthalpy is a state function).

## Key formulas and data

| Quantity | Formula or value | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Heat in one phase | q = m c ΔT | ΔT = T_final − T_initial; c(water, l) = 4.184 J/(g·°C); ice 2.09; steam 2.01 |
| Heat capacity of an object | C = m c (J/°C) | bomb calorimeter: q = C_cal ΔT |
| Conservation | q_hot + q_cold = 0; q_reaction = −q_solution | opposite signs |
| Final temperature, same substance | T = (m₁T₁ + m₂T₂)/(m₁ + m₂) | otherwise solve m₁c₁(T − T₁) + m₂c₂(T − T₂) = 0 |
| Phase change | q = n ΔH_fus; q = n ΔH_vap | no ΔT; water 6.01 and 40.7 kJ/mol |
| Heating curve total | sum of q for each segment | boiling dominates for water |
| ΔH per mole | ΔH = q_rxn / n(limiting) | J → kJ |
| Bond enthalpies | ΔH ≈ Σ BE(broken) − Σ BE(formed) | count from Lewis structures; gases only |
| Enthalpies of formation | ΔH° = Σ n ΔH°f(prod) − Σ n ΔH°f(react) | ΔH°f(element, standard state) = 0 |
| Hess's law | ΔH(target) = Σ ΔH(steps) | multiply and reverse steps as needed |
| Reverse Ea | Ea(rev) = Ea(fwd) − ΔH | from an energy diagram |

Useful values: H₂O(l) −285.8, H₂O(g) −241.8, CO₂(g) −393.5, CH₄(g) −74.8, NH₃(g) −45.9, NO(g) +90.3 kJ/mol; bond enthalpies H–H 436, O=O 495, O–H 467, C–H 413, C–C 347, C=C 614, C=O 799, N≡N 945, N–H 391, Cl–Cl 243, H–Cl 431 kJ/mol.

## Reading the three kinds of picture

- **Energy diagram:** two levels, gap = ΔH (down is exothermic); the hump between is Ea, which says nothing about ΔH. Hess's law is this diagram with a third level: the drop from top to bottom is the same by any route.
- **Heating curve:** sloped segments warm one phase (slope 1/(m c), so liquid water is the shallowest); plateaus are phase changes (length n ΔH; boiling's is seven times melting's).
- **Temperature–time plot from a calorimeter:** sharp rise, peak, slow cooling; extrapolate the cooling line back to the mixing time to correct for heat loss.

## Common errors

- Sign of q from the wrong point of view (the beaker feels cold means the system is endothermic).
- Using the solute's or the water's mass instead of the whole solution's in q = mcΔT.
- Forgetting q_reaction = −q_solution, or dividing by the moles of the excess reactant.
- Putting a ΔT into q = nΔH_fus, or using 4.184 for ice and steam.
- Formed − broken (bond enthalpies) or reactants − products (formation): always check that a combustion comes out negative.
- Counting bonds from formulas (CO₂ has two C=O; O₂ has one O=O).
- Giving an element a non-zero ΔH°f, or using O(g) as the standard state.
- Scaling an equation without scaling ΔH; reversing without changing the sign.
- Mixing H₂O(l) and H₂O(g): they differ by 44.0 kJ/mol.
- Assuming an exothermic reaction is fast, or that ΔH says anything about rate.

## Worked example: one problem, every method

**Calorimetry.** 2.00 g of Mg (24.31 g/mol) reacts with excess 1.0 M HCl in 100.0 g of solution; the temperature rises from 21.0 °C to 58.4 °C. q_solution = 102.0 × 4.184 × 37.4 = 15,960 J; q_rxn = −15,960 J; n = 2.00/24.31 = 0.0823 mol; ΔH = −15,960/0.0823 = −194,000 J/mol = **−194 kJ/mol** of Mg (the accepted value is near −467 kJ/mol for Mg(s) + 2 H⁺(aq); the shortfall is heat lost to the air and the cup during a vigorous reaction, which is why real experiments use smaller samples and extrapolate).

**Enthalpies of formation.** Mg(s) + ½ O₂(g) → MgO(s): ΔH° = ΔH°f(MgO) = −601.6 kJ/mol directly from the table, because the reactants are elements.

**Hess's law.** Measured: Mg + 2 H⁺ → Mg²⁺ + H₂, −467 kJ; MgO + 2 H⁺ → Mg²⁺ + H₂O, −151 kJ; known H₂ + ½ O₂ → H₂O(l), −285.8 kJ. Keep the first, reverse the second (+151), keep the third; sum: Mg + ½ O₂ → MgO, ΔH = −467 + 151 − 285.8 = **−602 kJ/mol**, matching the table.

**Bond enthalpies (a gas-phase example).** CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl: broken C–H 413 + Cl–Cl 243 = 656; formed C–Cl 339 + H–Cl 431 = 770; ΔH ≈ **−114 kJ**.

**Phase change.** Condensing 36.0 g of steam at 100 °C releases 2.00 mol × 40.7 = **81.4 kJ**; cooling that water to 25 °C releases a further 36.0 × 4.184 × 75 = 11.3 kJ.

## The unit exam

Forty items, about 80 minutes, every topic from 6.1 to 6.9 plus review. Three stimulus sets (an endothermic dissolving in a calorimeter, a table of enthalpies of formation, a described heating curve), stand-alone multiple choice, select-all, true/false, fill-in-the-blank and five numeric calculations (an enthalpy of solution, a formation-table calculation, a final mixing temperature, a bond-enthalpy estimate, a Hess's-law combination), two short-answer questions and one long free-response question built around a calorimetry experiment analysed by every method in the unit. The equations sheet carries q = mcΔT and the formation and bond-enthalpy relations; the specific heats of ice and steam and the enthalpies of fusion and vaporisation will be given in the question.
