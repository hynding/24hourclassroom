# AP Chemistry · Exam Prep Guide

**Exam date:** Thursday 6 May 2027, afternoon session · **Weeks:** 33 (this guide) and 34 (the practice exam) · **Practices:** 1 (Models and Representations), 2 (Question and Method), 3 (Representing Data and Phenomena), 4 (Model Analysis), 5 (Mathematical Routines), 6 (Argumentation)

## The format

The AP Chemistry exam is 3 hours 15 minutes long, in two sections of equal weight.

**Section I** is 60 multiple-choice questions in 90 minutes, worth 50% of the score. Each item has four options and one correct answer; there is no penalty for guessing, so answer every one. Roughly a third of the items come in sets of two to four that share a single stimulus (a data table, a particle diagram, a graph, a lab description or a table of constants). The rest stand alone. The sets are where the exam tests whether you can read a representation, so expect to spend longer on them than on stand-alone items. Ninety seconds per question is the budget; a set of four with a table should take about six minutes.

**Section II** is 7 free-response questions in 105 minutes, also worth 50%. Questions 1 to 3 are **long** (10 points each) and questions 4 to 7 are **short** (4 points each), for 46 raw points. Long questions are built around a single scenario, usually a laboratory experiment, a set of thermodynamic or kinetic data, or an equilibrium system, and have six to eight lettered parts that move from description through calculation to explanation. Short questions have two to four parts and often test one representation: a particle diagram, a Lewis structure, a titration curve, an energy profile. The exam weights the free-response parts by topic to match the unit weights, so Units 7 and 8 (equilibrium, acids and bases) and Unit 4 (reactions) carry more of Section II than Units 5 or 9.

**Unit weights** as the Course and Exam Description gives them: Unit 1, 7–9%; Unit 2, 7–9%; Unit 3, 18–22%; Unit 4, 7–9%; Unit 5, 7–9%; Unit 6, 7–9%; Unit 7, 7–9%; Unit 8, 11–15%; Unit 9, 7–9%. Unit 3 (intermolecular forces, gases, solutions, spectroscopy) is the single largest slice of Section I; Unit 8 is the next. Spread your review accordingly.

**Digital delivery.** The exam is taken in the Bluebook app on a school-managed or personal device. Multiple-choice answers are selected on screen; free-response answers are typed. Practise typing chemistry: write subscripts and superscripts in plain text as this course does (H2O or H₂O, SO4 2− or SO₄²⁻, 1.8 × 10^−5 or 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ are all readable), state units on every number, and label each part by its letter.

## Calculator rules

A scientific or graphing calculator is allowed in **both** sections. The list of approved models is on AP Central; a four-function calculator is permitted but will slow you down on logarithms and exponentials. Calculators may not be shared, and programs or stored notes must be removed. Three habits save time and marks:

- Keep full precision in the calculator and round only the answer you write. Rounding ln K or a square root in the middle of a weak-acid calculation moves the pH by a tenth.
- Know where the log, ln, 10ˣ and eˣ keys are and which is which. pH = −log[H₃O⁺] uses base 10; ΔG° = −RT ln K uses base e. The Nernst equation is given with ln on the sheet; if you prefer log, the conversion is 2.303 (or 0.0592 V at 298 K).
- Write the equation, then the substituted numbers, then the result. A correct setup with a slip in the arithmetic usually earns the method point; a bare wrong number earns nothing.

## The equations and constants sheet

A two-page sheet is provided in both sections. Knowing what is on it means you memorise nothing that is there, and knowing what is *not* on it means you memorise exactly those things. On the sheet:

- **Constants:** R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) and 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K); N_A = 6.022 × 10²³; F = 96,485 C/mol; h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s; c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s; 1 atm = 760 torr = 101.3 kPa; STP = 273 K and 1.0 atm; the specific heat of water, 4.184 J/(g·°C).
- **Atomic structure:** E = hν, c = λν, ΔE = hν (for spectroscopy and the photoelectric effect).
- **Gases, liquids, solutions:** PV = nRT, P_A = X_A·P_total, P_total = P_A + P_B + ..., n = m/M, K = °C + 273, D = m/V, KE = ½mv², molarity = mol/L, A = εbc (Beer–Lambert).
- **Thermochemistry and thermodynamics:** q = mcΔT, ΔS° = Σ S°(products) − Σ S°(reactants), ΔH° = Σ ΔH°f(products) − Σ ΔH°f(reactants) (and the same for ΔG°), ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°, ΔG° = −RT ln K, ΔG° = −nFE°, I = q/t.
- **Kinetics:** the integrated rate laws for zero, first and second order ([A]ₜ − [A]₀ = −kt; ln[A]ₜ − ln[A]₀ = −kt; 1/[A]ₜ − 1/[A]₀ = kt) and the first-order half-life t½ = 0.693/k.
- **Equilibrium and acids:** the generic K expression, Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C, pH = −log[H⁺], pOH = −log[OH⁻], pH + pOH = 14, Ka × Kb = Kw, pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]), Kp = Kc(RT)^Δn.
- **Electrochemistry:** E = E° − (RT/nF) ln Q (the Nernst equation).
- A periodic table with atomic numbers and average atomic masses.

**Not on the sheet, so learn them:** the Arrhenius equation and its ln k versus 1/T form (ln k = −Ea/(RT) + ln A); the second-order half-life; the quadratic formula; the relation Q versus K and what each comparison means; 0.0592 V (the Nernst coefficient at 298 K); the list of strong acids (HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄, HClO₄) and strong bases (group 1 and the heavier group 2 hydroxides); the solubility rules; the rules for assigning oxidation numbers; the periodic trends and their explanations (effective nuclear charge, shielding, distance); VSEPR shapes and bond angles; the hierarchy of intermolecular forces; the sign conventions for q, w and ΔH; the four sign cases of ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°; what a catalyst, a change in concentration, pressure or temperature does to rate, to K and to the equilibrium position; and the definitions of anode, cathode and the direction of electron and ion flow.

## The six science practices

Every free-response part and every multiple-choice item is written to one of the six science practices. Recognising which one is being asked tells you what kind of answer earns the point.

1. **Models and representations.** Describe or draw a particle diagram, a Lewis structure, a graph; translate between a diagram and the chemistry it shows. Points go for the specific feature asked (the number of particles, the lone pairs, the shape of the curve), not for a general description.
2. **Question and method.** Design or critique an experiment: identify the variable, the control, the measurement that would answer the question, the source of error. The point is for the *specific* procedural detail (which mass to weigh, when to take the reading, why a second trial), never for "be more careful".
3. **Representing data and phenomena.** Draw or complete a graph, a table, a curve; plot or extrapolate. Label axes with quantities and units; mark the equivalence point or the half-life explicitly.
4. **Model analysis.** Use a model to predict or explain: what happens to the rate, the pressure, the pH, the voltage when something changes. The point requires the direction of the change *and* the reason in terms of the model (collisions, Q versus K, the Nernst equation).
5. **Mathematical routines.** Calculate. Show the equation, the substitution, the answer with units and sensible significant figures. A result given to ten digits, or without a unit, is marked down.
6. **Argumentation.** Make a claim, back it with evidence (a number, an observation, a trend) and connect the two with reasoning. "The reaction is favourable because ΔG° is −41 kJ/mol, which means K > 1" earns the point; "it is favourable because it is exothermic" does not, because enthalpy alone is not the criterion.

## How free-response questions are scored

Each part is worth one or two points and is scored against a rubric that names exactly what earns each one. The readers look for the required element, not for length. Some patterns you can rely on:

- **Calculation parts** usually give one point for a correct setup (correct equation, correct quantities substituted, consistent units) and one for the correct answer. Errors carry forward: a wrong number from part (b) used correctly in part (c) earns the part (c) point.
- **"Justify" and "explain" parts** give the point only when the reason is stated in chemical terms. The accepted reasons are predictable: more collisions per second, a weaker bond, a larger charge over a smaller distance, more gas moles, Q less than K, more dispersal of matter. Learn to write them in one sentence.
- **"Identify" and "determine"** parts want the thing itself: the limiting reactant, the oxidising agent, the stronger acid. A word or a formula is enough; adding a wrong second answer loses the point.
- **Particle diagrams** are scored on the count and the arrangement asked for; draw what was asked and nothing extra. If the question gives a legend, use it.
- **Significant figures** are checked only in parts that ask for them; everywhere else, two or three is safe. Units are always expected on a final numerical answer.
- **Lab questions** want procedural chemistry: mass before and after, volume at the equivalence point, temperature change read from the maximum, why the calorimeter's heat capacity matters, why the burette is read at eye level. Vague answers ("do it more accurately") never score.

The long questions are built so that the first parts are accessible (a balanced equation, a limiting-reactant calculation) and the last parts are harder (a justification using Q, a non-standard ΔG). Do every first part of every question before any last part; the easy points are worth exactly as much as the hard ones.

## The multiple-choice section

- Read the stem for the quantity actually asked (which is *not* favourable; the *reducing* agent; the pH of the *salt*) before reading the options. Half of wrong answers come from answering a nearby question.
- For stimulus sets, read the data once, write the two or three numbers you will need in the margin (K, ΔH°, the equivalence volume), then take the items in order.
- Estimate before computing. A weak-acid pH is between 2 and 4 for the usual concentrations; a Kp from a negative ΔG° of −30 kJ is around 10⁵; a cell with potentials of +0.80 and −0.76 V gives 1.56 V. If an option list has one value in the right range and three outside it, you may not need the calculator.
- Particle-diagram items: count particles and check conservation first (atoms in, atoms out), then phase and arrangement.
- Items with "which best explains" are testing Practice 6: pick the option that names a mechanism, not the one that merely restates the observation.

## Two-week review plan

**Week 33 (this week): the whole course in order, one unit a day.**

- Day 1 · Units 1 and 2: the mole, mass spectra, electron configuration and PES, periodic trends; bonding, lattice energy, Lewis structures, formal charge, VSEPR, hybridisation, polarity. Redo the unit exams' calculation items.
- Day 2 · Unit 3: intermolecular forces and properties, ideal gas and kinetic molecular theory, deviations, solutions and chromatography, Beer–Lambert. This is the heaviest unit; spend the longest day on it.
- Day 3 · Units 4 and 5: net ionic equations, stoichiometry and titration, redox; rate laws from data, integrated rate laws, Arrhenius, mechanisms and catalysts.
- Day 4 · Units 6 and 7: calorimetry, Hess's law, bond enthalpies; the ICE table, Q versus K, Le Châtelier, Ksp and the common ion.
- Day 5 · Unit 8: strong and weak acids, salts, titration curves, buffers and Henderson–Hasselbalch. The second-largest unit, and the one whose calculations are most often asked in Section II.
- Day 6 · Unit 9: entropy, ΔG°, kinetic control, ΔG° and K, cells, Nernst, Faraday.
- Day 7 · The five night-before sheets you found hardest, and a timed run of 30 multiple-choice items from past unit exams.

**Week 34 (next week): the practice exam and the mop-up.**

- Day 1 · Sit the practice exam's Section I in one 90-minute block. Mark it. For every wrong answer, write one line: what the item tested and which option you fell for.
- Day 2 · Sit Section II in one 105-minute block, typed. Score it against the notes, honestly.
- Day 3 · Rework every Section II part you lost, then find the matching week's study guide and redo its Try-it problems.
- Day 4 · The three weakest topics from your error list, one hour each, from the study guide and the flashcards.
- Day 5 · Rewrite from memory, on one page, the not-on-the-sheet list above. Check it against this guide and fill the gaps.
- Day 6 · A short timed set (20 multiple choice, one long and one short free-response from any unit exam). Stop early.
- Day 7 · Rest.

## The night before and on the day

Charge the device and the calculator; put spare batteries, a pencil and your admission ticket in the bag. Read your one-page list once, then stop. Sleep is worth more than a last hour of flashcards. On the day, eat, arrive early, and expect the first items to feel strange; the exam settles after a few minutes. In Section II, read all seven questions in the first two minutes and start with the one you like best. Write the equation before the number, the unit after it, and the reason after the claim. Leave nothing blank: a sensible partial answer to the last part of a long question is often a point.
