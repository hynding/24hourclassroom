# Week 05 · Writing Equations That Model a Situation

**Lessons:** 2.1 Planning a Pizza Party · 2.2 Writing Equations to Model Relationships (Part 1) · 2.3 Writing Equations to Model Relationships (Part 2) · 2.4 Equations and Their Solutions
**Practices:** MP2 (move between a situation and its symbols), MP4 (model with an equation), MP6 (define every variable with its unit)
**Built from:** IM Algebra 1, Unit 2, Lessons 1–4, in our own words
**Spiral:** Unit 1 taught you to name a variable precisely (minutes of sleep, number of pets). The same precision, this quantity, this unit, is the first step of every equation you write this week.

## What you need to be able to do

- **2.1** Identify the quantities in a situation, say which are known and which vary, and express a budget or total as an expression in those quantities.
- **2.2** Write an equation relating two or more quantities from a verbal description, including situations where one quantity is a constant plus a rate times another.
- **2.3** Write equations from a table or from a pattern, using letters for unknown quantities, and check that the equation reproduces the table.
- **2.4** Explain what it means for a value, or a pair of values, to be a solution of an equation, test whether a given value is a solution, and interpret a solution in context.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Quantity | Something that can be measured or counted, with a unit: cost in dollars, time in hours. |
| Variable | A letter standing for a quantity that can vary or is unknown. |
| Constant | A quantity whose value is fixed in the situation (the $12 delivery fee). |
| Expression | A combination of numbers, variables and operations with no equals sign: 12 + 9p. |
| Equation | A statement that two expressions are equal: 12 + 9p = 66. |
| Model | An equation that describes a relationship in a real situation. |
| Solution of an equation in one variable | A value of the variable that makes the equation true. |
| Solution of an equation in two variables | A pair of values (x, y) that makes the equation true; there are usually infinitely many. |
| Constraint | A condition the quantities must satisfy; the equation is the constraint written in symbols. |

## From a situation to an expression (2.1)

A pizza party has a fixed cost and a cost that depends on how many people come. Suppose a venue charges a $40 room fee and each pizza costs $11.

- If you order 5 pizzas, the cost is 40 + 11 × 5 = $95.
- If you order 8 pizzas, the cost is 40 + 11 × 8 = $128.
- If you order p pizzas, the cost is **40 + 11p** dollars.

The number of pizzas varies, so it gets a letter. The room fee and the price per pizza are constants in this situation. The expression 40 + 11p is a formula for the cost that works for any number of pizzas, and writing it is the key move: once the pattern "fixed cost plus rate times count" is recognised, the arithmetic for 5 and 8 pizzas is the same as the algebra for p pizzas.

**Habit:** before writing any symbols, list the quantities and their units. "p = number of pizzas; C = total cost in dollars." A letter without a definition is a guess, not a model.

**Try it.** A streaming service charges $9 per month plus $3 per movie rented. Write an expression for a month's cost when m movies are rented.
*Answer:* 9 + 3m dollars.

## Writing equations from descriptions (2.2)

An equation says that two expressions are equal. Three patterns cover most situations in this unit.

**Total = part + part.** A jar holds dimes and nickels worth $4.50 altogether. With d dimes and n nickels: 0.10d + 0.05n = 4.50.

**Total = fixed + rate × amount.** A taxi charges $3.50 plus $2.25 per mile. For a fare of F dollars after m miles: F = 3.50 + 2.25m.

**Rate × time = distance (or rate × quantity = total).** A cyclist rides at 14 miles per hour for t hours and covers d miles: d = 14t.

**Worked example.** A school sells tickets at $6 for students and $10 for adults and collects $1,320. Write an equation.
Quantities: s = number of student tickets, a = number of adult tickets. Money from students is 6s, from adults 10a, and the total is 1,320: **6s + 10a = 1,320**.

**Worked example.** A tank holds 500 litres and drains at 20 litres per minute. Write an equation for the volume V after t minutes.
The volume starts at 500 and loses 20 each minute: **V = 500 − 20t**. Check: after 5 minutes, 500 − 100 = 400 litres.

**Try it.** A phone plan costs $25 per month plus $0.10 per text. Write an equation for the monthly bill B after x texts, and find the bill for 180 texts.
*Answers:* B = 25 + 0.10x; for x = 180, B = 25 + 18 = $43.

## Equations from tables and patterns (2.3)

A table hides an equation. Find the rule by asking how the output changes when the input changes by 1, and what the output is when the input is 0.

| Hours worked, h | Pay, P (dollars) |
|---|---|
| 0 | 20 |
| 1 | 35 |
| 2 | 50 |
| 3 | 65 |

Pay rises by 15 for each hour, and the pay at 0 hours is 20 (a fixed bonus). So **P = 20 + 15h**. Check the last row: 20 + 15 × 3 = 65.

When the table does not include the input 0, work back: if h = 4 gives 80 and h = 5 gives 95, the rate is 15 and the value at h = 0 is 80 − 4 × 15 = 20.

Some tables are not "fixed plus rate": the number of squares in a growing pattern might be n² + 1 (2, 5, 10, 17 for n = 1, 2, 3, 4). Always test the rule against every row, not just the first two.

**Worked example.** Write an equation for the perimeter P of a rectangle whose length is 3 cm more than its width w.
Length = w + 3. Perimeter = 2(width) + 2(length) = 2w + 2(w + 3). So **P = 4w + 6**.

**Try it.** A table shows x = 2, 4, 6 paired with y = 11, 19, 27. Write an equation.
*Answer:* y rises by 8 when x rises by 2, so by 4 per unit of x. At x = 2, y = 11, so at x = 0, y = 11 − 8 = 3. y = 4x + 3.

## Solutions of equations (2.4)

A **solution** makes the equation true. For an equation in one variable, substitute and check: is 6 a solution of 40 + 11p = 106? 40 + 66 = 106, yes. Is 5? 40 + 55 = 95, no.

For an equation in two variables, a solution is a **pair**. Is (s, a) = (120, 60) a solution of 6s + 10a = 1,320? 720 + 600 = 1,320, yes: 120 student tickets and 60 adult tickets is one way to collect $1,320. So is (20, 120): 120 + 1,200 = 1,320. There are many solutions, and in context only some make sense (whole numbers of tickets, no negatives).

**Interpreting a solution.** The solution p = 6 of 40 + 11p = 106 means "6 pizzas cost exactly $106". The pair (120, 60) means "120 student tickets and 60 adult tickets bring in exactly $1,320". Always translate the numbers back into the situation.

**Worked example.** Is x = 4 a solution of 3(x − 1) = 2x + 1? Left side: 3(3) = 9. Right side: 8 + 1 = 9. Yes.

**Try it.** Which of the pairs (2, 7), (5, 1) and (0, 11) are solutions of 2x + y = 11?
*Answer:* (2, 7): 4 + 7 = 11 ✓. (5, 1): 10 + 1 = 11 ✓. (0, 11): 0 + 11 = 11 ✓. All three.

## Common mistakes

- Writing an expression without defining what each letter means (and its unit).
- Mixing up which quantity is fixed and which varies: 11 + 40p instead of 40 + 11p.
- Translating "3 more than w" as 3w, or "5 less than x" as 5 − x (it is x − 5).
- Checking only one row of a table before declaring a rule.
- Calling a single number a solution of a two-variable equation; the solution is a pair.
- Forgetting that in context a solution may have to be a whole number or non-negative.

## Self-check

1. A gym charges a $50 joining fee and $30 per month. Write an expression for the cost of m months.
2. A rectangle's length is twice its width w. Write an equation for its perimeter P.
3. A table pairs x = 1, 2, 3 with y = 9, 13, 17. Write the equation.
4. Is y = 5 a solution of 4y − 7 = 13?
5. Give two different solutions of x + 3y = 12 and interpret one of them if x is adult tickets and y is child tickets sold for $1 and $3.

*Answers:* (1) 50 + 30m. (2) P = 2w + 2(2w) = 6w. (3) y = 4x + 5. (4) 20 − 7 = 13, yes. (5) (12, 0), (9, 1), (6, 2), (3, 3), (0, 4) all work; (6, 2) means 6 adult tickets and 2 child tickets bring in $12.
