# Week 31 · Designing With Everything We Know

**Standards:** 5.OA.A.2 Write and read simple expressions without evaluating them · 5.G.A.2 Graph points in the first quadrant to solve problems
**Built from:** IM K–5 Math Grade 5, Unit 8, Section D, in our own words

## What you will learn

- Plan a design, such as a park or a garden, on a **coordinate grid**.
- Find distances between points that share an x-coordinate or a y-coordinate.
- Place the corners of a rectangle and find its perimeter and area.
- Write **expressions** for a plan, and read what an expression says before you work it out.

## Words to know

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Coordinate grid | A grid with a horizontal x-axis and a vertical y-axis, both starting at 0. |
| Origin | The point (0, 0), where the two axes meet. |
| Ordered pair | Two numbers that name a point, like (4, 8): across first, then up. |
| x-coordinate | The first number: how far right from the origin. |
| y-coordinate | The second number: how far up from the origin. |
| Expression | Numbers and signs that stand for an amount, like 4 × (6 + 9). |

## Points on a map

A map on a coordinate grid tells you exactly where things are. Imagine a park with a gate at (2, 3), a slide at (7, 3), a pond at (4, 8) and a garden at (8, 7).

To find the pond, start at the origin, go 4 units right and 8 units up. The order matters: (8, 4) would be 8 right and 4 up, a different spot.

**Moving on the grid.** Going right adds to the x-coordinate. Going up adds to the y-coordinate. From the gate at (2, 3), walking 3 units right takes you to (5, 3). Walking 5 units up from there takes you to (5, 8).

## Distances along grid lines

When two points have the same y-coordinate, they sit on one horizontal line. Subtract the x-coordinates to find the distance. The gate (2, 3) and the slide (7, 3) are 7 − 2 = 5 units apart.

When two points have the same x-coordinate, they sit on one vertical line. Subtract the y-coordinates. A swing at (5, 2) and a tree at (5, 8) are 8 − 2 = 6 units apart.

**Try it.** A bench is at (3, 2) and a fountain at (3, 9). How far apart are they? Both have x = 3, so 9 − 2 = 7 units.

## Rectangles on a grid

A rectangle's sides follow grid lines, so its corners share coordinates in pairs. If three corners are A (1, 2), B (6, 2) and C (6, 6), the fourth corner D is straight above A and level with C: D = (1, 6).

Then the sides are easy to find. AB is 6 − 1 = 5 units, and BC is 6 − 2 = 4 units. If each unit is 1 meter, the area is 5 × 4 = 20 square meters and the perimeter is 2 × (5 + 4) = 18 meters.

## Expressions for a plan

Designers write down how to work something out before they do it. An **expression** records the steps.

| In words | Expression |
|---|---|
| Add 6 and 9, then multiply by 4 | 4 × (6 + 9) |
| Multiply 4 by 6, then add 9 | 4 × 6 + 9 |
| Half of the sum of 36 and 54 | (36 + 54) ÷ 2 |
| Twice the sum of a length of 5 and a width of 3 | 2 × (5 + 3) |
| Two boxes, each 2 by 5 by 7 | 2 × (2 × 5 × 7) |

Parentheses show what happens first. Brackets [ ] work the same way around a bigger group: 2 × [15 + (9 − 4)] means find 9 − 4, then add 15, then double it, which gives 40.

**Reading without calculating.** You can compare expressions without working them out. 25 × (48 + 17) is 25 times as large as 48 + 17. And 5 × (132 + 64) is five times the sum of 132 and 64.

## Putting it all together

A real plan uses the whole year's math. Suppose the park gets a sandbox 6 feet long, 4 feet wide and 2 feet deep. The sand fills 6 × 4 × 2 = 48 cubic feet. If a path on the map is 5 units long and each unit is 1.5 meters, the path is 5 × 1.5 = 7.5 meters. Each step needs a number, a unit and a check that the answer makes sense.

**A common mistake.** Switching the numbers in an ordered pair is the most common slip. Say "across, then up" every time you plot or read a point. With expressions, watch the parentheses: 4 × 6 + 9 is 33, but 4 × (6 + 9) is 60.

## For grown-ups at home

Draw a simple grid on paper and design something together: a bedroom layout, a garden or a treasure map. Take turns giving clues in ordered pairs, like "the treasure is 3 right and 5 up from the tree." Ask your child to say what an expression means in words before working it out.

## Check yourself

1. What are the coordinates of a point 5 units right and 2 units up from the origin?
2. How far apart are (1, 4) and (9, 4)?
3. Three corners of a rectangle are (2, 1), (7, 1) and (7, 3). Where is the fourth corner?
4. Write an expression for "subtract 8 from 20, then multiply by 3."
5. Without calculating, how does 6 × (39 + 75) compare with 39 + 75?

*Answers:* (1) (5, 2). (2) 9 − 1 = 8 units. (3) (2, 3). (4) 3 × (20 − 8), which is 36. (5) It is 6 times as large.
