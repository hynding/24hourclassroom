# Week 28 · Multiplying and Dividing Whole Numbers, One More Time

**Standards:** 5.NBT.B.5 Multiply multi-digit whole numbers with the standard algorithm · 5.NBT.B.6 Divide up to four-digit numbers by two-digit numbers
**Built from:** IM K–5 Math Grade 5, Unit 8, Section A, in our own words

## What you will learn

- Multiply multi-digit numbers with the **standard algorithm**, and find a mistake in someone's work.
- Divide a number with up to four digits by a two-digit number with **partial quotients** or an area model.
- Estimate first, so you know about how big the answer should be.
- Decide what a **remainder** means in a story.

## Words to know

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Product | The answer when you multiply. |
| Standard algorithm | A way to multiply in columns, one row for each digit of the bottom number. |
| Quotient | The answer when you divide. |
| Divisor | The number you divide by. In 1,536 ÷ 32, the divisor is 32. |
| Partial quotient | One chunk of the quotient. Add the chunks at the end. |
| Remainder | What is left over when no more full groups fit. It must be less than the divisor. |

## The standard algorithm for multiplication

You have used area models and partial products all year. The standard algorithm is a short way to write the same parts.

For 437 × 26, multiply 437 by each digit of 26:

- **First row: 437 × 6.** 7 × 6 = 42: write 2, carry 4. 3 × 6 = 18, plus 4 is 22: write 2, carry 2. 4 × 6 = 24, plus 2 is 26. The row is 2,622.
- **Second row: 437 × 20.** The 2 in 26 means 2 tens, so write a 0 in the ones place first. Then 437 × 2 = 874 goes in front of it: 8,740.
- **Add the rows:** 2,622 + 8,740 = 11,362.

**Try it.** 608 × 35. First row: 608 × 5 = 3,040. Second row: 608 × 30 = 18,240. Sum: 21,280.

## Estimate before you calculate

Round each factor to a friendly number and multiply. 389 × 52 is about 400 × 50 = 20,000. If your exact answer were 2,028 or 202,280, the estimate would warn you that a place went missing. (The exact product is 20,228.)

Some products are worth knowing by heart. 125 × 8 = 1,000, so 125 × 48 = 6 × 1,000 = 6,000.

## Dividing by a two-digit number

Division asks "how many groups of the divisor fit?" With partial quotients, take out big, easy chunks first. It helps to list a few multiples of the divisor before you start: for 24, that is 24 × 10 = 240, 24 × 20 = 480 and 24 × 100 = 2,400.

**3,312 ÷ 24:**

- 24 × 100 = 2,400. 3,312 − 2,400 = 912 left.
- 24 × 30 = 720. 912 − 720 = 192 left.
- 24 × 8 = 192. 192 − 192 = 0 left.

Add the chunks: 100 + 30 + 8 = 138. As an **area model**, this is a rectangle with one side 24 and an area of 3,312, split into parts of 2,400, 720 and 192. The other sides of the parts, 100, 30 and 8, add to the quotient.

**Check by multiplying back.** 24 × 138 = 2,400 + 720 + 192 = 3,312. It matches.

**Try it.** 2,925 ÷ 45. 45 × 60 = 2,700, leaving 225. 45 × 5 = 225, leaving 0. The quotient is 65.

## What does the remainder mean?

In a story, the remainder needs a decision.

- **Round up** when everyone needs a place. 230 students ride buses that hold 48. 230 ÷ 48 = 4 R 38, so they need 5 buses.
- **Drop it** when only full groups count. 1,000 juice boxes go in cases of 24. 1,000 ÷ 24 = 41 R 16, so there are 41 full cases.
- **Use it** when the question asks what is left. Those same juice boxes leave 16 boxes over.

**A common mistake.** In the standard algorithm, some students forget the 0 in the second row. For 342 × 57, they write 342 × 5 = 1,710 instead of 342 × 50 = 17,100, and get 4,104. An estimate of 300 × 60 = 18,000 shows that 4,104 is far too small; the right answer is 19,494. In division, watch for a remainder that is as big as the divisor or bigger. 1,536 ÷ 32 = 46 R 64 is not finished, because 64 holds two more 32s. The answer is 48.

## For grown-ups at home

Ask your child to estimate before every calculation and to say whether the answer is close. Let them choose a method: the standard algorithm, partial products and partial quotients all rest on place value. Everyday questions help: "A case has 24 cans. How many cans are in 36 cases?" or "We need 1,200 programs for the school show, printed 48 to a pack. How many packs?"
