# Week 01 · Volume: Filling Space With Unit Cubes

**Standards:** 5.MD.C.3 Understand volume and unit cubes · 5.MD.C.4 Measure volume by counting unit cubes
**Built from:** IM K–5 Math Grade 5, Unit 1, Section A, in our own words

## What you will learn

- Explain what **volume** measures and why we use **unit cubes** to measure it.
- Count every cube in a picture of a prism, including the ones you cannot see.
- Count cubes quickly by finding the cubes in one **layer** and the number of layers.
- Choose a sensible **cubic unit** for an object.

## Words to know

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Solid figure | A shape that takes up space in three directions: length, width and height. |
| Volume | The amount of space a solid figure takes up. |
| Unit cube | A cube whose edges are each 1 unit long. |
| Cubic unit | The space inside one unit cube; the unit we use for volume. |
| Rectangular prism | A box shape with 6 rectangle faces and square corners. |
| Layer | One flat level of cubes in a prism. |

## What is volume?

In earlier grades you measured **length** with a ruler and **area** by covering a flat shape with unit squares. Volume is the next step. It tells how much **space** a solid figure takes up.

To measure volume, we fill the solid with **unit cubes**, cubes whose edges are each 1 unit long. Each unit cube is **1 cubic unit**. A box that 30 unit cubes fill exactly has a volume of 30 cubic units.

Two rules keep the count honest:

- **No gaps.** Empty space between cubes would not be counted.
- **No overlaps.** Two cubes cannot share the same space, or that space is counted twice.

Moving the cubes around does not change the volume. If you build a long flat prism from 20 cubes and then rebuild them into a tall tower, the tower still has a volume of 20 cubic units.

## Counting the cubes you cannot see

A picture of a prism shows only three faces: the front, the top and one side. The prism is solid, so there are cubes **behind** and **under** the ones you can see.

Think about a big cube built 3 small cubes long, 3 wide and 3 high. From the front, top and right you can see 19 small cubes. But 8 more are hidden in the back corner, under the top and behind the front. The big cube has **27** small cubes, so its volume is 27 cubic units.

That is why counting only what you see gives the wrong answer. We need a better way.

## Counting by layers

A rectangular prism of cubes is a stack of **layers** that are all the same.

1. Count the cubes in **one layer**. A layer is rows of cubes: rows × cubes in each row.
2. Count the **number of layers**.
3. Multiply: cubes in a layer × number of layers.

**Try it.** A prism is 6 cubes long, 2 cubes wide and 4 cubes high.

- One layer has 2 rows of 6 cubes: 2 × 6 = 12 cubes.
- There are 4 layers.
- 12 × 4 = 48, so the volume is **48 cubic units**.

**Try it.** A staircase is 2 cubes deep. Its columns are 4, 3, 2 and 1 cubes tall. Count the front slice first: 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 10 cubes. The staircase is 2 slices deep, so it has 10 × 2 = **20 cubes**. Slices work as well as layers.

**Try it.** A box is 5 cubes long and 4 cubes wide. Three layers fill exactly half of it. One layer is 4 × 5 = 20 cubes. If 3 layers fill half, the whole box takes 6 layers: 20 × 6 = **120 cubes**.

## Cubic units for real things

Real volumes use cubes with real edge lengths.

| Unit | The unit cube | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Cubic centimeter (cm³) | Edges of 1 centimeter, like a base-ten ones block | A pencil box, a juice box |
| Cubic inch (in³) | Edges of 1 inch | A jewelry box, a small gift |
| Cubic foot (ft³) | Edges of 1 foot | A toy chest, a fridge |
| Cubic meter (m³) | Edges of 1 meter | A classroom, a moving truck |

The small 3 in cm³ is read "cubic." It reminds us that volume uses three directions. In this course we write "cubic centimeters" in sentences.

**Common mistake.** Adding the edge counts. A prism 6 cubes long, 2 wide and 4 high does not have 6 + 2 + 4 = 12 cubes. Adding gives a length, not a count of cubes. Another common slip is counting one layer and stopping. Always ask, "How many layers are there?"

## For grown-ups at home

Volume can feel abstract until children handle it. Sugar cubes, dice or building blocks make good unit cubes. Build a small box shape together, take it apart, and rebuild it in a new shape: the number of cubes stays the same. When you pack a lunch box or a moving box, ask, "How many of these would fit in one layer? How many layers?" Encourage your child to say "cubic units" instead of just "units."

## Check yourself

1. A prism is 3 cubes long, 3 cubes wide and 2 cubes high. What is its volume?
2. Omar fills a box with cubes but leaves a gap. Is his count the volume?
3. One layer of a box holds 9 cubes. The box holds 5 layers. How many cubes fill it?
4. Which unit would you use for the volume of a swimming pool: cubic centimeters or cubic meters?

*Answers:* (1) 3 × 3 = 9 cubes in a layer, 2 layers, so 18 cubic units. (2) No: the gap is space that is not counted, so his count is too small. (3) 9 × 5 = 45 cubes. (4) Cubic meters; cubic centimeters are far too small.
