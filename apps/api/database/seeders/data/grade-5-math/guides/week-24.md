# Week 24 · The Coordinate Plane and Ordered Pairs

**Standard:** 5.G.A.1 Understand the coordinate plane and ordered pairs
**Built from:** IM K–5 Math Grade 5, Unit 7, Section A, in our own words

## What you will learn

- Name the parts of a coordinate grid: the **x-axis**, the **y-axis** and the **origin**.
- Read the **ordered pair** for a point, like (3, 7).
- Plot a point when you are given its ordered pair.
- Explain why (3, 7) and (7, 3) are different points.

## Words to know

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Coordinate grid | Two number lines that cross at a right angle at 0, with grid lines drawn across. Also called the coordinate plane. |
| x-axis | The horizontal number line along the bottom. |
| y-axis | The vertical number line up the left side. |
| Origin | The point where the axes cross. Its ordered pair is (0, 0). |
| Ordered pair | Two numbers in parentheses, like (4, 9), that name one point. |
| Coordinates | The two numbers of an ordered pair: the x-coordinate first, then the y-coordinate. |

## Two number lines make a grid

You already know how to find a number on a number line. A coordinate grid uses two of them. The **x-axis** runs left to right, and its numbers grow as you move right. The **y-axis** runs straight up, and its numbers grow as you move up. The two axes meet at 0, at a point called the **origin**.

Grid lines run across and up from every number on the axes. A point sits where two grid lines cross. That means we can name any point with two numbers: one from each axis.

This year every grid you use is the **first quadrant**: the corner of the plane where both numbers are 0 or more.

## Reading an ordered pair

An **ordered pair** is written in parentheses with a comma, like (5, 2).

- The **first** number is the **x-coordinate**. It tells how far to move **right** from the origin.
- The **second** number is the **y-coordinate**. It tells how far to move **up**.

A helpful way to remember the order: walk along the hall, then climb the stairs. Across first, then up.

**Try it.** Plot (5, 2). Start at the origin. Move right 5 along the x-axis. Then move up 2. Put a dot there.

**Try it.** A point is straight above 6 on the x-axis and straight across from 4 on the y-axis. What is its ordered pair? Look down to read x = 6 and across to read y = 4, so it is (6, 4).

## The order matters

Order really matters. (2, 9) means 2 right and 9 up, near the top left of the grid. (9, 2) means 9 right and only 2 up, near the bottom right. Same two numbers, two different places. That is why we call it an *ordered* pair.

## Points on an axis

Sometimes one of the coordinates is 0.

- If the **y-coordinate is 0**, you move right but never up, so the point sits on the **x-axis**. Example: (7, 0).
- If the **x-coordinate is 0**, you never move right, only up, so the point sits on the **y-axis**. Example: (0, 3).
- If both are 0, you are at the origin.

## Moving from point to point

Moving right or left changes only the x-coordinate. Moving up or down changes only the y-coordinate.

**Try it.** Start at (2, 5) and move 3 units right. The x-coordinate becomes 2 + 3 = 5, and the y-coordinate stays 5. The new point is (5, 5).

**Try it.** Start at (6, 1) and move 4 units up. The new point is (6, 5).

Points with the same x-coordinate line up on one vertical line, like (3, 1), (3, 4) and (3, 8). Points with the same y-coordinate line up on one horizontal line, like (1, 6) and (9, 6).

**Watch out.** The most common mistake is going up first. In (6, 1), the 6 is how far *right*, so the point is low and to the right. Another mistake is counting squares instead of grid lines. Points sit where the lines cross, and the origin is 0, not 1. Count the lines you land on: 0, 1, 2, 3.

## For grown-ups at home

Play "secret point" on graph paper. Draw axes numbered 0 to 10. One person picks a point and writes its ordered pair on a slip of paper; the other asks only "Is your x-coordinate more than 5?" style questions until they can name it. Then switch. You can also mark a few spots on a simple map of your home or a park, and ask your child to give each one's ordered pair. Ask often, "Which number tells you how far right?" so the order becomes automatic.

## Check yourself

1. What is the ordered pair for the origin?
2. In (4, 10), which number tells how far up to move?
3. Is the point (0, 8) on the x-axis or the y-axis?
4. Start at (1, 3) and move 5 units right. Where are you now?
5. True or false: (5, 8) and (8, 5) are the same point.

*Answers:* (1) (0, 0). (2) 10, the second number, the y-coordinate. (3) The y-axis, because its x-coordinate is 0. (4) (6, 3). (5) False: (5, 8) is 5 right and 8 up, but (8, 5) is 8 right and 5 up.
