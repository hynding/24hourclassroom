# Week 08 · Fractions of Fractions and Scaling

**Standards:** 5.NF.B.5 Understand multiplication as scaling · 5.NF.B.4 Multiply a fraction or whole number by a fraction, including area with fractional sides
**Built from:** IM K–5 Math Grade 5, Unit 3, Section A, in our own words

## What you will learn

- Find a **fraction of a fraction**, like 1/2 of 3/4, and write it as a multiplication.
- Use an **area model** in a unit square to see the product of two fractions.
- Multiply fractions quickly: numerators times numerators, denominators times denominators.
- Tell whether a product is bigger or smaller than a factor **without multiplying**. This is called **scaling**.

## Words to know

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Factor | A number being multiplied. In 2/3 × 6, both 2/3 and 6 are factors. |
| Product | The answer to a multiplication. |
| Unit square | A square with sides 1 unit long. Its area is 1 square unit. |
| Area model | A rectangle drawn to show a product as an area. |
| Scaling | Multiplying to make a number bigger or smaller. |

## A fraction of a fraction

Priya has 3/4 of a pan of lasagna left. Her family eats 1/2 of what is left. How much of the whole pan do they eat?

The word **of** tells us to multiply: 1/2 of 3/4 is **1/2 × 3/4**.

Picture the pan cut into 4 equal columns, with 3 of them left. Now cut every column in half across the middle. The pan is now cut into 8 equal pieces, and half of the 3 columns is 3 of those pieces. So 1/2 × 3/4 = **3/8** of the pan.

**Try it.** What is 1/3 of 1/2? *Cut a half into 3 equal parts. Each part is 1/6 of the whole, so 1/3 × 1/2 = 1/6.*

## The area model

A unit square has an area of 1. To show 2/3 × 4/5:

1. Cut the bottom side into 5 equal parts and mark 4 of them.
2. Cut the left side into 3 equal parts and mark 2 of them.
3. Shade the rectangle that is 4/5 wide and 2/3 tall.

Lines across the square make 5 × 3 = **15** small equal rectangles, so each one is 1/15 of the square. The shaded part is 4 × 2 = **8** of them. So 2/3 × 4/5 = **8/15**.

Look at where the numbers came from. The 8 is 2 × 4, the numerators. The 15 is 3 × 5, the denominators. That gives the rule:

**To multiply two fractions, multiply the numerators and multiply the denominators.**

3/4 × 5/6 = (3 × 5)/(4 × 6) = **15/24**.

You do not have to simplify, but it is fine to: 6/12 and 1/2 name the same amount.

**Try it.** What is 2/5 × 2/3? *2 × 2 = 4 and 5 × 3 = 15, so 4/15.*

## Scaling: bigger or smaller?

Multiplying does not always make a number bigger. Look at what happens to 8:

| Multiply 8 by | Product | Compared with 8 |
|---|---|---|
| 3/4 (less than 1) | 6 | smaller |
| 4/4 (equal to 1) | 8 | the same |
| 5/4 (greater than 1) | 10 | bigger |

So you can decide without multiplying:

- A factor **less than 1** shrinks the other number.
- A factor **equal to 1** leaves it the same.
- A factor **greater than 1** stretches it.

Example: Is 11/12 × 30 more or less than 30? 11/12 is less than 1, so the product is **less than 30**. It is only a little less, because 11/12 is close to 1.

Example: Is 2/3 × 3/5 more or less than 3/5? 2/3 is less than 1, so the product is **less than 3/5**. When both factors are less than 1, the product is smaller than both of them.

On a number line, mark the starting number. A factor less than 1 lands to its left, closer to 0. A factor greater than 1 lands to its right.

**Try it.** Put these in order from least to greatest without multiplying: 7/5 × 20, 1/2 × 20, 20. *1/2 × 20 is less than 20, and 7/5 × 20 is more, so: 1/2 × 20, 20, 7/5 × 20.*

## A common mistake

Some students add when they should multiply, or they multiply the numerators but **add** the denominators. 2/5 × 1/3 is not 3/8 and not 2/8. Think of the area model: the denominators tell you how many small rectangles fill the whole square, and that is a multiplication (5 × 3 = 15). So 2/5 × 1/3 = **2/15**.

Another mistake is thinking "multiplying always makes it bigger." That is true only when the factor is greater than 1.

## For grown-ups at home

Cook or bake together and halve a recipe: "The recipe says 3/4 cup of milk. What is half of that?" Fold a sheet of paper into thirds one way and into fourths the other way, shade some of each, and count the overlap. Ask "Will the answer be more or less than what we started with?" before working anything out.

## Check yourself

1. What is 1/4 of 2/3?
2. What is 3/5 × 3/4?
3. An area model has a unit square cut into 6 columns and 2 rows. How many small rectangles does it have?
4. Is 9/10 × 50 more or less than 50?
5. Which is greater: 5/3 × 12 or 3/5 × 12?

*Answers:* (1) 1/4 × 2/3 = 2/12. (2) 9/20. (3) 6 × 2 = 12. (4) Less, because 9/10 is less than 1 (it is 45). (5) 5/3 × 12, because 5/3 is greater than 1 and 3/5 is less than 1. (They are 20 and 7 1/5.)
