# Week 18 · Multiplying Decimals and Patterns With Powers of 10

**Standards:** 5.NBT.B.7 Add, subtract, multiply and divide decimals to hundredths · 5.NBT.A.2 Explain patterns when multiplying or dividing by powers of 10
**Built from:** IM K–5 Math Grade 5, Unit 5, Section C, in our own words

## What you will learn

- Multiply and divide decimals by **10, 100 and 1,000**, and explain what happens to the digits.
- Multiply a **whole number by a decimal**.
- See why a **tenth times a tenth** is a hundredth.
- Use **area models** and **estimates** to multiply decimals and place the decimal point.

## Words to know

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Product | The answer to a multiplication. |
| Factor | A number being multiplied. In 0.3 × 0.4 = 0.12, the factors are 0.3 and 0.4. |
| Power of 10 | A number made by multiplying 10 by itself, like 10, 100 or 1,000. |
| Partial products | The smaller products you add up to get the whole product. |
| Area model | A rectangle split into parts, used to show a multiplication. |

## Multiplying and dividing by 10, 100 and 1,000

You learned that each place is worth 10 times the place to its right. So when you **multiply by 10**, every digit moves **one place to the left**:

| Problem | Digits move | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 0.35 × 10 | 1 place left | 3.5 |
| 4.25 × 100 | 2 places left | 425 |
| 0.062 × 1,000 | 3 places left | 62 |
| 36.8 ÷ 10 | 1 place right | 3.68 |
| 57 ÷ 100 | 2 places right | 0.57 |

Count the zeros: 100 has two zeros, so the digits move two places. **Dividing** moves them to the right, because each digit becomes 1/10 as much each time.

**Watch out.** "Just add a zero" works for whole numbers (25 × 10 = 250) but not for decimals. 2.5 × 10 is **25**, not 2.50. Writing a zero at the end after the point does not change the value. Move the digits instead.

## A whole number times a decimal

6 × 0.8 means 6 groups of 8 tenths. That is 48 tenths, which is **4.8**.

Money works the same way. 12 pencils at $0.35 each: 10 pencils cost $3.50 and 2 more cost $0.70, so 12 pencils cost **$4.20**.

**Try it.** 3 × 0.25. Three groups of 25 hundredths make 75 hundredths: **0.75**.

## A tenth times a tenth

Draw a big square with sides of 1 and split it into a 10 by 10 grid. Now shade a rectangle **0.3** wide and **0.4** tall. It covers 3 columns of 4 small squares: **12** small squares out of 100. So 0.3 × 0.4 = **0.12**.

Each small square is 0.1 wide and 0.1 tall, and it is 1/100 of the big square. That is why **a tenth times a tenth is a hundredth**.

Notice that 0.12 is less than both 0.3 and 0.4. When you multiply by a number less than 1, you get only part of the other number, so the product is smaller.

## Area models for bigger decimals

To find 2.4 × 1.3, split 2.4 into 2 and 0.4, and 1.3 into 1 and 0.3. Draw a rectangle with four parts and multiply each pair:

| × | 2 | 0.4 |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2 | 0.4 |
| 0.3 | 0.6 | 0.12 |

Add the partial products: 2 + 0.4 + 0.6 + 0.12 = **3.12**.

## Estimate to check the decimal point

Before you multiply, round. 3.9 × 5.1 is about 4 × 5 = **20**. If you multiply 39 × 51 and get 1,989, the estimate tells you the product is 19.89, not 198.9 or 1.989.

**Watch out.** 0.5 × 0.5 is not 2.5. Half of a half is a quarter: **0.25**.

## For grown-ups at home

Play "move the digits": say a decimal like 3.07 and a power of 10, and have your child say the answer and show where each digit moves (3.07 × 100 = 307). For products like 0.6 × 0.3, ask "Should the answer be more or less than 0.6?" Thinking about size first catches most decimal-point mistakes.
