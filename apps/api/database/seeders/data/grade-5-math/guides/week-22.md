# Week 22 · Fraction Stories and Benchmark Estimates

**Standard:** 5.NF.A.2 Solve word problems by adding and subtracting fractions
**Built from:** IM K–5 Math Grade 5, Unit 6, Section B, in our own words

## What you will learn

- Use **benchmark** fractions 0, 1/2 and 1 to estimate sums and differences.
- Decide whether an answer is **reasonable** before and after you solve.
- Choose to add or subtract in a fraction story.
- Draw a tape diagram for a story and solve it with a common denominator.
- Solve two-step stories and stories with mixed numbers.

## Words to know

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Benchmark | A friendly number to compare with, such as 0, 1/2 or 1. |
| Estimate | A close answer found with easy numbers. |
| Reasonable | Makes sense: close to the estimate and fits the story. |
| Tape diagram | A bar drawing that shows the parts and the whole in a story. |

## Benchmarks

Ask, "Is this fraction closest to 0, to 1/2 or to 1?"

- 5/6 is only 1/6 away from 1, so it is close to 1.
- 3/8 is 1/8 away from 4/8, which is 1/2, so it is close to 1/2.
- 1/9 is a tiny piece, so it is close to 0.

A quick test for "more than 1/2": double the numerator. For 5/8, 5 + 5 = 10, which is more than 8, so 5/8 is more than 1/2.

**Try it.** Estimate 7/8 + 4/9. *Answer:* 7/8 is about 1 and 4/9 is about 1/2, so the sum is about 1 1/2.

For mixed numbers, round to the nearest whole number (or half). 3 1/8 − 1 5/6 is about 3 − 2 = 1.

## Is the answer reasonable?

Estimating catches mistakes. Suppose someone writes 2/5 + 1/2 = 3/7. But 3/7 is less than 1/2, and adding 2/5 to 1/2 must give more than 1/2. So 3/7 cannot be right. The real sum is 4/10 + 5/10 = 9/10, which is close to 1.

Two more shortcuts:

- If both fractions are less than 1/2, their sum is less than 1.
- If both fractions are more than 1/2, their sum is more than 1.

## Solving fraction stories

1. **Read** and ask what the story wants. "In all" or "altogether" means add. "How much more", "how much farther" or "how much is left" means subtract.
2. **Draw** a tape diagram. Mark the whole, the parts you know, and a question mark.
3. **Estimate** with benchmarks.
4. **Solve** with a common denominator.
5. **Check** that the answer is close to the estimate, and label it.

**Try it.** Rosa's walk to school is 3/4 mile. She has walked 1/3 mile. How much farther does she have to walk?

*Answer:* The whole is 3/4 and one part is 1/3, so subtract. Estimate: about 3/4 − 1/4 = 1/2. Solve: 9/12 − 4/12 = 5/12 mile. 5/12 is a little less than 1/2, so it is reasonable.

**Try it.** Ben needs 7/8 cup of oats. He has 1/2 cup. How much more does he need? *Answer:* 1/2 = 4/8, so 7/8 − 4/8 = 3/8 cup.

## Two-step stories

Some stories hide two steps. A garden is split up: 1/3 for beans and 1/4 for peppers. What fraction is left for flowers?

*Answer:* First add the parts used: 4/12 + 3/12 = 7/12. Then subtract from the whole, 1 = 12/12: 12/12 − 7/12 = 5/12 for flowers.

## Mixed numbers in stories

Liam hikes 1 1/2 miles in the morning and 2 3/4 miles after lunch. How far does he hike?

*Answer:* Wholes: 1 + 2 = 3. Parts: 2/4 + 3/4 = 5/4 = 1 1/4. Total: 4 1/4 miles. Estimate check: about 1 1/2 + 3 = 4 1/2, which is close.

## Common mistakes

- **Adding when the story asks "how much more".** That calls for subtracting.
- **Skipping the estimate.** It is the fastest way to spot an impossible answer.
- **Forgetting the whole.** "What is left" of one whole means starting from 1, such as 12/12.
- **Dropping the label.** The answer is 5/12 mile, not just 5/12.

## For grown-ups at home

Fraction stories are everywhere at home: recipes, rides and screen time. Ask your child to estimate first: "We used 2/3 of the bag of flour and then 1/4 more. Did we use more or less than the whole bag?" Then work out the exact answer together and compare it with the estimate.

## Check yourself

1. Which benchmark is 4/7 closest to?
2. Estimate 9/10 + 5/12.
3. Is 1/4 a reasonable answer for 1/2 + 1/3? Why?
4. A jug had 5/6 L of juice. Mia poured out 1/2 L. How much is left?
5. Tia read 1 2/3 hours on Saturday and 3/4 hour on Sunday. How long did she read in all?

*Answers:* (1) 1/2 (it is 1/14 more than 1/2). (2) About 1 + 1/2 = 1 1/2. (3) No: 1/4 is less than 1/2, but the sum must be more than 1/2. It is 5/6. (4) 5/6 − 3/6 = 2/6 = 1/3 L. (5) 1 8/12 + 9/12 = 1 17/12 = 2 5/12 hours.
