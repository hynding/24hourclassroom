# Week 12 · The Standard Algorithm for Multiplication

**Standard:** 5.NBT.B.5 Multiply multi-digit whole numbers with the standard algorithm
**Built from:** IM K–5 Math Grade 5, Unit 4, Section A, in our own words

## What you will learn

- See how the **standard algorithm** for multiplication matches partial products.
- Multiply a three- or four-digit number by a one-digit number, regrouping as you go.
- Multiply by a two-digit number with two rows, and know why the second row ends in 0.
- Estimate to check that a product makes sense.

## Words to know

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Standard algorithm | A short written method that multiplies one place at a time, starting with the ones. |
| Partial product | The product of one factor and one part of the other factor, such as 56 × 20. |
| Regroup | Trade 10 of one place for 1 of the next place, like 18 ones for 1 ten and 8 ones. |
| Estimate | A close answer found with rounded numbers. |

## From partial products to the algorithm

In Grade 4 you multiplied with partial products. Here is 473 × 6:

- 400 × 6 = 2,400
- 70 × 6 = 420
- 3 × 6 = 18

2,400 + 420 + 18 = **2,838**.

The standard algorithm finds the same parts but adds as it goes, so it is shorter. Start with the ones.

1. **Ones:** 3 × 6 = 18 ones. Write 8 in the ones place. Regroup 1 ten: write a small 1 above the tens place.
2. **Tens:** 7 tens × 6 = 42 tens. Add the regrouped 1 ten: 43 tens. Write 3 in the tens place. Regroup 4 hundreds: write a small 4 above the hundreds place.
3. **Hundreds:** 4 hundreds × 6 = 24 hundreds. Add the regrouped 4 hundreds: 28 hundreds. Write 28.

The product is 2,838, the same as before. Each small digit is worth the place it sits above.

**Try it.** 3,208 × 4. 8 × 4 = 32: write 2, regroup 3 tens. 0 × 4 = 0, plus 3 = 3 tens. 2 × 4 = 8 hundreds. 3 × 4 = 12 thousands. The product is **12,832**. A 0 in a factor still needs its regrouped amount added.

## Multiplying by a two-digit number

For 56 × 23, think of 23 as 20 + 3. The algorithm writes one row for each part.

- **First row:** 56 × 3 = 168.
- **Second row:** 56 × 20 = 1,120. Because you are multiplying by 2 **tens**, the row ends in 0. Many people write the 0 first, then multiply 56 × 2 = 112 to the left of it.
- **Add the rows:** 168 + 1,120 = **1,288**.

| Row | What it multiplies | Value |
|---|---|---|
| First | 56 × 3 | 168 |
| Second | 56 × 20 | 1,120 |
| Total | 56 × 23 | 1,288 |

**Try it.** 609 × 47. First row: 609 × 7 = 4,263. Second row: 609 × 40 = 24,360. Add: 4,263 + 24,360 = **28,623**.

**Try it.** A school orders 38 boxes of 144 pencils. 144 × 8 = 1,152 and 144 × 30 = 4,320, so the school orders 5,472 pencils.

## Estimate to check

Round each factor to a friendly number and multiply. 487 × 62 is about 500 × 60 = 30,000. The exact product is 30,194, so it is close. If you got 3,019 or 301,940, the estimate tells you something went wrong.

A three-digit number times a two-digit number is at least 1,000 (100 × 10) and less than 100,000 (1,000 × 100).

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting the 0 in the second row.** For 312 × 24, the rows are 1,248 and **6,240**, not 1,248 and 624. The product is 7,488. Without the 0, the second row is ten times too small.
- **Forgetting a regrouped digit.** Multiply first, then add the small digit. In 473 × 6, 7 × 6 = 42, and 42 + 1 = 43.
- **Adding the small digit before multiplying.** 7 × 6 + 1 is 43, but (7 + 1) × 6 is 48. Multiply, then add.

## For grown-ups at home

This may be the method you learned in school. Your child learned partial products first, so ask "What is that little number worth?" and "Why is there a 0 at the end of the second row?" If they can answer, they understand the shortcut and are not just following steps. Real-life practice: seats in a stadium, pages in a stack of books, or steps walked in a month.
