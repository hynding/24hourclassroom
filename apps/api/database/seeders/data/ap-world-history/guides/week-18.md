## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | Smith's doctrine: minimal state interference in markets; applied in Britain through free trade (Corn Laws repealed 1846) and resistance to regulation. |
| Corporation | A company owned by shareholders with limited liability (shareholders risk only their investment), permitted generally in Britain from 1855–1862; the form of large industrial firms and railways. |
| Stock exchange | A market in company shares and government bonds; London's and New York's financed railways and industries worldwide. |
| Gold standard | Currencies fixed to gold (Britain from 1821, Germany 1873, United States 1900), which stabilised exchange rates and eased international trade and lending. |
| Transnational business | Firms operating across borders: the Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corporation (1865), Unilever (Lever Brothers in Britain and the Netherlands), the United Fruit Company. |
| Consumerism | The purchase of mass-produced goods (clothing, soap, newspapers, bicycles) by working- and middle-class families; department stores and advertising. |
| Trade union | A workers' association to bargain over wages and hours; legalised in Britain in 1824, growing after 1870. |
| Chartism | The British working-class movement (1838–1848) demanding the vote, secret ballot and paid members of Parliament; defeated, but most demands later enacted. |
| Utopian socialism | Owen, Fourier and Saint-Simon's model communities and cooperatives, rejecting competition without class struggle. |
| Marxism | Marx and Engels's theory: history is class struggle; capitalism creates a proletariat that will overthrow the bourgeoisie; the Manifesto (1848) and *Capital* (1867). |
| Anarchism | The rejection of the state as such (Proudhon, Bakunin); influential among workers in Spain, Italy and Russia, with some assassinations. |
| Bismarck's social insurance | Sickness (1883), accident (1884) and old-age (1889) insurance in Germany, meant to undercut the socialists. |
| Self-Strengthening Movement | Qing China's programme (1860s–1890s) of arsenals, shipyards and schools under the slogan of Chinese learning as the base and Western learning for use; limited by conservatism and defeat by Japan (1895). |
| Cult of domesticity | The middle-class ideal that a woman's sphere is the home and a man's the market; separate spheres. |
| Middle class | Factory owners, managers, professionals, clerks and shopkeepers, whose wealth came from industry and commerce, not land. |
| Working class | Wage-earners in factories, mines, railways and docks, dependent on employers and vulnerable to unemployment. |

## The economy of the industrial age (5.7)

**Capitalism's institutions.** Factories, railways and steamships needed more capital than a family or partnership could risk. The answer was the **corporation** with **limited liability**, in which shareholders lost only what they invested; British law made the form generally available in the 1850s and 1860s and the **stock exchange** traded the shares. Banks pooled savings and lent them; by the 1870s British capital was building railways in Argentina, India and the United States. The **gold standard**, Britain's since 1821 and the world's by 1900, fixed exchange rates and made the pound the world's money. Firms became **transnational**: the Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corporation (1865) financed trade in China; Lever Brothers made soap in Britain from palm oil grown in West Africa and sold it in both places; the Singer company sold sewing machines from Moscow to Buenos Aires.

**Free trade.** Smith's argument became British policy when Parliament repealed the Corn Laws (1846), the tariffs that had protected landowners' grain. Britain, the workshop of the world, wanted open markets; others, catching up, did not. Germany (1879) and the United States (throughout the century) protected their infant industries with tariffs, and so, after 1899, did Japan. Free trade was the ideology of the leader and protection the strategy of the followers.

**The new global division of labour.** Industrial countries exported manufactures and capital and imported food and raw materials: wheat from the American prairie, the Argentine pampas and Russia; wool from Australia; cotton from India, Egypt and the American South; rubber, palm oil and copper from the tropics. Regions that did not industrialise were drawn into the system as suppliers, a pattern Unit 6 calls economic imperialism (6.4–6.5). Prices of manufactured goods fell; the working family in Manchester by 1900 could buy cotton clothes, soap, tea, sugar, a newspaper and perhaps a bicycle, which is **consumerism**: mass production meeting mass demand through department stores and advertising.

## Reactions: unions, socialists, reformers and states (5.8)

The industrial economy's first effects on workers were long hours, low wages, unemployment in slumps, child labour and slums; the responses came from workers, intellectuals and governments.

**Workers.** The Luddites (1811–1816) broke the machines that displaced them. **Trade unions**, illegal in Britain until 1824, grew among skilled workers and after 1889 among dockers and the unskilled; **strikes** won shorter hours and recognition; friendly societies and cooperatives (Rochdale, 1844) pooled savings. **Chartism** (1838–1848) demanded the vote for working men with petitions of millions of signatures and was refused; the **Reform Acts** of 1867 and 1884 gave most working men the vote anyway, and the Labour Party (1900) followed. In Germany the Social Democratic Party, Marxist in programme, was the largest party in the Reichstag by 1912.

**Socialists.** **Utopian socialists** proposed cooperation in place of competition: Robert Owen ran New Lanark mills with shorter hours and schools and founded New Harmony in Indiana; Fourier designed phalanxes; Saint-Simon wanted engineers to govern. **Marx and Engels** dismissed them as dreamers. The *Manifesto* (1848) opens: "The history of all hitherto existing societies is the history of class struggles." The bourgeoisie, having destroyed feudal ties, "has left remaining no other nexus between man and man than naked self-interest, than callous 'cash payment'"; it has drawn "all, even the most barbarian, nations into civilisation" by its cheap goods; and it has created its own gravedigger, the proletariat, whose revolution will abolish private property and class. The workers "have a world to win": "WORKING MEN OF ALL COUNTRIES, UNITE!" Engels's *Condition of the Working-Class in England* (1845) had already described Manchester, where in the crowded streets "the brutal indifference, the unfeeling isolation of each in his private interest becomes the more repellant and offensive, the more these individuals are crowded together," so that "the social war, the war of each against all, is here openly declared." **Anarchists** (Proudhon, Bakunin, Kropotkin) went further and rejected the state itself.

**Reformers and governments.** Parliamentary inquiries (the Sadler Committee, week 17) led to the **Factory Acts** (1833, 1844, 1847), which limited children's and women's hours and created inspectors; the Mines Act (1842) removed women and children from underground; Public Health Acts (1848, 1875) built sewers. **Bismarck**, to take the ground from under the Social Democrats, created sickness, accident and old-age **insurance** (1883–1889), the first welfare state. Liberals who had preached laissez-faire accepted that factories needed law. **Samuel Smiles** spoke for the self-help creed that opposed both socialism and state aid: "'Heaven helps those who help themselves' is a well-tried maxim"; "Help from without is often enfeebling in its effects, but help from within invariably invigorates"; and "no laws, however stringent, can make the idle industrious, the thriftless provident, or the drunken sober."

**States facing industrial power.** The Ottoman **Tanzimat** and Muhammad Ali's programme (week 17) were reactions to the industrial economy from outside it. So was Qing China's **Self-Strengthening Movement** (1861–1895): after the Opium Wars and the Taiping Rebellion, provincial officials (Zeng Guofan, Li Hongzhang) built the Jiangnan Arsenal, the Fuzhou shipyard, a steamship company, telegraphs, mines and schools of Western languages, under the formula of Chinese learning for the essence and Western learning for practical use. The court's conservatism, the Empress Dowager Cixi's diversion of naval funds, and the decentralisation of the programme left China without a national industrial policy; Japan's victory in 1895 exposed the result. Reform from above was tried in every empire the industrial powers threatened; its success depended on whether the state could tax, borrow and keep control of its own economy.

## Society in the industrial age (5.9)

**Classes.** Industry made two new classes. The **middle class** of owners, managers, professionals and clerks displaced the landed gentry as the leading group in Britain by mid-century and set the age's values: work, thrift, respectability, the family home. The **working class**, propertyless and dependent on wages, lived in the industrial towns; within it skilled artisans, factory operatives and casual labourers were separated by wage and respectability. Class replaced estate and caste as the organising fact of society, and class identity, expressed in unions, parties and neighbourhoods, is the industrial age's social invention.

**Women and children.** Factories employed women and children first: they were cheap, and the machines needed no strength. In 1830s Lancashire women were about half of cotton operatives; children under thirteen worked twelve-hour days until the 1833 Act. The pattern changed in two ways. Laws removed children and limited women's hours, and schooling (compulsory in Prussia early, Britain from 1880, Japan from 1872) took children out of mills. And the middle class made the home a woman's sphere: the **cult of domesticity** held that a wife's work was the household and moral guardianship, and a man's the market; working-class families aspired to a wife who need not go out to work. The result by 1900 was a sharper separation of home and work than before industrialisation, when household and workshop were one, and the demand of Seneca Falls (1848) that women have "the elective franchise" and a voice in the laws was made against this new order as much as the old.

**Cities.** Manchester grew from 25,000 (1772) to 300,000 (1851); London passed two million; Berlin, Chicago and Osaka followed. The cities were built fast and badly: back-to-back housing, cellars, no sewers, water from polluted rivers, coal smoke that blackened the sky and the lungs. Cholera epidemics (1832, 1848) and typhus killed the poor and frightened the rich into public health reform; by 1900 sewers, waterworks, parks, trams and building codes had made the cities liveable, and death rates fell. Crime, drink and prostitution were the moralists' themes; police forces (London's from 1829) were the state's answer.

**Standards of living.** The debate is old and the evidence mixed. In the first generation (1790–1840) real wages barely rose while hours lengthened, diets worsened and towns killed; from the 1850s real wages rose steadily, prices of food and cloth fell, and by 1900 the British working family ate meat, wore cotton, read newspapers and lived longer than its grandparents. Industrialisation made people poorer before it made them richer, and the timing differed by country and class.

## Continuity and change across Unit 5 (5.10)

| Pattern | Change, 1750–1900 | Continuity |
|---|---|---|
| Production | Machines, factories, fossil fuel; output per worker multiplied | Most of the world's people still farmed by hand in 1900 |
| Energy and environment | Coal and then oil; smoke, slag, deforested uplands | Water, wood and muscle outside the industrial regions |
| Trade | Global market in manufactures, food and raw materials; gold standard; steamships and telegraph | Older patterns of unequal exchange: raw materials from the periphery (Unit 4's silver and sugar, now cotton and wheat) |
| Labour | Free wage labour and the working class; unions; end of serfdom (1861) and slavery (Britain 1833, U.S. 1865, Brazil 1888) | Coerced labour continued in new forms: indenture, convict labour, forced cultivation (Unit 6) |
| Social hierarchy | Middle and working classes replaced estates; careers open to talent | Landed aristocracies kept wealth and power in Britain, Germany, Russia; racial hierarchies hardened (Unit 6) |
| Gender | Women in mills; then the separation of home and work; the suffrage demand | Patriarchal law: the Napoleonic Code; no votes for women anywhere before 1893 (New Zealand) |
| The state | Nation-states; constitutions and suffrage; factory law and welfare; state-led industrialisation | Monarchies survived nearly everywhere; empires grew (Unit 6) |
| Ideas | Liberalism, socialism, nationalism, feminism | Religion remained the belief of most people; evangelical revival |

The Unit 5 argument: the industrial revolution and the political revolutions together produced the most rapid change in human production, politics and social organisation in history, concentrated in a few regions, while most of humanity experienced them as the arrival of cheap cloth, new taxes and foreign power. Extent depends on where you stand.

## Primary sources to read closely

**Marx and Engels, Manifesto of the Communist Party (1848; English edition 1888).** "The history of all hitherto existing societies is the history of class struggles." The bourgeoisie "has left remaining no other nexus between man and man than naked self-interest, than callous 'cash payment'" and "draws all, even the most barbarian, nations into civilisation." "They have a world to win. WORKING MEN OF ALL COUNTRIES, UNITE!" *Use:* the Marxist analysis of the industrial economy, and a source that both praises the bourgeoisie's achievements and predicts its overthrow.

**Engels, The Condition of the Working-Class in England in 1844 (1845; translated 1885), "The Great Towns."** In London's crowds "the brutal indifference, the unfeeling isolation of each in his private interest becomes the more repellant and offensive, the more these individuals are crowded together"; "the social war, the war of each against all, is here openly declared." *Use:* the industrial city as seen by its sharpest critic; compare Hobbes's war of all against all (week 15).

**Smiles, Self-Help (1859).** "'Heaven helps those who help themselves' is a well-tried maxim"; "no laws, however stringent, can make the idle industrious, the thriftless provident, or the drunken sober." *Use:* the middle-class answer to socialism and state aid; the values of the Victorian bourgeoisie.

**Declaration of Sentiments (1848).** "He has withheld from her rights which are given to the most ignorant and degraded men - both natives and foreigners." *Use:* the women's rights demand as a reaction to the industrial age's separate spheres as well as to the old law.

## Common mistakes

- **"Marx wanted to abolish industry."** He admired its productive power; he wanted the workers to own it.
- **"Laissez-faire meant no state."** The state built police, sewers, schools and, by 1900, factory law and insurance; free trade was the policy, not anarchy.
- **"Industrialisation liberated women."** It first put them in mills, then sent middle-class women home; the vote came after 1900.
- **"Living standards rose immediately."** The first generation suffered; the gains came after 1850.
- **"Self-Strengthening failed because China rejected Western technology."** It built arsenals and shipyards; it failed through decentralisation, court conservatism and lack of fiscal control, and because Japan did it better.
