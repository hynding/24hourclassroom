## What you need to be able to do

- **4.3** Explain the causes and effects of the Columbian Exchange: the transfer of **diseases** (smallpox, measles, malaria) that devastated Indigenous populations; of **animals** (horses, cattle, pigs, sheep) and **crops** (wheat, sugar, rice, bananas eastward; maize, potatoes, cassava, tomatoes, cacao westward) that transformed diets, environments and populations on both sides; the **population growth** in Afro-Eurasia fed by American crops; the **African** foods (okra, rice varieties) carried to the Americas with the slave trade; and environmental change: deforestation, soil depletion from plantations, new grazing land.
- **4.4** Explain the process by which maritime empires were established: **Portugal's** trading-post empire (Goa, Malacca, Hormuz, Macau, Mozambique) built on sea power, with little inland territory; **Spain's** conquest of the Aztec and Inca empires and its territorial empire in the Americas and the Philippines (Manila); the **Dutch, French and English** empires of trading companies, colonies and competition; the role of **disease** and **Indigenous allies** in the Spanish conquests; and the continuities of the Indian Ocean trade under European intrusion, including the **Omani** and **Asian** merchants who kept trading.

In plain language: what crossed the Atlantic in each direction and with what results, and how each European state built an empire with its own method.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Columbian Exchange | The two-way transfer of plants, animals, people and diseases between the Americas and Afro-Eurasia after 1492. |
| Virgin-soil epidemic | An epidemic among a population with no prior exposure or immunity; smallpox in the Americas killed perhaps 90 per cent over a century. |
| Smallpox | The deadliest import; reached Tenochtitlan in 1520 and the Inca before Pizarro arrived. |
| Maize, potato, cassava | American staples that fed population growth in Africa, Europe and China. |
| Sugar | The plantation crop that drove the slave trade; from Madeira to Brazil and the Caribbean. |
| Horse | Reintroduced to the Americas by the Spanish; transformed Plains and Pampas societies. |
| Trading-post empire | Portugal's model: fortified ports controlling sea lanes, not territory. |
| Goa; Malacca; Hormuz; Macau | Portuguese bases taken 1510, 1511, 1515, 1557. |
| Hernán Cortés | Led the conquest of the Aztec Empire (1519–1521) with Tlaxcalan allies and smallpox. |
| Francisco Pizarro | Captured the Inca Atahualpa at Cajamarca (1532); Inca Empire fell by 1533. |
| Viceroyalty | Spanish territorial administration: New Spain (1535) and Peru (1542), each under a viceroy. |
| Manila galleon | Annual ship from Acapulco to Manila (from 1571) carrying American silver to buy Chinese goods. |
| Dutch East India Company (VOC) | Chartered 1602; took Batavia (1619), Malacca (1641), the Spice Islands and Ceylon. |
| Chartered company | A private company granted monopoly, military and governing powers by its state: VOC, English East India Company (1600), French (1664). |
| Omani Empire | Arab maritime state that drove the Portuguese from Muscat (1650) and Mombasa (1698) and took over Swahili trade. |

## The Columbian Exchange (4.3)

**Disease.** The Americas had been separated from Afro-Eurasia for over ten thousand years and had none of the crowd diseases that cattle, pigs and dense cities had bred there. **Smallpox**, measles, influenza, typhus and later malaria and yellow fever arrived with Europeans and Africans and swept through populations without immunity: Hispaniola's Taino were nearly gone within thirty years; smallpox reached Tenochtitlan in 1520 during Cortés's siege and killed the emperor Cuitláhuac; it killed the Inca Huayna Capac and his heir around 1527, setting off the civil war Pizarro exploited. Over the century after 1492 the Indigenous population of the Americas fell by perhaps **ninety per cent**, the greatest demographic catastrophe in history. Las Casas, who saw it in Hispaniola, wrote that "of Three Millions of Persons, which lived in Hispaniola itself, there is at present but the inconsiderable remnant of scarce Three Hundred" (1689 English). His numbers are a polemicist's; the scale is not in doubt.

**Animals and crops eastward.** Europeans brought **horses**, cattle, pigs, sheep, goats and chickens; **wheat**, barley, rice, **sugar**, coffee (later), citrus, grapes and olives; and from Africa, with the slave trade, **okra**, yams, rice varieties and the knowledge of growing them. Horses remade the Great Plains and the Pampas; cattle and sheep turned Mexican and Argentine grasslands into ranches and ate Indigenous crops; pigs ran wild. **Sugar** was the crop that mattered most: the Mediterranean and Atlantic-island plantation model (Unit 2) crossed to Brazil and the Caribbean and demanded the labour of enslaved Africans (week 13).

**Crops westward.** **Maize** fed Africa, southern Europe and China; the **potato** fed northern Europe and let Ireland's and Prussia's populations grow; **cassava** (manioc) became a West and Central African staple; **tomatoes**, peppers, beans, squash, peanuts, cacao, tobacco and vanilla entered diets worldwide. The CED's conclusion: American crops drove **population growth in Afro-Eurasia** from the 1600s (China's population doubled under the Qing partly on maize and sweet potatoes grown on hillsides), while Afro-Eurasian diseases caused population collapse in the Americas.

**Environment.** Plantations cleared forests in Brazil and the Caribbean; sugar exhausted soils; European grazing animals changed grasslands and spread weeds; silver mining at Potosí poisoned rivers with mercury; the depopulation of the Americas let forests regrow over abandoned fields. The exchange was a biological reunification of the planet, and its costs fell overwhelmingly on the peoples of the Americas.

## Maritime empires established (4.4)

**Portugal: the trading-post empire.** Too small to conquer territory, Portugal used cannon-armed ships to seize chokepoints and tax the trade that passed them: **Goa** (1510) as capital, **Malacca** (1511) controlling the strait, **Hormuz** (1515) at the Gulf, Mozambique and Mombasa on the Swahili coast, **Macau** (1557) for the China trade, later Nagasaki for Japan. Ships without a Portuguese pass could be seized. The system never controlled more than a fraction of Indian Ocean trade (Asian and Omani merchants kept sailing), and by 1650 the Dutch had taken most of it.

**Spain: the territorial empire.** In the Americas Spain conquered states. **Cortés** landed in 1519 with about six hundred men; he won because of steel, horses and cannon against obsidian and cotton armour, because tens of thousands of **Tlaxcalans** and other subjects of the Aztecs joined him, and because **smallpox** struck Tenochtitlan during the siege; the city fell in 1521. Bernal Díaz, a soldier of Cortés, described the first sight of Tenochtitlan from the causeway as something out of a romance, and recorded the Tlaxcalan alliance and the disease that followed. **Pizarro** found the Inca Empire torn by a succession war after smallpox had killed its emperor, captured **Atahualpa** at Cajamarca (1532) with fewer than two hundred men, took a room of gold as ransom and executed him; Cuzco fell in 1533. Cieza de León, travelling Peru in the 1540s, admired the Inca roads, storehouses and labour organisation the Spaniards had inherited. Spain then governed through **viceroyalties**, the Church, and the extraction of silver (**Potosí**, 1545) and forced labour (week 13). From 1571 the **Manila galleons** linked Mexican silver to Chinese silk, making Spain's an empire across two oceans.

**The Dutch, English and French: companies and colonies.** The northern powers entered after 1600 through **chartered companies** that combined trade, war and government. The **VOC** (1602) seized Batavia, drove the Portuguese from Malacca (1641) and Ceylon, monopolised nutmeg and cloves by force, and founded Cape Town (1652) as a way-station. The **English East India Company** (1600) traded at Surat and Madras and would later rule India (Unit 6). In the Atlantic, England planted **Jamestown** (1607) and New England, and took Jamaica (1655); France settled **Quebec** (1608) and the fur trade, then Saint-Domingue and Louisiana; the Dutch held New Amsterdam briefly and sugar Brazil briefly. Their colonies were of three kinds: trading posts, plantation islands worked by enslaved Africans, and settler societies in North America.

**Who kept trading.** European intrusion did not end Asian commerce. Gujarati, Chinese, Japanese and Arab merchants continued; the **Omani** sultanate took Muscat (1650) and Mombasa (1698) from Portugal and ran the Swahili trade; the Mughals and Ming ignored the sea powers or taxed them. Europeans dominated the Atlantic because they had made it; in the Indian Ocean they were one more set of armed merchants until the eighteenth century.

## Primary sources to read closely

**Las Casas, *A Brief Account of the Destruction of the Indies* (1552; English 1689).** "...that of Three Millions of Persons, which lived in Hispaniola itself, there is at present but the inconsiderable remnant of scarce Three Hundred." Of the encomienda labour: "they sent the Males to the Mines to dig and bring away the Gold, which is an intollerable labor; but the Women they made use of to Manure and Till the ground ... The Men died in Mines, hunger starved and oppressed with labor, and the Women perished in the Fields". *Sourcing:* a Dominican friar writing to the Spanish king to end the encomienda; his numbers are inflated for effect and his purpose is reform, not census. *Use:* evidence of labour abuse and depopulation; the origin of the "Black Legend" used by Spain's Protestant enemies, which is why the 1689 English edition exists.

**Bernal Díaz, *True History of the Conquest of New Spain* (described).** An old soldier's memoir written decades later: the wonder of the causeway into Tenochtitlan, the Tlaxcalan alliance, the massacre and retreat of the Noche Triste, the siege and the smallpox. *Use:* evidence for Indigenous allies and disease as causes of the conquest; written to claim credit for the ordinary conquistadors against Cortés's official historians.

**Cieza de León, *Chronicle of Peru* (described).** Roads, bridges, storehouses, the mit'a and the Inca census, recorded by a Spaniard impressed by what his countrymen had destroyed.

## Common mistakes

- **"Europeans conquered the Americas because they were superior."** They won because of disease, Indigenous allies, and steel and horses against a Stone Age arsenal; and they did not conquer the Indian Ocean at all.
- **"The exchange was one way."** Maize, potatoes and cassava changed Afro-Eurasia as much as horses and smallpox changed the Americas.
- **"Portugal ruled the Indian Ocean."** It taxed a share of its trade from a chain of forts and lost most of them to the Dutch within 150 years.
- **"Potatoes came from Ireland."** From the Andes.
