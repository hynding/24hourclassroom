## What you need to be able to do

- **7.7** Explain similarities and differences in how governments conducted the Second World War: total mobilisation (economies, women, colonial subjects), new technologies (radar, aircraft carriers, strategic bombing, the atomic bomb), propaganda and ideology (fascist, communist and liberal states all used them), and the repression of civilians (internment, forced labour, the targeting of cities).
- **7.8** Explain the causes and consequences of mass atrocities in the twentieth century: the Armenian genocide (1915–17), the Holocaust (1941–45), the Cambodian genocide (1975–79), the Rwandan genocide (1994), and ethnic violence in Bosnia (1992–95), with the role of racial ideology, war, state power and the failure of outsiders to act.
- **7.9** Explain the relative significance of the causes of global conflict in the period 1900–1945: the unit's causation review, from the collapse of old regimes through two world wars.

In plain language: know how 1939–45 was fought and how it differed from 1914–18; know five genocides with dates, perpetrators, victims and the reasoning behind each; and be ready to argue which causes of the century's wars mattered most.

| Armenian genocide (1915–17) | The Ottoman government's deportation and murder of its Armenian population during the First World War; about a million dead. |
| Holocaust | The Nazi murder of about six million European Jews, with Roma, disabled people, Poles, Soviet prisoners and others; the death camps of 1942–45. |
| Nuremberg trials (1945–46) | The trial of Nazi leaders by the four Allied powers for crimes against peace, war crimes and crimes against humanity. |
| Total war (1939–45) | Mobilisation on a scale beyond 1914–18: about 70 million dead, most of them civilians. |

## How the war was fought (7.7)

**The course of the war in one paragraph.** Germany took Poland (1939), Denmark, Norway, the Low Countries and France (1940), and failed to defeat Britain in the air (Battle of Britain). Italy attacked Greece and British Egypt; Germany invaded the Balkans and then, on 22 June 1941, the Soviet Union, reaching Moscow's suburbs before winter. Japan, at war with China since 1937 and cut off from American oil, attacked Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941 and in six months took the Philippines, Malaya, Singapore, Burma and the Dutch East Indies. The turning points came in 1942–43: Midway (June 1942), El Alamein (October 1942), Stalingrad (ended February 1943). The Allies invaded Italy (1943) and Normandy (June 1944) while the Red Army, which did most of the fighting against Germany, drove west to Berlin (May 1945). American forces crossed the Pacific island by island; after the atomic bombs and the Soviet declaration of war, Japan surrendered on 15 August 1945.

**Total mobilisation.** Every belligerent organised its economy for war. The United States built about 300,000 aircraft and became the "arsenal of democracy", supplying Britain and the Soviet Union under Lend-Lease; the Soviet Union moved whole factories east of the Urals and outproduced Germany in tanks; Britain rationed and conscripted women into industry and the services; Germany used about 12 million forced labourers from occupied Europe; Japan mobilised its empire's rice, rubber and oil. Colonial troops fought again: about 2.5 million Indians (the largest volunteer army in history), Africans in Burma and Italy, Algerians and Senegalese in the French armies, and Australians, Canadians and New Zealanders throughout. Women served in factories, farms, the Soviet front line and resistance movements.

**Technology.** Radar, sonar, the aircraft carrier (the decisive weapon of the Pacific), long-range bombers, the jet engine and the V-2 rocket, penicillin, code-breaking (Enigma at Bletchley Park) and finally the atomic bomb, built by the Manhattan Project. **Strategic bombing** made cities the target: the German Blitz on Britain, the Allied firestorms at Hamburg (1943), Dresden (February 1945) and Tokyo (March 1945, about 100,000 dead in a night). President Truman announced on 6 August 1945: "Sixteen hours ago an American airplane dropped one bomb on Hiroshima, an important Japanese Army base. That bomb had more power than 20,000 tons of T.N.T." About 80,000 died at once in Hiroshima and 40,000 at Nagasaki; many more later.

**Ideology and propaganda.** Fascist, communist and liberal states all mobilised belief. Roosevelt and Churchill issued the **Atlantic Charter** (14 August 1941), declaring that their countries "seek no aggrandizement, territorial or other" and that "they respect the right of all peoples to choose the form of government under which they will live". Roosevelt told Congress on 8 December 1941 that the previous day was "a date which will live in infamy". Stalin invoked Russian patriotism and the Orthodox Church in the "Great Patriotic War"; Hitler fought a self-declared race war in the east; Japan promised a "Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere". Every state censored, interned enemy aliens (about 120,000 Japanese Americans in the United States) and used film and radio.

**Repression of civilians.** The war killed more civilians than soldiers: the siege of Leningrad (about a million dead), the Bengal famine of 1943 (about three million), the occupation of Poland and the Soviet Union, the Rape of Nanjing (1937), forced labour and "comfort women" in Japan's empire, the deportations of whole peoples inside the Soviet Union.

| | First World War | Second World War |
|---|---|---|
| Deaths | about 10 million soldiers, several million civilians | about 70 million, the majority civilians |
| Front | static trenches in the west | mobile fronts on every continent and ocean |
| Decisive technology | machine gun, artillery, gas | tank, carrier, bomber, radar, atomic bomb |
| Civilians | blockade, some bombing | strategic bombing, genocide, occupation, famine |
| End | armistice and negotiated treaties | unconditional surrender and occupation |

## Mass atrocities (7.8)

**Armenia.** In April 1915 the Young Turk government, fighting Russia and fearing Armenian disloyalty, deported the Armenian population of Anatolia toward the Syrian desert; men were shot, women and children marched without food. About a million died. Wartime conditions, the CUP's Turkish nationalism and the breakdown of a multinational empire combined. The U.S. ambassador Henry Morgenthau reported the deportations to Washington at the time, and the postwar Ottoman courts-martial of 1919–20 convicted some organisers in absentia; the Turkish Republic has denied the genocide since.

**The Holocaust.** Nazi racial doctrine made Jews the enemy of the German people; persecution began in 1933 (the Nuremberg Laws of 1935, Kristallnacht in 1938), became ghettoisation and mass shooting with the invasion of Poland and the Soviet Union (the Einsatzgruppen killed over a million in 1941–42), and was industrialised after the Wannsee conference (January 1942) in death camps at Auschwitz, Treblinka, Sobibor and others. About six million Jews, two-thirds of Europe's Jewish population, were murdered, with Roma, disabled people, Soviet prisoners, Polish elites, homosexuals and political prisoners. Collaborators across Europe took part; a few states (Denmark, Bulgaria) and many individuals resisted. At Nuremberg the American prosecutor Robert Jackson opened the case in November 1945: "The wrongs which we seek to condemn and punish have been so calculated, so malignant, and so devastating, that civilization cannot tolerate their being ignored, because it cannot survive their being repeated." The trials established that leaders could be tried for crimes against humanity; the UN adopted the Genocide Convention in 1948.

**After 1945.** The Khmer Rouge under Pol Pot emptied Cambodia's cities and killed about 1.7 million (a fifth of the population) in 1975–79 through execution, starvation and forced labour, targeting educated people, minorities and anyone linked to the old regime. In Rwanda in 1994 the Hutu-led government and militias killed about 800,000 Tutsi and moderate Hutu in a hundred days while the UN force withdrew. In Bosnia (1992–95) Serb forces expelled and murdered Bosnian Muslims, including about 8,000 men and boys at Srebrenica (1995). The pattern across cases: a state in crisis or at war, an ideology that defines a group as a threat, prior exclusion and propaganda, and the indifference of outsiders.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Armenian | 1915–17 | Ottoman CUP government | about 1 million Armenians | world war; Turkish nationalism; imperial collapse |
| Holocaust | 1941–45 | Nazi Germany and collaborators | about 6 million Jews, and others | race ideology; war in the east; state bureaucracy |
| Cambodia | 1975–79 | Khmer Rouge | about 1.7 million | revolutionary utopianism; Vietnam War aftermath |
| Rwanda | 1994 | Hutu government and militias | about 800,000 Tutsi and moderate Hutu | colonial ethnic categories; civil war; radio propaganda |
| Bosnia | 1992–95 | Bosnian Serb forces | tens of thousands, Srebrenica 8,000 | Yugoslav collapse; nationalism |

## Causation in global conflict (7.9)

The unit asks you to weigh causes. A workable hierarchy:

1. **Structural:** industrial technology that made mass killing possible; the nationalism and imperialism of the nineteenth century; the collapse of multinational empires.
2. **Intermediate:** the failed settlement of 1919; the Depression; the ideologies that grew in it.
3. **Immediate:** the July Crisis; the invasion of Poland; Pearl Harbor.

Consequences to name: about 80 million dead in the two wars; the end of the European empires' legitimacy; the United States and the Soviet Union as superpowers; the United Nations; the atomic age; the moral vocabulary of genocide and human rights.

## Common mistakes

- **Thinking the Holocaust began in 1939 or 1942.** Persecution began in 1933; the shift to murder came with the war in the east in 1941.
- **Forgetting the Eastern Front and China.** Most German soldiers died fighting the Soviet Union; China tied down a million Japanese troops.
- **Treating genocide as spontaneous hatred.** Every case required a state, an ideology and organisation.
- **Writing 7.9 as a list.** The exam wants causes ranked and linked, with a reason for the ranking.
