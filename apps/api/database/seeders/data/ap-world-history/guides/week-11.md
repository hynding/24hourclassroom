## What you need to be able to do

- **4.1** Explain how cross-cultural interactions resulted in the diffusion of technology and facilitated changes in patterns of trade and travel: Europeans adopted and adapted the **magnetic compass** (Chinese), the **astrolabe** (Islamic), the **lateen sail** (Indian Ocean), **lateen-rigged caravels**, and the Chinese-derived **stern-post rudder**; improved cartography and knowledge of **wind patterns** (the volta do mar, the trade winds and westerlies) made oceanic sailing possible; new ship types (the **caravel**, the **carrack**, the Dutch **fluyt**) suited trade and exploration.
- **4.2** Explain the causes and events of European exploration: **state sponsorship** of voyages by Portugal, Spain, France, England and the Netherlands, driven by desire for wealth (spices, gold), religious motives (Christian expansion, the Reconquista spirit), and competition between states; **Portugal's** trading-post empire along Africa and into the Indian Ocean (Dias 1488, da Gama 1498); **Spain's** sponsorship of Columbus (1492) and Magellan (1519–1522); the **Northern Europeans'** search for a northwest passage and their later entry; the **Treaty of Tordesillas**.

In plain language: what did Europeans borrow, what did they build, why did their monarchs pay for ships, and who went where first.

|---|---|

## Borrowed technology (4.1)

Nothing Europeans sailed with in 1450 was wholly their own. The **compass** came from Song China through the Indian Ocean and Islamic worlds; the **astrolabe** was an ancient Greek instrument refined by Islamic astronomers for finding latitude; the **lateen sail** was the rig of the Indian Ocean dhow; the **stern-post rudder** was Chinese. Arabic and Jewish cartographers in Majorca drew the first good **portolan charts**; Ptolemy's *Geography*, recovered through Islamic and Byzantine copies, gave Europeans a grid of latitude and longitude. What Europeans added was combination and purpose. The Portuguese **caravel** put lateen sails on a small, strong hull that could beat into the wind and explore coasts and rivers; the **carrack** mixed square sails for speed with lateen for handling and carried the cannon that Unit 3 had made decisive. Decades of sailing down the African coast taught the **volta do mar**: that a ship could not fight the northeast trade winds home but could swing out into the Atlantic and ride the **westerlies**. That discovery of the Atlantic wind system, more than any instrument, made the ocean crossable. The **Dutch fluyt** of the 1590s was the last innovation: a cheap, capacious cargo ship needing few sailors, which made Dutch freight the cheapest in Europe.

The CED's point is diffusion. Technologies developed in Asia and the Islamic world, transmitted by the networks of Unit 2, were adopted, adapted and combined by Europeans at the western edge of Afro-Eurasia, who then used them to join the ocean system that had produced them.

## Why they sailed (4.2)

**Wealth.** Spices, silk and porcelain reached Europe through Venetian and Muslim middlemen at prices that made a direct route worth any risk. Gold from West Africa reached Europe across the Sahara; Portugal wanted it at the source. **Religion.** Portugal and Spain had completed centuries of war against Muslim states (the Reconquista; Granada fell in 1492, the year Columbus sailed) and carried the crusading idea to sea: converting the heathen, outflanking Islam, finding the legendary Christian king Prester John. **State competition.** Monarchs who had just built the gunpowder states of Unit 3 wanted revenue and glory; a successful voyage cost less than a war. Portugal, small and poor, began; Spain followed to compete; France, England and the Netherlands joined when the first two had shown the way. **Knowledge.** Renaissance geography, the printing press and Polo's book made the goal thinkable.

**Portugal.** Prince **Henry** sponsored voyages down the African coast from the 1420s: Madeira and the Azores (sugar, the Atlantic model), the Senegal and Gambia (gold and enslaved people), São Tomé. **Dias** rounded the Cape in 1488; **da Gama** reached **Calicut** in 1498, guided across from Malindi by a pilot who knew the monsoon, and found a trading world that wanted nothing he carried: the Calicut ruler, da Gama's journal records, was unimpressed with his gifts of cloth and hats. Portugal returned with cannon. **Spain.** Isabella funded **Columbus** in 1492; he reached the Bahamas, Cuba and Hispaniola and reported to the crown that the people there "have no iron, nor steel, nor weapons" and that he could send "as much gold as they desire ... and as many slaves as they choose to send for, all heathens" (Letter to Sant Angel, 1493, 1892 English). The **Treaty of Tordesillas** (1494) divided the world: a line "three hundred and seventy leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands", everything east "pertain forever to, the said King of Portugal", everything west to "the said King and Queen of Castile" (Davenport's translation, 1917). The line gave Brazil to Portugal and the rest of the Americas to Spain. **Magellan**'s fleet (1519–1522) crossed the Pacific and proved the earth could be circled; one ship of five came home. **Northern Europe.** Cabot (1497), Cartier (1534), Hudson (1609) and others sought a **northwest passage** to Asia and found fish, furs and the St Lawrence instead; England, France and the Netherlands turned to settlement and trading companies in the 1600s (week 12).

## Primary sources to read closely

**Columbus, Letter to Luis de Sant Angel (1493; 1892 English).** "They have no iron, nor steel, nor weapons, nor are they fit for them, because although they are well-made men of commanding stature, they appear extraordinarily timid. ... they are so unsuspicious and so generous with what they possess, that no one who had not seen it would believe it. They never refuse anything that is asked for." And at the close: "I can give them as much gold as they desire, if they will give me a little assistance, ... and as many slaves as they choose to send for, all heathens."

*Sourcing:* a letter to the royal treasurer by a man who needed a second voyage funded; every observation is an argument for investment. *Use:* evidence for European motives (gold, slaves, converts), for the first European view of the Taino, and for the gap between what Columbus saw and what he promised.

**Treaty of Tordesillas (1494; Davenport, 1917).** The line "three hundred and seventy leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands", lands to the east "pertain forever to, the said King of Portugal", lands to the west "to, the said King and Queen of Castile". *Use:* state competition as a cause; the assumption that two monarchs and a pope could divide the world; the accident that put Brazil on Portugal's side.

**Da Gama's journal (described).** The anonymous account of the 1497–1499 voyage records the Muslim pilot taken on at Malindi, the arrival at Calicut, the Zamorin's indifference to Portuguese gifts, and the hostility of Muslim merchants who saw the newcomers for what they were. *Use:* the Indian Ocean as an established system Europeans entered, not created.

## Common mistakes

- **"Europeans invented the compass and astrolabe."** They borrowed both; the caravel and the volta do mar were their contributions.
- **"Columbus proved the earth was round."** Educated Europeans knew that; he underestimated its size.
- **"Religion was a pretext."** For Portugal and Spain, fresh from the Reconquista, it was a motive alongside gold.
- **"Tordesillas divided the Americas."** It divided the world along one line, which is why Portugal held Brazil and claimed Africa and Asia.
- **"Da Gama conquered India."** He reached a port, was rebuffed, and came back with guns.
