# Week 21 · Global Economic Development and Economic Imperialism

## What you need to be able to do

- **6.4** Explain how various environmental factors contributed to the development of the global economy from 1750 to 1900: how colonies and other regions were reorganised to export raw materials and single cash crops (cotton, rubber, palm oil, guano, meat, coffee, tea) to industrial states, and to import their manufactured goods.
- **6.5** Explain how industrial states controlled the economies of regions they did not formally rule: the Opium Wars and the unequal treaties in China, spheres of influence, British and American investment in Latin America, and the role of debt and intervention.

In plain language: know what each region exported and to whom, what "monoculture" did to an economy, and how Britain and the United States could dominate China and Latin America without colonising them.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Export economy | An economy organised around producing a few raw materials or crops for foreign markets. |
| Cash crop / monoculture | A crop grown for sale rather than food; dependence on a single such crop (Cuban sugar, Ceylon tea, Gold Coast cocoa). |
| Economic imperialism | Control of a region's economy by foreign states or companies through trade, loans, investment and, when needed, force, without formal annexation. |
| Unequal treaties | Treaties imposed on China (from 1842), Japan (1850s) and others opening ports, fixing tariffs and granting extraterritoriality. |
| Extraterritoriality | Foreigners subject to their own consuls' courts, not local law. |
| Treaty ports | Chinese ports opened to foreign trade and residence, beginning with the five named at Nanjing. |
| Spheres of influence | Regions of China where one power held exclusive economic rights (railways, mines) after 1895. |
| Open Door policy | The U.S. proposal (1899–1900) that all powers trade equally in China and respect its territorial integrity. |
| Boxer Rebellion (1899–1901) | Anti-foreign rising in northern China crushed by an eight-nation force; the Boxer Protocol imposed a huge indemnity. |
| Guano | Seabird droppings mined on Peruvian islands as fertiliser, 1840s–70s; Peru's chief export and source of debt. |
| Roosevelt Corollary (1904) | Theodore Roosevelt's claim that the United States could exercise "international police power" in the Western Hemisphere. |
| Informal empire | Historians' term for economic domination without political rule, especially British influence in Latin America. |

## Industrial demand remakes the world's farms and mines

Factories needed raw materials in quantities the industrial countries could not grow, and railways, steamships, the Suez Canal (1869) and the telegraph made it cheap to bring them from anywhere. The result by 1900 was a world divided between a few industrial exporters of manufactured goods and many regions that exported raw materials and bought those goods. Three mechanisms created the export economies:

- **Colonial policy.** Governments forced or steered colonial producers toward cash crops: the Dutch **cultivation system** on Java (1830) required villages to grow sugar, coffee and indigo for the state; Britain pushed Indian cotton and jute and Egyptian cotton, and planted tea in Assam and Ceylon after the China trade became uncertain; the Congo Free State extracted **rubber** by terror; British Malaya planted rubber trees (from seeds smuggled out of Brazil via Kew Gardens in 1876) and mined tin.
- **Private investment.** British capital built the railways and ports of Argentina, Brazil and India, and the mines of Chile and South Africa; U.S. companies planted bananas in Central America and sugar in Cuba and Hawaii.
- **Environmental advantage.** Some regions turned climate and soil into exports with little foreign coercion: Argentine beef and wheat (refrigerated ships from the 1870s), Brazilian coffee, Peruvian guano, Chilean nitrates, West African palm oil and cocoa grown by African smallholders.

| Region | Main exports by 1900 | Main buyer or investor |
|---|---|---|
| India | cotton, jute, tea, opium (to China), wheat | Britain |
| Egypt | cotton | Britain, France |
| Java | sugar, coffee | Netherlands |
| Malaya | tin, rubber | Britain |
| Congo | rubber, ivory | Belgium (Leopold II) |
| West Africa | palm oil, cocoa, groundnuts | Britain, France, Germany |
| Argentina | beef, wheat, wool | Britain |
| Brazil | coffee, rubber (Amazon, to 1912) | Britain, United States |
| Peru, Chile | guano, nitrates, copper | Britain, later United States |
| Cuba | sugar | United States |

**Consequences.** Export agriculture brought cash, railways and ports, and some producers (Gold Coast cocoa farmers, Argentine landowners) prospered. But monoculture left economies at the mercy of one price; land went to export crops while food was imported; forests were cleared and soils exhausted; and famine followed when crops failed or prices fell, as in India in 1876–78 and 1896–1900, when grain was still exported during the famines. The Indian economist **Dadabhai Naoroji** argued in *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India* (1901) that Britain was draining India's wealth through taxes, salaries and remittances, the "drain theory" that nationalists later took up; describe his argument in your own words.

## China: the Opium Wars and the unequal treaties

Qing China ran a trade surplus with Britain in the 1700s because Britain bought tea and China wanted little in return except silver. The East India Company's solution was **opium** grown in Bengal and sold through private traders at Canton. By the 1830s silver was flowing *out* of China and addiction was spreading. In 1839 the commissioner **Lin Zexu** confiscated and destroyed about 20,000 chests of opium at Canton and wrote to Queen Victoria asking how Britain could forbid opium at home and sell it abroad; the letter apparently never reached her. Britain went to war (1839–42) and steam gunboats and modern artillery won it easily.

The **Treaty of Nanjing** (29 August 1842), the first of the unequal treaties, opened five ports and ceded an island:

> "His Majesty the Emperor of China cedes to Her Majesty the Queen of Great Britain, etc., the Island of Hongkong, to be possessed in perpetuity by Her Britannic Majesty, Her Heirs and Successors" (Article III). Article II opened "the Cities and Towns of Canton, Amoy, Foochow-fu, Ningpo, and Shanghai" to British merchants and consuls.

It also imposed a $21 million indemnity and a fixed low tariff; a supplementary treaty added **extraterritoriality** and most-favoured-nation status, which France and the United States soon copied. The Second Opium War (1856–60) ended with Anglo-French troops burning the Summer Palace outside Beijing, the legalisation of opium, and more ports. Meanwhile the **Taiping Rebellion** (1850–64), led by Hong Xiuquan, who believed himself the younger brother of Jesus, killed perhaps twenty million people and showed how weak the dynasty had become. After Japan's victory in 1895 the powers carved out **spheres of influence** with railway and mining rights (Germany in Shandong, Russia in Manchuria, Britain in the Yangzi valley, France in the south); the United States, with no sphere, proposed the **Open Door** (1899). The **Boxer** rising of 1899–1901 against foreigners and Christians was crushed by an eight-nation army, and the Boxer Protocol's indemnity of 450 million taels mortgaged Chinese customs revenue for decades. China was never a colony; it was something the CED calls economic imperialism, with sovereignty kept and control lost.

## Latin America: independence without economic independence

The new republics of the 1820s (unit 5) kept the colonial export pattern and added foreign loans. British merchants replaced Spanish ones; British banks lent to governments and built railways to carry exports to ports they also built. By 1900 Britain held more investment in Argentina than in any colony except India. Peru's **guano** boom (1840s–70s) paid for a railway programme on borrowed money; when the deposits ran out Peru defaulted (1876) and lost the nitrate provinces to Chile in the **War of the Pacific** (1879–84), after which British firms dominated Chilean nitrates. Mexico under Porfirio Díaz (1876–1911) welcomed U.S. and British capital into railways, mines and oil while rural Mexicans lost land, one cause of the revolution of 1910 (week 23).

The United States added force to finance. The **Monroe Doctrine** (1823) had warned Europeans that "we should consider any attempt on their part to extend their system to any portion of this hemisphere as dangerous to our peace and safety." In his annual message of 6 December 1904 Theodore Roosevelt turned the warning into a licence:

> "Chronic wrongdoing, or an impotence which results in a general loosening of the ties of civilized society, may in America, as elsewhere, ultimately require intervention by some civilized nation, and in the Western Hemisphere the adherence of the United States to the Monroe Doctrine may force the United States, however reluctantly, in flagrant cases of such wrongdoing or impotence, to the exercise of an international police power."

The occasion was European debt collection in Venezuela and the Dominican Republic; the result was U.S. customs receiverships, marine landings (Cuba, Nicaragua, Haiti, the Dominican Republic) and the Panama Canal (1904–14) on territory detached from Colombia in 1903. The **United Fruit Company** (1899) owned plantations, railways and ports across Central America: "banana republics" were the result.

## Comparison: three kinds of economic subjection

| | Formal colony (India, Java) | Semi-colony (China) | Independent but dependent (Argentina, Peru) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Political control | foreign government | own government, limited by treaties and spheres | own government |
| Economic lever | colonial policy, forced crops, taxes | unequal treaties, tariffs fixed, extraterritoriality, indemnities | loans, foreign-owned railways and mines, trade |
| Force used | conquest and garrisons | Opium Wars, 1900 expedition | debt collection, U.S. interventions |
| Resistance | 1857, Maji Maji | Taiping, Boxers | nationalist economics after 1910 |

## Common mistakes

- **Saying China was colonised.** Hong Kong was ceded; the rest of China kept its emperor. The point of 6.5 is control *without* annexation.
- **Forgetting the environment.** 6.4 is an ENV topic: cash crops changed land use, cleared forests, exhausted soils and exposed regions to famine.
- **Treating Latin America as colonised by Britain.** Britain never ruled Argentina; the dependence was financial and commercial, the historians' "informal empire".
- **Ignoring local agency.** West African cocoa and palm oil were grown by African farmers who chose to enter the market; Argentine landowners profited from British railways.
