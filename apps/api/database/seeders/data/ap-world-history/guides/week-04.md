## How rulers gained and kept power: four methods, many examples

The CED's organising question for 1.7 is *how* states were built. Across the six regions, rulers used four tools in different mixes.

**1. Bureaucracy and law.** Song China is the extreme case: a salaried, examined, rotated bureaucracy that reached every county, backed by a Confucian ideology of obedience. The Inca built something comparable without writing: provincial governors, resettlement, Quechua, storehouses and quipu accounts. Islamic states relied on sharia courts and qadis (judges) who could be hired anywhere; Mali appointed governors and relied on Muslim scribes. European kings had the smallest bureaucracies in 1200 and were building them by 1450.

**2. Military elites and their rewards.** Feudal Europe paid knights in land (fiefs). Islamic states paid Turkic soldiers in land grants or in the plunder of conquest; the Mamluks turned a slave army into a ruling class. Japan's samurai, Mali's cavalry, the Aztec warrior nobility rewarded with tribute: every state needed soldiers and had to find something to give them.

**3. Religion as legitimacy.** Nearly every ruler claimed a religious sanction: the Chinese emperor's Mandate of Heaven; the sultan as defender of Islam; the Khmer and Majapahit kings as Hindu-Buddhist god-kings; the Inca as son of the sun; the Aztec tlatoani feeding the sun with sacrifice; Ethiopia's Solomonic descent; European kings anointed by the Church. What differed was whether the religion had its own powerful institution (the Catholic Church, the ulama) that could push back.

**4. Trade and tribute.** Sea-based and Sahelian states lived by taxing routes (Srivijaya, Malacca, Kilwa, Mali). Land empires taxed farmers (Song, Khmer, Delhi) or demanded labour (Inca) or goods (Aztec). The source of revenue shaped the form of the state: taxing a trade route needs a port and a fleet, taxing peasants needs a bureaucracy, collecting tribute needs an army.

## Why forms differed

| Form | Examples | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Centralised empire | Song China, Inca, Mali (loosely), Delhi Sultanate | Large agricultural base; strong ideology; roads or canals; a professional class of officials or soldiers |
| Tribute network | Aztec Triple Alliance | Many city-states already existed; cheaper to tax than to govern |
| City-states | Swahili coast, Hausa, Italian cities, Maya | Wealth from trade concentrated in ports; no need or means to rule a hinterland |
| Feudal decentralisation | Europe c. 1200, Japan under the shogunate | Weak kings, strong local lords, defence organised locally |
| Religious-commercial confederation | Dar al-Islam as a whole | Shared law and faith without a shared ruler after the Abbasid collapse |

## Continuity and change across the period

- **Continuities:** Confucian government in China survived the Mongol conquest; Islamic law and scholarship survived the loss of Baghdad; Hinduism remained India's majority faith under Muslim rule; the Church spanned Europe throughout.
- **Changes:** Turkic peoples replaced Arabs as rulers of Islam's heartland; Islam reached Southeast Asia and deeper into Africa; European kings grew stronger and serfdom weakened in the west; the Aztec and Inca empires rose after 1400; Angkor, Srivijaya and Cahokia declined.

## Two sources side by side

**Marco Polo on the Great Khan's money (1903 edition):** "nobody, however important he may think himself, dares to refuse them on pain of death." **Magna Carta (1215):** "No scutage not aid shall be imposed on our kingdom, unless by common counsel of our kingdom." One shows a ruler who could command the currency of a continent; the other shows a king who could not tax without his barons' consent. Both are about the same thing, the reach of state power, and together they frame the unit's comparison: China's centralised state against Europe's negotiated one. Remember that each has a purpose: Polo wanted to astonish, the barons wanted to protect themselves.

## Historical thinking: the comparison essay

The LEQ comparison rubric rewards three moves. **Make the claim evaluative:** not "they were similar and different" but "they shared religious legitimacy but differed fundamentally in administration, because ...". **Organise by point of comparison**, not by state: a paragraph on legitimacy covering both, a paragraph on administration covering both. **Explain the reason for the difference:** geography, economy, prior history. The example below is built for topic 1.7.

**Prompt:** *Compare the methods used by two states in different regions to maintain control over their territories, c. 1200–1450.*

**Sketch:** Thesis: Song China and the Mali Empire both depended on a literate religious-philosophical elite and on taxing a productive economy, but China governed through a centralised examined bureaucracy while Mali ruled a looser empire through provincial governors, cavalry and control of trade routes, differences explained by China's dense agriculture and ancient state tradition against Mali's dispersed population and recent formation. Context: both faced nomadic or desert neighbours; both were integrated into long-distance trade. Point 1 (ideology): Confucian examinations versus Islamic scholarship and law. Point 2 (administration): salaried officials rotated by the court versus governors and tributary kings. Point 3 (revenue): agricultural taxes and paper money versus tolls on gold and salt. Complexity: both states' methods had limits: China fell to the Mongols in 1279, Mali fragmented in the 1400s as provinces broke away.

## Common mistakes

- **Listing instead of comparing.** A paragraph about China followed by a paragraph about Mali is not a comparison until a sentence links them.
- **Comparing unlike things.** Compare state with state, religion's role with religion's role; do not compare China's economy with Europe's religion.
- **Forgetting "why".** The top essays explain the reason for a difference.
- **Assuming Europe was advanced.** In 1200 Europe was the least centralised and least commercialised of the six regions; its rise comes later.
