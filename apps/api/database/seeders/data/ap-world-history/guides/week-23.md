## What you need to be able to do

- **7.1** Explain how internal and external factors contributed to change in various states after 1900: the Mexican Revolution (1910), the fall of the Qing and the founding of the Chinese republic (1911–12), the Russian Revolution of 1917 and the Bolshevik seizure of power, and the Young Turk revolution (1908) and the end of the Ottoman Empire.
- **7.2** Explain the causes and consequences of World War I: the alliance system, militarism and the arms race, imperial rivalry, nationalism (especially in the Balkans), and the assassination at Sarajevo as the immediate trigger.

In plain language: for each of four collapsing states, be able to name the old regime's weakness, the revolution's leaders and the new state's character; for 1914, be able to rank the long-term causes and explain how a Balkan assassination became a world war within five weeks.

## Four states transformed (7.1)

**Mexico.** Porfirio Díaz gave Mexico thirty-five years of order and growth on foreign capital (week 21): railways, mines and oil owned abroad, haciendas swallowing village land, a rural population pushed toward peonage. In 1910 Francisco Madero's challenge to another rigged election set off risings led by Emiliano Zapata in the south (land for villages) and Pancho Villa in the north; Díaz fled in 1911. A decade of civil war followed among revolutionary factions, with U.S. intervention at Veracruz (1914) and against Villa (1916). The **Constitution of 1917** declared the nation's ownership of land and subsoil resources (Article 27), limited the Church, and guaranteed labour rights: a revolution that was nationalist, agrarian and anticlerical rather than socialist, and the first great revolution of the twentieth century.

**China.** The Qing survived the Taiping and the Boxers but not the humiliation of the Boxer Protocol and Japan's victory over Russia in 1905, which showed an Asian constitutional state could beat a European empire. Late reforms (abolition of the examination system in 1905, provincial assemblies) came too late. An army mutiny at Wuchang in October 1911 spread province by province; the child emperor Puyi abdicated in February 1912, ending two thousand years of imperial rule. **Sun Yat-sen**, whose Three Principles called for nationalism, democracy and the people's livelihood, became provisional president, but handed power to the general Yuan Shikai, who tried to make himself emperor; after his death in 1916 China fell to regional warlords. The May Fourth Movement (1919), a protest against the Versailles award of Shandong to Japan, revived nationalism and produced both the reorganised Guomindang and, in 1921, the Chinese Communist Party (week 25).

**The Ottoman Empire.** A century of losses (Greece, Serbia, Romania, Bulgaria, Egypt, Tunisia, Bosnia) and foreign financial control (the Public Debt Administration, 1881) discredited the sultan's absolutism. In 1908 the **Young Turks** of the Committee of Union and Progress forced Abdulhamid II to restore the constitution of 1876; they soon ruled as a nationalist, modernising and increasingly Turkish-centred dictatorship. Italy took Libya (1911–12) and the Balkan states took almost all of Ottoman Europe (1912–13). The CUP's decision to join Germany in 1914 ended in defeat, the Armenian genocide (week 26) and the empire's partition; **Mustafa Kemal (Atatürk)** founded the Turkish Republic in 1923 (week 25).

**Russia.** Defeat by Japan and the shooting of petitioners on Bloody Sunday (January 1905) brought strikes, mutinies and peasant risings; Nicholas II granted a Duma in the October Manifesto and then hollowed it out. Industrialization had created a working class in Petrograd and Moscow with few rights, and the countryside still wanted the nobles' land. The strain of the First World War (defeats, food shortages, inflation) broke the regime: in February 1917 bread riots and a garrison mutiny in Petrograd forced the tsar's abdication; a Provisional Government shared power with soviets (councils) of workers and soldiers and chose to stay in the war. In October **Lenin**'s Bolsheviks, promising "peace, land and bread", seized power in Petrograd, took Russia out of the war at Brest-Litovsk (March 1918) and won a civil war (1918–21) against the Whites and foreign interventions. The world's first communist state had been born from the war that 7.2 explains.

## Why the great powers went to war (7.2)

Historians still argue about weight, but the exam expects you to know five long-term causes and one trigger, usually remembered as MAIN plus the assassination.

**Militarism.** The German and French armies grew through conscription; Britain and Germany raced to build dreadnought battleships after 1906; general staffs drew up mobilisation timetables (Germany's Schlieffen Plan required attacking France through Belgium before turning on Russia) that turned any crisis into a race to mobilise first.

**Alliances.** Bismarck's system of isolating France broke down after 1890: Germany and Austria-Hungary (with Italy) formed the Triple Alliance (1882); France and Russia allied (1894); Britain settled its differences with France (1904) and Russia (1907) in the Triple Entente. Two blocs meant a local war could become general.

**Imperialism.** Rivalries over Morocco (1905, 1911), the Balkans and the Ottoman inheritance, and the sense in Berlin that Germany had been denied its "place in the sun", poisoned relations; the scramble for Africa had been managed, but the habit of competition remained.

**Nationalism.** French desire to recover Alsace-Lorraine; pan-Slav sympathy in Russia for Serbia; Serbian ambition to unite South Slavs, which threatened the multinational Austro-Hungarian Empire; and the mass politics of the press, which made backing down look like humiliation.

**The Balkans.** Ottoman retreat left a vacuum. Austria annexed Bosnia in 1908; Serbia doubled in size in the Balkan Wars of 1912–13; Austria resolved to crush Serbia at the next opportunity.

**The trigger and the July Crisis.** On 28 June 1914 Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb trained by a Serbian nationalist society, shot Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo. Germany gave Austria a "blank cheque"; Austria's ultimatum to Serbia (23 July) was designed to be refused; Austria declared war on 28 July; Russia mobilised to support Serbia; Germany declared war on Russia (1 August) and France (3 August) and invaded Belgium; Britain declared war on 4 August. Five weeks from assassination to general war, because the alliances, the mobilisation plans and the fear of losing face left no one willing to stop.

**Consequences in brief.** Four empires (German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian, Ottoman) fell; the United States and Japan rose; the war's course and its treaties are weeks 24 and 25.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the assassination as the cause.** It was the trigger; the exam wants the long-term causes and the mechanism (alliances and mobilisation) that turned a Balkan murder into a world war.
- **Confusing the two Russian revolutions.** February ended the tsar; October brought the Bolsheviks. The Provisional Government's decision to stay in the war is the link.
- **Calling the Mexican Revolution communist.** It was agrarian, nationalist and anticlerical; the Constitution of 1917 is its measure.
- **Forgetting the Ottomans and China.** 7.1 is global; Russia is one of four cases.
