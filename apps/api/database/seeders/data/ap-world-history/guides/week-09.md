## What you need to be able to do

- **3.2** Explain how rulers used a variety of methods to legitimise and consolidate their power: **bureaucratic elites and military professionals** (the Ottoman devshirme, salaried samurai in Tokugawa Japan, Chinese examination graduates, European standing armies); **religious ideas, art and monumental architecture** (the divine right of kings, the Qing emperors' portraits and patronage of Buddhism, Songhai's promotion of Islam, Ottoman and Safavid mosques, the Taj Mahal, Versailles, the Forbidden City, Inca sun temples); and **tax-collection systems** (Mughal zamindars, Ottoman tax farming, the Ming collection of taxes in silver, Mexica tribute lists, Russian serfdom and poll taxes) to generate revenue for expansion.

In plain language: every ruler had to answer three questions: who serves me, why should they obey me, and how do I pay for it? Know each empire's answer.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Legitimacy | The accepted right to rule; claimed through religion, lineage, law, ceremony and buildings. |
| Devshirme | Ottoman levy of Christian boys for the janissaries and the palace administration; a bureaucratic elite owing everything to the sultan. |
| Salaried samurai | Under the Tokugawa (from 1603) the samurai were paid in rice stipends and kept at the lord's castle town, turning warriors into administrators. |
| Daimyo; sankin-kotai | Japanese regional lords; the rule that they alternate residence in Edo and leave families there as hostages. |
| Zamindar | Mughal landholder responsible for collecting land tax from peasants and passing a share to the state. |
| Tax farming | Selling the right to collect a tax to a private bidder who pays the state up front and keeps what he collects above that; used by the Ottomans and others. |
| Single Whip reform | Ming consolidation of taxes into a single payment in silver (1570s), tying China's revenue to the world's silver supply. |
| Divine right of kings | The European doctrine that monarchs are appointed by God and answerable only to Him (James I, Bossuet for Louis XIV). |
| Versailles | Louis XIV's palace (from 1682) where the French nobility was kept at court, dependent and watched. |
| Forbidden City | Ming and Qing palace complex in Beijing, the ceremonial centre of the Son of Heaven. |
| Taj Mahal | Shah Jahan's marble mausoleum for his wife (1632–1653), the summit of Mughal architecture and a statement of imperial wealth. |
| Qing imperial portraits | Paintings of the emperors (Kangxi, Qianlong) as Confucian scholar, Manchu warrior and Buddhist patron, addressing each audience of the empire. |
| Intendants | Louis XIV's salaried royal officials in the provinces, bypassing the nobility. |
| Serfdom (Russia) | Peasants legally bound to the land and their lords, formalised in 1649; the labour and tax base of the Russian state. |

## Who serves the ruler: bureaucracies and armies

Every empire needed officials and soldiers whose loyalty was to the centre. The Ottomans built both out of the **devshirme**: boys taken from Balkan Christian villages, converted, educated in the palace school, and sent either to the **janissaries** or into administration, where the ablest became grand viziers. Because they were legally the sultan's slaves and barred from founding noble families, they were a counterweight to the Turkish aristocracy. Shah Abbas copied the method with Caucasian captives. The **Mughals** ranked every officer in a numbered hierarchy (**mansabdars**) paid from assigned revenues; Akbar deliberately mixed Persians, Central Asians, Indian Muslims and Hindu Rajputs so that no group could dominate. **China** kept the examination system under both Ming and Qing; the Qing added Manchu quotas and a dual appointment system so that a Han official and a Manchu colleague watched each other, while the **banner** armies remained a hereditary Manchu military caste. In **Tokugawa Japan** (after 1603) the samurai, once landed warriors, became **salaried** retainers living in castle towns on rice stipends, forbidden to farm or trade, and the **daimyo** were made to spend alternate years in Edo (**sankin-kotai**), leaving their families as hostages; peace turned the warrior class into bureaucrats. **Louis XIV** of France ruled through salaried **intendants** in the provinces and a standing army of unprecedented size, while **Peter the Great** forced Russian nobles to serve in the army or bureaucracy according to a Table of Ranks (1722). The common pattern: salaried servants dependent on the ruler replaced hereditary lords with their own power bases.

## Why they obey: religion, art and buildings

Power needed a story. In Europe the story was the **divine right of kings**: Louis XIV's bishop Bossuet argued that the king's authority came from God; Louis called himself the Sun King and built **Versailles**, where ritual, from his morning rising to his supper, organised the nobility around his person and turned former rebels into courtiers competing for the honour of handing him his shirt. The **Ottomans** claimed the title of caliph and the guardianship of Mecca and Medina, and built imperial mosques (the Süleymaniye) that announced Islam's champion. The **Safavids** made **Shia Islam** the identity of the state and made **Isfahan** its showcase: the great square, the Shah Mosque, the bazaar. The **Mughals** built to overawe: the Red Forts, Fatehpur Sikri, and the **Taj Mahal**, which told every visitor what the emperor could afford; Akbar also used religious inclusiveness (week 10) to bind Hindu subjects. The **Qing** emperors sat in the **Forbidden City** as Confucian Sons of Heaven, patronised Tibetan Buddhism to bind Mongols and Tibetans, commissioned **portraits** of themselves in each guise, and kept Manchu dress and language to remind the Han who ruled. **Songhai's** Askia Muhammad made the pilgrimage and patronised Timbuktu's scholars to legitimise a usurpation. In Russia the tsar was the Orthodox protector, Moscow the "Third Rome" after Constantinople's fall; Peter later subordinated the Church to a state department. The **Inca** sun temples and the Aztec Templo Mayor, though pre-1450 in origin, are the CED's reminder that the same logic operated in the Americas.

Machiavelli, writing in Florence in 1513, gave the unsentimental version of this topic. Asking "whether it be better to be loved than feared or feared than loved", he answered that "it is much safer to be feared than loved, when, of the two, either must be dispensed with", because men are "ungrateful, fickle, false, cowardly, covetous"; but "a prince ought to inspire fear in such a way that, if he does not win love, he avoids hatred" (*The Prince*, ch. XVII, Marriott translation, 1908). The sultans, shahs and tsars of this unit practised what he preached: ceremony to win awe, salaried servants to be feared, and care not to be hated by those who paid.

## How they pay: taxation

Gunpowder armies, palaces and bureaucracies cost more than any medieval state had raised, and each empire reorganised revenue. The **Mughals** under Akbar's minister Todar Mal surveyed the land, fixed assessments on measured fields and expected cash payment; local **zamindars** collected from peasants and kept a share, becoming a hereditary rural elite that later resisted the centre. The **Ottomans**, as the timar system decayed, turned to **tax farming**: the state sold the right to collect a province's taxes to the highest bidder, gaining cash now at the price of oppression and lost control later. The **Ming** consolidated their many levies into the **Single Whip** payment in silver, which made China's revenue dependent on silver from Japan and the Americas (Unit 4). The **Qing** kept land tax low and stable to win Han consent. **Tokugawa Japan** taxed rice from peasants assessed village by village. **Russia** fixed **serfdom** by law (1649), binding the peasants who paid the tsar's poll tax and the nobles' dues, and Peter taxed everything from beards to coffins. **France** relied on the taille, from which nobles were exempt, and on sold offices, which is why the crown was always short of money. The Mexica **tribute lists** (Unit 1) show the older model: goods demanded from conquered towns. In every case the question of who paid, and who was exempt, decided the state's later health.

## Primary sources, described

**The *Ain-i-Akbari* (c. 1590).** Abu'l Fazl, Akbar's minister and chronicler, compiled a vast administrative handbook: the organisation of the household and army, the mansabdar ranks, and province-by-province revenue statistics with the assessed yield of crops. It is evidence for a state that knew its land to the field, and it is also propaganda, presenting Akbar as the perfect ruler of a perfectly ordered realm.

**Versailles.** Memoirs of the court (the duc de Saint-Simon's, written in the early 1700s) describe the king's day as public theatre: nobles paid to attend his rising and retiring, his glance was a reward, and his failure to notice a courtier was a punishment. Louis is reported to have remarked of any absent noble that he did not know him. Historians read this as the domestication of a class that had rebelled in the Fronde (1648–1653).

**The Forbidden City.** Nearly a thousand buildings on a north–south axis, entered through successive gates and courtyards that reduced the visitor and elevated the throne; the emperor faced south, and petitioners knelt. It expressed in stone the Confucian order the Manchus adopted.

## Common mistakes

- **"Divine right was universal."** It is the European doctrine; the Chinese Mandate of Heaven could be lost, and Islamic rulers claimed to serve God's law, not to embody God's choice.
- **"The samurai were abolished."** They were salaried and demilitarised; abolition came in the 1870s (Unit 5).
- **"Tax farming was efficient."** It raised cash quickly and ruined provinces slowly.
- **"Versailles was just a palace."** It was an instrument for controlling the nobility.
