## What you need to be able to do

- **2.3** Explain the causes and effects of growth in Indian Ocean exchange: the technologies (lateen sail, dhow, junk, compass, astrolabe, knowledge of the monsoon), the commercial practices and the spread of Islam that connected East Africa, Arabia, India, Southeast Asia and China; the rise of port cities (Kilwa, Calicut, Malacca, Hormuz); the diasporic merchant communities (Arab, Persian, Chinese, Gujarati, Jewish) and their cultural effects; and the Ming voyages of Zheng He.
- **2.4** Explain the causes and effects of growth in trans-Saharan exchange: the camel saddle and caravans, the oasis and salt-mine way-stations, the gold-salt exchange, and the empires (Mali) and cities (Timbuktu) built on it; and the movement of Islam, scholars and enslaved people across the desert.

In plain language: for each network, know the technology that made it work, the goods and people that moved, the cities that grew, and the states that taxed it.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Monsoon | Seasonal winds of the Indian Ocean: from the southwest in summer, the northeast in winter; ships sailed out on one and home on the other. |
| Dhow | Arab and Indian sailing ship with lateen (triangular) sails, able to sail closer to the wind. |
| Junk | Large Chinese ship with watertight compartments, multiple masts and a stern rudder; the biggest vessels of the age. |
| Astrolabe | Instrument for finding latitude from the sun or stars, refined in the Islamic world. |
| Diasporic community | Merchants of one origin settled permanently in a foreign port (Arabs in Calicut, Chinese in Malacca, Gujaratis in Kilwa). |
| Calicut | Port of southwestern India where pepper was sold and Arab, Chinese and later Portuguese ships met. |
| Hormuz | Port at the mouth of the Persian Gulf, gateway to Persia and the overland routes. |
| Malacca; Kilwa | Week 2 and week 3 port states, now seen as nodes of the ocean network. |
| Zheng He | Ming admiral who led seven voyages (1405–1433) with huge fleets to Southeast Asia, India, Arabia and East Africa. |
| Camel saddle | Saddles developed in Arabia and North Africa that let camels carry heavy loads and riders, making trans-Saharan caravans practical. |
| Taghaza | Salt mine in the Sahara whose slabs of salt were cut and carried south to the Sahel. |
| Trans-Saharan gold | Gold from West African forest regions carried north to Mediterranean mints; much of Europe's gold coinage came from it. |
| Timbuktu | Niger-bend city of trade and scholarship under Mali and Songhai. |

## The Indian Ocean network

**Why it worked.** The Indian Ocean is the world's oldest long-distance maritime trading system, and its engine is the **monsoon**. Ships left East Africa and Arabia on the summer southwest wind, reached India, waited, and came home on the winter northeast wind; from India the same rhythm carried ships to Southeast Asia and China. Knowledge of these winds, passed between generations of sailors, was the basic technology. To it the period added better ships and instruments: the **dhow** with its lateen sail; the Chinese **junk**, with watertight compartments and several masts, which Ibn Battuta saw at Calicut and described as carrying hundreds of sailors and soldiers with cabins for merchants; the **magnetic compass** from China and the **astrolabe** from the Islamic world. Islamic commercial law and credit, and the shared faith of merchants from Mogadishu to Malacca, lowered the cost of trust.

**What moved.** Unlike the Silk Roads, the ocean could carry bulk: timber, grain, dates and horses as well as pepper from Malabar, cloves and nutmeg from the Moluccas, cotton cloth from Gujarat and Bengal, porcelain and silk from China, gold and ivory from Africa, enslaved people from East Africa and elsewhere. Textiles were the staple: Indian cottons clothed much of the ocean's shores.

**Who moved.** Merchants settled where they traded, forming **diasporic communities**: Arab and Persian Muslims in the Swahili towns and Calicut, Gujarati Hindus and Muslims in East Africa and Malacca, Chinese in Southeast Asian ports, Jewish traders in Cairo and India. They married locally, carried languages, religions and architecture, and were the main channel by which Islam reached Southeast Asia (week 2) and the Swahili coast (week 3). Cities grew as **entrepôts**: **Kilwa**, **Mombasa**, **Aden**, **Hormuz**, **Calicut**, **Malacca**, **Quanzhou**. Their rulers taxed trade, protected merchants and competed for them; Malacca's conversion to Islam around 1400 is the model case.

**China's fleets.** Between 1405 and 1433 the Ming emperor Yongle and his successor sent the admiral **Zheng He**, a Muslim eunuch, on seven voyages with fleets of dozens of large junks and tens of thousands of men to Southeast Asia, Ceylon, Calicut, Hormuz, Aden and the Swahili coast. The purpose was prestige and tribute, not conquest or colonies: foreign rulers sent envoys and exotic goods (a giraffe from East Africa among them) to the Ming court. After 1433 the voyages stopped, as Confucian officials judged them costly and the northern frontier more urgent; China did not withdraw from trade, but the state withdrew from the ocean. Within seventy years the Portuguese arrived (Unit 4).

## The trans-Saharan network

**Why it worked.** The Sahara is a sea of sand and rock; the camel is its ship. The **camel saddle**, developed in Arabia and North Africa in the centuries before 1200, let camels carry heavy loads and riders; camels can go days without water and cross terrain horses cannot. Caravans of hundreds or thousands of animals moved between **oases** and wells under guides who knew the route, and caravanserai and the Berber peoples of the desert provided water, pasture and protection for a price.

**What moved.** The exchange was built on two necessities found on opposite sides of the desert: **salt**, cut in slabs at mines such as **Taghaza** and carried south to a Sahel that had none, and **gold**, panned in the forest zone of West Africa and carried north to the mints of Morocco, Egypt and Europe. Ibn Battuta crossed the desert in 1352 and described a town where houses and the mosque were built of salt blocks and where no one lived but the enslaved miners. Alongside gold and salt moved copper, cloth, horses, books, ivory and **enslaved people**, taken from the Sahel to North Africa and the Islamic world in a trade that long predated the Atlantic one.

**Who moved.** Muslim Berber and Arab merchants dominated the northern ends; in the Sahel, West African merchants (Wangara, Dyula) carried gold to the market towns. Islam travelled with them (week 3); so did scholars, books and architects, and **Timbuktu** and **Djenné** became cities of mosques and manuscripts. Mali's rulers taxed the trade and policed the routes; **Mansa Musa's** pilgrimage (1324) is the trans-Saharan network's most famous journey. By the 1400s the **Songhai** Empire was replacing Mali as the power on the Niger bend, and by the end of the period Portuguese ships on the West African coast had begun to divert gold toward the Atlantic.

## Primary sources, described

Ibn Battuta's account of the Indian Ocean survives only in modern translations, so we describe rather than quote it. At Kilwa in 1331 he found a well-built town of wood and coral whose Muslim sultan was known for generosity to the poor and for raids on the non-Muslim interior for plunder and captives. At Calicut he saw Chinese junks, the largest with four decks, private cabins with latrines, and crews of up to a thousand; he reports that Chinese ships alone sailed the China route, and that the Malabar coast was governed by Hindu rulers who protected Muslim merchants because the trade depended on them. In 1352 he crossed the Sahara with a caravan, describing a salt-mining town built of salt, a ten-day waterless stage, and the custom of sending a scout ahead to arrange water from the next oasis. Each of these details is evidence: of port rulers who tolerated foreign faiths for trade's sake, of Chinese shipbuilding, and of the organisation a desert crossing required.

## Common mistakes

- **"The Indian Ocean trade was created by Europeans."** It was ancient; the Portuguese joined and tried to tax it after 1498.
- **"Zheng He founded colonies."** The voyages sought tribute and prestige and ended in 1433; China built no overseas empire.
- **"Trans-Saharan trade was only gold."** Salt was as important, and the trade in enslaved people was large and old.
- **"Merchants stayed foreign."** Diasporic communities married locally and produced blended cultures such as Swahili.
