# Week 22 · Migration in an Interconnected World and Causation in the Imperial Age

**Topics:** 6.6 Causes of Migration in an Interconnected World · 6.7 Effects of Migration · 6.8 Causation in the Imperial Age
**Themes:** SIO (Social Interactions and Organization), ECN (Economic Systems), ENV (Humans and the Environment), CDI (Cultural Developments and Interactions), GOV (Governance)
**Reasoning processes:** Causation (why people moved, and the unit's capstone: the causes and effects of imperialism as a whole); comparison (free, indentured and coerced migration)
**Spiral:** The export economies of week 21 created the demand for plantation and railway labour; the industrial transport of unit 5 (5.5) carried the migrants; the Atlantic slave trade of unit 4 (4.4, 4.7) is the coerced migration this period's indenture both replaced and resembled. Topic 6.8 asks you to pull weeks 19–22 together.

## What you need to be able to do

- **6.6** Explain how and why new patterns of migration developed from 1750 to 1900: population growth, famine and poverty, the demand for labour in export economies, and steamships and railways; and distinguish free, semi-coerced (indentured, convict) and coerced (enslaved) movement.
- **6.7** Explain how and why new migration patterns affected societies: male-dominated migration and changed gender roles at home, ethnic enclaves abroad, cultural mixing, and receiving societies' restrictions such as the Chinese Exclusion Act and the White Australia policy.
- **6.8** Explain the relative significance of the effects of imperialism on the world between 1750 and 1900: the causation capstone for the whole unit.

In plain language: know who moved where and why (at least six named flows), what happened to the places they left and arrived in, and be able to write a causation argument about imperialism that weighs several causes and several effects.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Push and pull factors | Conditions that drive people out (famine, poverty, persecution) and draw them in (jobs, land, wages). |
| Indentured labour | Workers bound by contract for a term of years (usually five) in exchange for passage and wages; replaced slavery on many plantations after abolition. |
| Coolie trade | The nineteenth-century term for the shipping of Chinese and Indian contract labourers, often under coercion. |
| Convict transportation | Britain's shipment of about 160,000 convicts to Australia, 1788–1868. |
| Chain migration | Migrants following relatives and neighbours to the same destination. |
| Ethnic enclave | A neighbourhood or district where migrants from one place cluster (Chinatowns, Little Italies). |
| Diaspora | A people dispersed from its homeland while keeping ties and identity (Indian, Chinese, Lebanese, Irish). |
| Nativism | Hostility to immigrants among a receiving population. |
| Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) | U.S. law suspending the immigration of Chinese labourers; the first U.S. restriction by nationality. |
| White Australia policy (1901) | Australia's Immigration Restriction Act, using a dictation test to exclude non-Europeans. |
| Irish Potato Famine (1845–52) | Blight-caused famine that killed about a million and drove over a million to emigrate. |
| Remittances | Money sent home by migrants; a major source of income for sending regions. |

## Why people moved: causes of migration (6.6)

**Demographic pressure.** World population roughly doubled between 1750 and 1900. Better food (potatoes and maize from the Americas), fewer epidemics and, in Europe, early industrial prosperity meant more people than land in rural Ireland, southern Italy, Scandinavia, Guangdong and Fujian, and the Punjab.

**Push: famine, poverty, persecution.** The **Irish Potato Famine** killed about a million people and sent more than a million abroad in a decade; Italian peasants fled land hunger and cholera; Jews fled pogroms in the Russian Empire after 1881; Chinese peasants fled the Taiping war's devastation and overpopulation; Indian labourers left districts hit by the famines of the 1870s and 1890s.

**Pull: the export economies' hunger for labour.** After Britain abolished slavery in its colonies (1833–38) planters in the Caribbean, Mauritius, Natal and Fiji needed workers; Peru's guano islands, Cuba's sugar fields, Malaya's tin mines, Ceylon's tea estates and the railways of East Africa, the United States and Canada all wanted hands. Argentina and Brazil wanted farmers and coffee pickers; the United States wanted everyone.

**Means: industrial transport.** Steamships cut the Atlantic crossing from weeks to about ten days by the 1870s and made the fare affordable; railways carried migrants to ports and then across continents; the Suez Canal shortened the route from India to East Africa and Europe; the telegraph and cheap post let migrants send money and news home. Migration on this scale was itself a product of the industrial revolution.

## Who moved where: the major flows

| Flow | Approximate scale | Type | Period |
|---|---|---|---|
| Europeans to the Americas (United States, Argentina, Brazil, Canada) | 50–55 million left Europe, 1846–1940 | free, often chain migration | peak 1880–1914 |
| Irish to the United States and Britain | over 2 million by 1870 | free, famine-driven | 1845–1870s |
| Italians to Argentina, Brazil and the United States | about 14 million left Italy, 1876–1914 | free; many returned ("birds of passage") | 1880–1914 |
| Indians to Caribbean, Mauritius, Natal, Fiji, Malaya, Ceylon, East Africa | over 1.3 million indentured, 1834–1917 | indentured | 1834–1917 |
| Chinese to Southeast Asia, Cuba, Peru, California, Australia | several million | free and indentured ("coolie trade") | 1840s–1900s |
| Japanese to Hawaii, the U.S. West Coast, Brazil and Peru | hundreds of thousands | contract and free | 1885–1920s |
| British convicts to Australia | about 160,000 | coerced | 1788–1868 |
| Lebanese and Syrians to the Americas and West Africa | hundreds of thousands | free, commercial | 1880–1914 |
| Rural to urban within countries (Manchester, Osaka, Buenos Aires) | tens of millions | internal | throughout |

**Indenture** deserves care. Contracts promised passage, wages and return; in practice recruiters deceived, ships were crowded (mortality on the Cuba and Peru routes was comparable to the slave trade), and planters controlled workers through fines, jail and debt. Many Indians stayed after their terms, which is why Mauritius, Guyana, Trinidad and Fiji have large Indian-descended populations today. Britain abolished Indian indenture in 1917 after Indian nationalist protest, in which Gandhi, who had organised Indians in Natal, played a part.

## What migration did: effects (6.7)

**On the places people left.** Migration was mostly male, so women took over farms, businesses and households in Guangdong, the Punjab, southern Italy and Ireland; remittances fed families and built houses and temples; some regions depended on money from abroad. Population pressure eased, and the returning "birds of passage" brought new ideas and savings.

**On the places people went to.** Migrants built **ethnic enclaves**: Chinatowns in San Francisco, Singapore and Lima; Little Italies in New York and Buenos Aires; Indian merchant communities in Durban, Nairobi and Zanzibar; Irish parishes in Boston and Liverpool. Enclaves kept languages, religions and foods alive and produced mixed cultures: Indo-Caribbean music and cuisine, Chinese-Peruvian *chifa* cooking, Italian-Argentine Spanish. Migrant labour built the infrastructure of the age: Chinese workers on the Central Pacific Railroad (1860s), Indian workers on the Uganda Railway (1896–1901), Italians in the ports and farms of the Río de la Plata.

**Receiving societies pushed back.** Nativist movements grew wherever migrants competed for jobs or looked different. The United States passed the **Chinese Exclusion Act** in 1882, suspending the entry of Chinese labourers, and renewed it until 1943; Australia's first federal parliament passed the **Immigration Restriction Act** of 1901 (the White Australia policy), using a dictation test in any European language to keep out Asians and Pacific Islanders; Canada imposed a head tax on Chinese migrants (1885); anti-Asian riots occurred in Vancouver (1907) and anti-Italian violence in New Orleans (1891). Compare Emma Lazarus's sonnet "The New Colossus" (1883), which welcomed "your huddled masses yearning to breathe free", with the law passed a year earlier.

## Causation in the imperial age: the unit's capstone (6.8)

Topic 6.8 asks you to judge the *relative significance* of imperialism's effects. Organise your thinking as a causation web rather than a list.

**Causes (weeks 19 and 21):** industrial demand for raw materials and markets; surplus capital seeking investment; nationalist rivalry after 1871; Social Darwinism and the civilizing mission; strategic needs (Suez, coaling stations); enabling technologies (steam, quinine, machine guns, telegraph); the weakness of the Mughal, Qing and Ottoman states.

**Effects, by theme:**

- **ECN:** a world economy divided into industrial exporters and raw-material producers; monoculture; debt and informal empire; the drain of wealth from colonies.
- **ENV:** forests cleared for plantations, soils exhausted, guano and rubber trees depleted, crops transplanted across continents.
- **GOV:** the partition of Africa; the Raj; China's loss of sovereignty without annexation; the destruction of states (Zulu, Sokoto, Mahdist) and the survival of a few (Ethiopia, Siam, Japan, which became an empire itself).
- **SIO:** new racial hierarchies in colonies; mass migration and diaspora communities; male migration reshaping gender roles; nativist restriction.
- **CDI:** missionary Christianity; Western-educated colonial elites who later led nationalist movements; religious revitalization movements; cultural mixing in enclaves.
- **TEC:** railways, ports, telegraphs built for export, which later served nationalists and independent states.

**Weighing significance.** A strong 6.8 argument makes a choice and defends it. For example: the most significant effect was economic, because the export economy outlived the empires and still shapes the former colonies; or political, because the borders drawn in 1884–1914 produced the states and conflicts of the twentieth century; or demographic, because migration created the multi-ethnic societies of the Americas, the Caribbean, Southeast Asia and East Africa. Whichever you choose, acknowledge the others and explain why yours outweighs them.

## Common mistakes

- **Calling indenture slavery, or calling it free.** It was semi-coerced: a contract signed, often under deception, and enforced by law and debt. Say so precisely.
- **Forgetting Asian migration.** European migration to the Americas was the largest flow, but Indian and Chinese migration reshaped the Caribbean, East Africa, Southeast Asia and the Pacific.
- **Treating effects on the home society as nothing.** Gender roles, remittances and return migration are CED points.
- **Writing a list for 6.8.** The capstone wants a judgment about relative significance, with reasons.
