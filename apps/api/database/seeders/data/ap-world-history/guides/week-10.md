## Europe divides: the Protestant and Catholic Reformations

In 1517 an Augustinian friar and professor at Wittenberg, **Martin Luther**, posted propositions for debate against the sale of indulgences. His first thesis held that Christ "willed that the whole life of believers should be repentance"; his twenty-seventh mocked the preachers who said "that so soon as the penny jingles into the money-box, the soul flies out [of purgatory]"; his eighty-sixth asked why the pope, "whose wealth is to-day greater than the riches of the richest", did not build St Peter's "with his own money, rather than with the money of poor believers" (Holman translation, 1915). Printing carried the theses across Germany in weeks; the **printing press**, a Unit 2 transfer, is why this protest succeeded where earlier ones had failed. Luther moved from indulgences to the authority of the pope and then to the core: salvation by **faith alone**, Scripture as the only authority, the priesthood of all believers, the Bible in German. Excommunicated in 1521 and protected by the Elector of Saxony, he survived; German princes who adopted Lutheranism gained church lands and independence from the emperor.

The movement split and spread. **Zwingli** in Zürich and **Calvin** in Geneva built stricter reformed churches; Calvin's doctrine of **predestination** and his disciplined congregations made Calvinism the international, militant form of Protestantism (Huguenots in France, Dutch rebels, Scottish Presbyterians, English Puritans). **Henry VIII** broke with Rome for dynastic reasons in 1534 and took the monasteries' land; under Elizabeth the Church of England settled between Rome and Geneva. The **Catholic Reformation** answered: the **Council of Trent** (1545–1563) reaffirmed every doctrine Luther had attacked, reformed clerical abuses and standardised training; the **Jesuits**, founded by Ignatius Loyola (1540), became the Church's teachers and missionaries, reaching Akbar's court, Ming China and Japan; the Roman **Inquisition** and the Index of banned books policed belief. The result was a century of **religious war**: the German wars ended by the **Peace of Augsburg** (1555, *cuius regio, eius religio*: the ruler chooses); the French Wars of Religion (1562–1598); the Dutch revolt against Spain; and the **Thirty Years' War** (1618–1648), which killed perhaps a third of Germany's population and ended at **Westphalia** with the principle that religion was each state's own business. Continuity: Christianity remained the frame of European life. Change: one Church became many, states gained control of churches, and the printed vernacular Bible put Scripture into lay hands.

## Islam: the Sunni–Shia frontier

Islam had been divided between Sunni and Shia since the seventh century, but in this period the division became a frontier between states. Shah Ismail's imposition of **Twelver Shia Islam** on Persia (1501) created a Shia state facing a Sunni Ottoman Empire whose sultan, after 1517, held the holy cities and the title of caliph. Each side persecuted the other's adherents within its borders, and the wars from Chaldiran (1514) to 1639 fixed a border that is still roughly the Turkey–Iraq–Iran line. Within the Ottoman Empire Sunni orthodoxy was managed by the state-appointed ulama under the Sheikh ul-Islam, while **Sufi** orders, especially the Bektashi attached to the janissaries, kept popular religion flexible. In the Safavid state Shia scholars were imported from Lebanon and Iraq to build a clerical establishment that would outlast the dynasty. Continuity: the two branches of Islam. Change: their fusion with rival empires.

## South Asia: synthesis and its limits

Northern India was the meeting place of **Hindu Bhakti** devotion, **Sufi** Islam and the rule of Muslim emperors over a Hindu majority. **Guru Nanak** (1469–1539), a Punjabi who had absorbed both Bhakti and Sufi teaching, taught that there was one God beyond Hindu and Muslim names, that caste and ritual were meaningless before Him, and that honest work and service were worship. His followers, the **Sikhs**, formed a community under successive gurus; after Mughal persecution (the execution of Guru Arjan in 1606 and Guru Tegh Bahadur in 1675) the tenth guru, Gobind Singh, founded the **Khalsa** (1699), a disciplined military brotherhood, and Sikhism became a martial faith resisting Aurangzeb.

**Akbar** (1556–1605) ruled by inclusion. He married Rajput princesses, abolished the **jizya** tax on non-Muslims (1564), appointed Hindus to high office, held debates at Fatehpur Sikri between Muslim, Hindu, Jain, Parsi and Jesuit scholars, and in 1582 proclaimed the **Din-i Ilahi**, a court cult centred on himself that borrowed from all of them; it attracted a few courtiers and vanished at his death, but his policy of tolerance (*sulh-i kul*, "peace with all") held through Jahangir and Shah Jahan. **Aurangzeb** reversed it: restored the jizya (1679), demolished some temples, enforced Islamic law and spent his reign at war with Marathas, Rajputs and Sikhs. Continuity: Hindu majority under Muslim rule, devotional religion among the people. Change: a new religion, a short-lived imperial synthesis, and a turn to orthodoxy that cost the empire its allies.

## Primary source to read closely

**Luther, *Disputation on the Power and Efficacy of Indulgences* (1517), Holman translation (1915).** "Out of love for the truth and the desire to bring it to light, the following propositions will be discussed at Wittenberg, under the presidency of the Reverend Father Martin Luther". Thesis 1: "Our Lord and Master Jesus Christ, when He said Poenitentiam agite, willed that the whole life of believers should be repentance." Thesis 27: "They preach man who say that so soon as the penny jingles into the money-box, the soul flies out [of purgatory]." Thesis 36: "Every truly repentant Christian has a right to full remission of penalty and guilt, even without letters of pardon." Thesis 62: "The true treasure of the Church is the Most Holy Gospel of the glory and the grace of God." Thesis 86: "Why does not the pope, whose wealth is to-day greater than the riches of the richest, build just this one church of St. Peter with his own money, rather than with the money of poor believers?"

*Sourcing:* an academic disputation by a professor, in Latin, addressed to theologians; its form is respectful, its content explosive. *Use:* evidence for what started the Reformation (the sale of pardons, papal wealth) and for its early, limited aims; the break with Rome came later.

## Common mistakes

- **"Luther wanted to found a new church."** He wanted to reform the one Church; the split followed.
- **"The Counter-Reformation only persecuted."** Trent reformed clerical training and discipline, and the Jesuits built the best schools in Europe.
- **"Sikhism is a mix of Hinduism and Islam."** Nanak drew on both and rejected both; the CED calls it a syncretic new religion, which is the testable phrasing.
- **"Akbar converted to Hinduism."** He stayed Muslim and invented a court cult; his policy was tolerance, not conversion.
- **"The Sunni–Shia split began with the Safavids."** It began in the seventh century; the Safavids made it a frontier between states.
