# Week 14 · Changing Social Hierarchies, and the Unit 4 Synthesis

**Topics:** 4.7 Changing Social Hierarchies from 1450 to 1750 · 4.8 Continuity and Change from 1450 to 1750
**Themes:** SIO, CDI, GOV, ECN (Social Interactions and Organization; Cultural Developments and Interactions; Governance; Economic Systems)
**Reasoning process:** Continuity and change (the Unit 4 capstone), with comparison across empires
**Built from:** the casta paintings and the colonial legal codes of Spanish America, the Qing regulations on Manchu and Han, the expulsion of the Jews from Spain and the Ottoman millet system, summarised in our own words; the week 12 and week 13 guides
**Spiral:** Unit 1 described the social orders of 1200 (Confucian scholar-gentry, caste, feudal estates, Aztec nobles and commoners); Unit 3 showed states creating new elites (devshirme, mansabdars, service nobility); weeks 12 and 13 showed conquest and coerced labour making new populations. This week asks how societies ranked the people the period had thrown together, and then what, across 1450–1750, changed and what did not.

## What you need to be able to do

- **4.7** Explain how social categories, roles and practices were maintained or changed: the rise of new elites (**merchants**, **creoles**) and the decline of old ones (the **Manchu** displacement of Han officials; the weakening of European nobility against monarchs); the creation of new ethnic and racial hierarchies in the Americas (the **casta** system, **mestizos**, **mulattoes**, **creoles** versus **peninsulares**); the treatment of **minorities**: the **expulsion of Jews** from Spain (1492) and Portugal and their reception in the Ottoman Empire and the Netherlands, the Ottoman and Mughal management of religious minorities, the restrictions on **Christians in Japan**; **gender** roles in colonial societies; and the role of **Indigenous elites** who kept local power under Spanish rule.
- **4.8** Explain the continuities and changes in economic, social and political patterns across the period: the **networks** that persisted (Indian Ocean, Silk Roads, trans-Saharan) and the new ones (Atlantic, Pacific); the continued dominance of **Asian economies** and the new role of **Europe** as the connector; the persistence of peasant agriculture beneath the new trades; and the beginnings of a single global economy.

In plain language: who was on top, who was new, who was excluded, and how; then step back and say what 1750 had that 1450 lacked.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Peninsulares | Spaniards born in Spain, holding the highest colonial offices. |
| Creoles (criollos) | People of Spanish descent born in the Americas; wealthy landowners and merchants excluded from the top offices, whose resentment fed Unit 5's revolutions. |
| Mestizos; mulattoes | People of mixed Indigenous–European and African–European descent. |
| Casta system | The Spanish colonial ranking of people by ancestry, with dozens of named categories, depicted in eighteenth-century casta paintings. |
| Limpieza de sangre | "Purity of blood": the Spanish requirement, applied first to converted Jews and Muslims, then to the colonies. |
| Banner system | The hereditary Manchu (and allied Mongol and Han) military-social organisation of the Qing, privileged above the Han population. |
| Queue | The Manchu hairstyle Han men were forced to wear as a sign of submission (1645). |
| Sephardim | Jews of Spain and Portugal expelled in 1492 and 1497, settling in the Ottoman Empire, North Africa, Italy and later Amsterdam. |
| Millet | Ottoman self-governing religious community (Orthodox, Armenian, Jewish) under its own leaders. |
| Kakure Kirishitan | "Hidden Christians" of Japan who kept the faith in secret after the ban. |
| Indigenous nobility (caciques, kurakas) | Native elites whom Spain used as intermediaries to collect tribute and labour, keeping rank and some land. |
| Gentry; bourgeoisie | The landed elite of England and the commercial middle class of Europe, both rising in this period. |

## New elites and old (4.7)

**Merchants rose.** The joint-stock company, the plantation and the silver trade created fortunes outside the old orders. Dutch regents, English merchants and gentry, Genoese bankers and the shareholders of Amsterdam and London bought land, titles and influence; in the Ottoman and Mughal empires, Armenian, Greek, Jewish and Gujarati merchant communities financed the state. In Tokugawa Japan merchants ranked lowest in Confucian theory and became the richest class in practice, lending to samurai whose rice stipends never changed.

**Creoles emerged.** In Spanish America a new elite of American-born Spaniards owned the haciendas, mines and trade of the colonies, yet the crown reserved viceroyalties, audiencias and bishoprics for **peninsulares** sent from Spain. The creoles' sense that they were the natural rulers of their lands, denied by Madrid, is the grievance that produced Bolívar (Unit 5).

**Old elites were displaced or tamed.** In China the **Manchu** conquest put a hereditary banner caste above the Han scholar-gentry, imposed the **queue** as a mark of submission and reserved half the top posts for Manchus; the examination elite survived but under new masters. In Europe, Unit 3's monarchs reduced the nobility from independent lords to courtiers and officers (Versailles; the Fronde's defeat), while the nobility kept its tax exemptions and social precedence. In Mughal India, Rajput and Persian nobles shared power under the mansab system until Aurangzeb's wars and the Marathas undermined both. In Japan, the samurai became stipended administrators.

## Racial hierarchies in the Americas (4.7)

Conquest, the slave trade and intermarriage produced populations the Spanish and Portuguese ranked by ancestry. At the top stood peninsulares and creoles (Spaniards); below them **mestizos**, then **mulattoes**, then Indigenous people and enslaved and free Africans, with dozens of intermediate categories (castizo, morisco, zambo) depicted in the **casta paintings** of eighteenth-century Mexico, each showing a couple of different ancestry and their child with a label. Law tied rank to rights: tribute and labour drafts fell on Indigenous people, slavery on Africans, offices on Spaniards; but wealth and marriage moved families across lines, and the categories were fluid in practice. The Spanish doctrine of **limpieza de sangre**, invented against converted Jews and Muslims at home, crossed the Atlantic as a doctrine of blood. In the English colonies a starker line was drawn: by the late 1600s Virginia and the Caribbean had codified slavery as hereditary through the mother and racial, with free people of colour a small, restricted group. In Brazil, Portuguese men, Indigenous women and enslaved Africans produced the most mixed society of all, under a slave regime as harsh as any.

**Indigenous elites** survived as intermediaries. Spain confirmed **caciques** in Mexico and **kurakas** in the Andes in their rank, land and exemption from tribute in return for delivering their communities' labour and taxes; some sent sons to Spanish schools and wrote in Spanish and Nahuatl. Their position was ambiguous: protectors and exploiters of their people, and the last Indigenous authority the colonial state recognised.

**Gender.** Spanish law and the Church prescribed patriarchal households, but demography made its own rules: a shortage of Spanish women meant unions with Indigenous and African women, and the casta order descended from them; Indigenous women in markets and crafts, African women on plantations and in towns, and the convents that gave some creole women education and property. In Africa the slave trade's export of men left women to farm and raised polygyny; in Southeast Asia, Chinese and European merchants married into local trading families whose women held property.

## Minorities (4.7)

**Jews.** Spain expelled its Jews in **1492** and Portugal in 1497 (after forced conversion); the **Sephardim** went to Ottoman cities (Salonika, Istanbul, where Sultan Bayezid II is said to have welcomed them as an asset), to Italy and North Africa, and after 1600 to **Amsterdam**, where they helped build Dutch commerce. The Ottomans ran religious minorities through **millets**, self-governing under rabbis and bishops and taxed with the jizya: tolerated, subordinate, useful. The Mughals under Akbar removed the jizya and under Aurangzeb restored it (Unit 3). **Christians in Japan** were banned after 1614, executed or forced to recant, and survived as hidden communities for two centuries. **Muslims and Jews in Spain** itself faced the Inquisition and, for the Moriscos, expulsion in 1609. The pattern: states of the period used religion to define belonging, and minorities were tolerated where useful and expelled where ideology or fear prevailed.

## Continuity and change, 1450–1750 (4.8)

| Pattern | Continuity | Change |
|---|---|---|
| Trade networks | Indian Ocean, Silk Roads, trans-Saharan routes continued; Asian merchants kept most of their trade | The Atlantic and Pacific became trade routes for the first time; Europeans connected every ocean |
| Economic centre | Asia (China, India) remained the largest producer and the magnet for silver | Europe became the intermediary and gained new wealth; a global silver economy linked Potosí to Beijing |
| Labour | Peasant agriculture, serfdom, mit'a, African and Islamic slavery persisted | Plantation chattel slavery, indenture and the Atlantic slave trade were new in scale and kind |
| States | Land empires (Unit 3) dominated Eurasia; Ming, Mughal, Ottoman unmoved by Europeans | Maritime empires and chartered companies ruled oceans and the Americas; Indigenous states of the Americas destroyed |
| Societies | Confucian, caste, feudal and Islamic hierarchies continued | New racial hierarchies in the Americas; creole and merchant elites; Manchu rule over Han |
| Environment | Old World farming spread | The Columbian Exchange reshaped diets, animals, disease and population on every continent |
| Religion | Islam, Buddhism, Hinduism, Confucianism in place | Christianity spread to the Americas, the Philippines and parts of Africa and Asia; Protestant–Catholic division (Unit 3) |

The CED's conclusion for 4.8: the period did not make Europe dominant (that is Unit 6), but it created the connections, the Atlantic system, the global silver trade and the racial and labour hierarchies on which later dominance was built, while most of the world's people lived under the same states, on the same land, growing the same crops with a few new ones.

## Primary sources, described

**Casta paintings.** Sets of sixteen panels, each showing a father, mother and child labelled by category ("Spaniard and Indian produce Mestizo"), painted in eighteenth-century Mexico for export to Spain. They are evidence of how the elite conceived the social order, of dress, occupation and domestic life by rank, and of the anxiety to classify a population that would not stay classified.

**The Qing queue edict (1645).** The regent Dorgon's order that all Han men shave the front of the head and braid the rest in the Manchu style within ten days, on pain of death: "keep your hair and lose your head, or keep your head and lose your hair", as the saying went. Resistance in the lower Yangzi cities was crushed with massacres; the queue remained the mark of Qing subjecthood until 1911.

**Bayezid II and the Sephardim.** Ottoman chroniclers record the sultan's welcome of the expelled Jews of Spain and his reported remark on the folly of a king who impoverished his own realm to enrich another's. Whatever its exact wording, the Sephardic communities of Salonika and Istanbul became the empire's physicians, printers and merchants.

## Common mistakes

- **"Creoles were mixed-race."** In Spanish America creoles were of Spanish descent born in the Americas; mestizos were mixed.
- **"The casta system was rigid."** Law was rigid; practice was fluid, which is why the paintings exist.
- **"Europe dominated the world by 1750."** Asia still produced most of the world's goods and absorbed most of its silver.
- **"Minorities were everywhere persecuted."** The Ottomans and Dutch profited from the Jews Spain expelled.
