# Week 27 · Setting the Stage, the Cold War, and Its Effects

**Topics:** 8.1 Setting the Stage for the Cold War and Decolonization · 8.2 The Cold War · 8.3 Effects of the Cold War
**Themes:** GOV (Governance), TEC (Technology and Innovation), ECN (Economic Systems), SIO (Social Interactions and Organization), CDI (Cultural Developments and Interactions)
**Reasoning processes:** Causation (why the wartime alliance became a forty-year rivalry); comparison (the two blocs' economies, alliances and methods)
**Spiral:** The Bolshevik state of 7.1 is one of the two superpowers; the Second World War's outcome (7.7) left the United States and the Soviet Union dominant and Europe ruined; the ideologies of 7.6 (liberal capitalism against a one-party planned economy) now divide the world; the Depression's lesson (7.4) shaped Bretton Woods; the empires of unit 6 (6.2) are about to dissolve in the same years.

## What you need to be able to do

- **8.1** Explain the historical context of the Cold War and decolonization after 1945: the collapse of European power, the rise of the United States and the Soviet Union, the United Nations, the Bretton Woods economic order, and the colonial nationalism the war had strengthened.
- **8.2** Explain the causes and effects of the ideological struggle: capitalism and democracy against communism; the Truman Doctrine, the Marshall Plan, the Berlin blockade, NATO and the Warsaw Pact; the arms race; proxy wars in Korea, Vietnam, Afghanistan and elsewhere; the Non-Aligned Movement as a third position.
- **8.3** Explain the effects of the Cold War: military alliances, nuclear deterrence, the space race, the division of Germany and Korea, proxy conflicts in Latin America, Africa and Asia, and the domestic effects on both blocs.

In plain language: be able to say why the Cold War began (three causes, with dates), name its institutions on each side, trace its main crises in order, and explain how it reached a village in Angola or a laboratory in California.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Superpower | A state with global military and economic reach; after 1945 only the United States and the Soviet Union. |
| United Nations (1945) | The successor to the League, with a Security Council of five permanent veto-holding members. |
| Bretton Woods (1944) | The postwar economic order: the IMF, the World Bank, currencies fixed to the dollar, the dollar to gold. |
| Iron Curtain | Churchill's phrase (1946) for the line dividing Soviet-dominated eastern Europe from the West. |
| Containment | The American strategy of preventing the spread of communism, set out by George Kennan in 1946–47. |
| Truman Doctrine (1947) | U.S. aid to Greece and Turkey and the pledge to support free peoples resisting subjugation. |
| Marshall Plan (1948–52) | About $13 billion in American aid to rebuild western Europe. |
| NATO (1949) / Warsaw Pact (1955) | The two military alliances. |
| Proxy war | A conflict in which the superpowers backed opposing sides without fighting each other directly. |
| Deterrence / MAD | The doctrine that nuclear arsenals prevented war because any attack meant mutual destruction. |
| Non-Aligned Movement | States, led by India, Egypt, Yugoslavia and Indonesia, that refused to join either bloc (Bandung 1955, Belgrade 1961). |
| Détente | The easing of tensions in the 1970s (SALT, Helsinki, Nixon in China). |

## The world of 1945 (8.1)

Europe had destroyed itself. Germany was occupied and divided into four zones; Britain was bankrupt and dependent on American loans; France had been occupied; the colonial empires had been beaten in Asia by Japan and had promised their subjects a new deal in the Atlantic Charter. Two states stood apart: the United States, with half the world's industrial output, the atomic bomb and an intact homeland, and the Soviet Union, with 27 million dead but the largest army in the world standing in the middle of Europe. They had been allies of necessity against Hitler; they disagreed about everything else, above all about Poland and eastern Europe, which Stalin intended to keep as a security zone and the West had promised free elections at Yalta (February 1945).

New institutions were built on the lessons of 1919 and 1929. The **United Nations** (San Francisco, 1945) gave the great powers a veto so that it would not go the way of the League. The **Bretton Woods** conference (1944) created the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank and fixed currencies to the dollar to prevent the competitive devaluations of the 1930s; the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (1947) began cutting tariffs. The Soviet Union stayed out of all three. Colonial nationalism had been strengthened by the war: India's independence was conceded in principle, Ho Chi Minh declared Vietnam independent in September 1945, Indonesia the month before, and African soldiers came home.

## The Cold War (8.2)

**Why it began.** Three causes work together. Ideology: a capitalist democracy and a one-party planned economy each believed history was on its side. Security: Stalin wanted a buffer in eastern Europe and installed communist governments in Poland, Romania, Bulgaria, Hungary and, in 1948, Czechoslovakia; the West saw expansion. Power: with Germany and Japan gone, only two states remained and each feared the other filling the vacuum. Churchill named the division at Fulton, Missouri, in March 1946 (the "iron curtain" speech, described here rather than quoted); Kennan's Long Telegram argued the Soviets could only be contained.

**The American response.** When Britain could no longer support Greece against communist insurgents, Truman asked Congress for aid in March 1947 and set out the doctrine: "I believe that it must be the policy of the United States to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures." He contrasted two ways of life, one "distinguished by free institutions, representative government, free elections", the other resting "upon the will of a minority forcibly imposed upon the majority" through "terror and oppression, a controlled press and radio; fixed elections". The **Marshall Plan** (announced June 1947) rebuilt western Europe and tied it to the United States; Stalin forbade his satellites to take part. When the Western powers moved to create a West German state, Stalin blockaded Berlin (June 1948 – May 1949); the Allies flew in supplies for eleven months. **NATO** (April 1949) committed the United States to Europe's defence; two German states were founded in 1949; the Soviet bomb (August 1949) and the Chinese communist victory (October 1949) ended the American monopoly and doubled the communist world. NSC-68 (1950), a secret planning paper, called for a massive American military buildup; the Korean War paid for it.

**Crises in order.**

| Year | Crisis | What it showed |
|---|---|---|
| 1948–49 | Berlin blockade and airlift | the West would hold Berlin without shooting |
| 1950–53 | Korean War | containment by proxy; China's entry; stalemate at the 38th parallel |
| 1956 | Hungary; Suez | the USSR would crush reform; Britain and France were no longer great powers |
| 1961 | Berlin Wall | the division of Europe made permanent |
| 1962 | Cuban missile crisis | the closest approach to nuclear war; followed by the hotline and the Test Ban Treaty |
| 1965–73 | American war in Vietnam | the limits of American power |
| 1979 | Soviet invasion of Afghanistan | the end of détente; the Soviet Vietnam |

**The arms race and deterrence.** Soviet bomb 1949, hydrogen bombs 1952–53, Sputnik 1957, ICBMs, submarine missiles; by the 1980s about 60,000 warheads. Deterrence rested on the certainty of retaliation. Eisenhower, who presided over the buildup, counted its cost in 1953: "Every gun that is made, every warship launched, every rocket fired signifies, in the final sense, a theft from those who hunger and are not fed, those who are cold and are not clothed." In his farewell address (1961) he warned against "the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex". Kennedy's inaugural (1961) promised to "pay any price, bear any burden, meet any hardship, support any friend, oppose any foe to assure the survival and the success of liberty"; eighteen months later he faced Khrushchev over missiles in Cuba.

**The third way.** Nehru's India, Nasser's Egypt, Tito's Yugoslavia, Sukarno's Indonesia and Nkrumah's Ghana refused both blocs. The Bandung conference (1955) of twenty-nine Asian and African states and the Belgrade conference (1961) founded the **Non-Aligned Movement**, which used superpower rivalry to extract aid from both sides.

## Effects (8.3)

**Military.** Alliances ringed the world: NATO, SEATO, CENTO, the Rio Pact, the Warsaw Pact. Germany and Korea were divided; Vietnam was divided and then reunited by war. Proxy wars killed millions: Korea (about three million), Vietnam (about two to three million), Angola, Mozambique, Ethiopia, Nicaragua, Afghanistan (about a million). Both superpowers overthrew governments: the CIA in Iran (1953), Guatemala (1954) and Chile (1973); the Red Army in Hungary (1956) and Czechoslovakia (1968).

**Economic and technological.** The space race (Sputnik 1957, Gagarin 1961, the Moon 1969) and computing, jet aircraft and the internet grew from military spending; the Soviet economy spent perhaps a quarter of its output on arms and starved consumers. The Marshall Plan and Japanese reconstruction produced the Western boom; Comecon bound eastern Europe to the Soviet economy.

**Domestic.** McCarthyism and loyalty oaths in the United States; purges, censorship and the Gulag in the Soviet bloc; the civil rights movement framed as a Cold War necessity; conscription and protest in the 1960s (week 29). Culture became a weapon: Radio Free Europe, Soviet sport, jazz tours, the Olympics.

## Common mistakes

- **Dating the Cold War from 1917 or 1950.** The rivalry was ideological from 1917, but the Cold War as a structure of alliances dates from 1947–49.
- **Treating the Non-Aligned Movement as neutral in all things.** Its members took aid from both sides and fought their own wars.
- **Forgetting that the superpowers never fought each other directly.** Every war was a proxy war, which is why the arms race and the crises matter so much.
- **Ignoring the UN and Bretton Woods.** 8.1 is about institutions as well as power.
