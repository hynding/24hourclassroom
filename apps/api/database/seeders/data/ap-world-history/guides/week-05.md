## What you need to be able to do

- **2.1** Explain the causes and effects of growth in Silk Road exchange from c. 1200 to c. 1450: improved commercial practices (caravanserai, credit, paper money, bills of exchange), new technologies (saddles, stirrups, the compass reaching the west), the rise of trading cities (Kashgar, Samarkand), and the increased demand for luxury goods that changed production in China, Persia and India.
- **2.2** Explain the process of the Mongol conquests, how the empire was organised and divided into khanates, and the effects of Mongol rule: increased trade and communication (the yam postal system), transfers of technology and knowledge, movement of peoples, and the Pax Mongolica, together with its destructive side.

In plain language: know why goods and ideas moved more between 1200 and 1450 than before, and what the Mongols had to do with it.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Silk Roads | The network of overland routes across Central Asia linking China, India, Persia and the Mediterranean; luxuries moved, not bulk goods. |
| Caravanserai | Inns a day's journey apart on trade routes, offering lodging, water and security to merchants and animals. |
| Bill of exchange | A written order to pay, letting merchants settle debts across distances without carrying coin. |
| Flying cash | Chinese credit notes (Tang) that became Song paper money. |
| Kashgar; Samarkand | Oasis cities of Central Asia that grew rich as markets and way-stations. |
| Chinggis (Genghis) Khan | Temüjin, who united the Mongol tribes in 1206 and began the conquests. |
| Khanate | One of the four successor states: the Yuan (China), the Ilkhanate (Persia), the Chagatai (Central Asia), the Golden Horde (Russia). |
| Yam | The Mongol relay-post system of stations and horses that carried messengers and officials across the empire. |
| Pax Mongolica | The century of relative security under Mongol rule (c. 1250–1350) in which travel and trade across Eurasia flourished. |
| Kublai Khan | Chinggis's grandson; founded the Yuan dynasty (1271) and completed the conquest of the Song (1279). |
| Ilkhanate | Mongol state in Persia founded by Hulagu after the sack of Baghdad (1258); converted to Islam by 1295. |
| Golden Horde | Mongol state over Russia and the steppe; extracted tribute from Russian princes; converted to Islam. |
| Tumen | A Mongol army unit of 10,000, organised by tens, hundreds and thousands. |
| Pax and plague | The same routes that carried silk and envoys carried the Black Death westward in the 1340s. |

## The Silk Roads revived

The Silk Roads were ancient, but between 1200 and 1450 traffic on them grew. The CED wants three causes and three effects.

**Causes.** First, **commercial practices improved**. Caravanserais, often built by rulers or pious foundations, made the desert and mountain stages survivable; Chinese paper money and Islamic credit instruments (bills of exchange, partnerships, deposit banking by the "money-changers" of Baghdad and Cairo) let merchants trade without hauling silver; guilds and merchant communities (Sogdian earlier, then Persian, Armenian, Jewish and Muslim networks) provided trust across distances. Second, **technology**. Better saddles and stirrups improved both cavalry and pack animals; the magnetic compass and better maps reached the Islamic world and Europe from China. Third, **security**. After 1250 the Mongols held most of the route, suppressed banditry and taxed trade rather than looting it.

**Effects.** Demand for luxury goods rose, and producers responded: Chinese workshops turned out porcelain and silk in export styles; Persian and Indian weavers made textiles for distant buyers; Song and Yuan China expanded iron and steel. Trading cities grew: **Kashgar**, where routes around the Taklamakan met, and **Samarkand**, which Timur would rebuild into a capital in the late 1300s, with markets, mosques and madrasas paid for by trade. And the roads carried people and ideas as well as goods: Buddhism had travelled them to China centuries before; now Islam moved east along them into Central Asia and western China, and Nestorian Christians served Mongol courts.

What moved? Silk, porcelain, tea, paper, gunpowder and printing eastward to westward; horses, glass, silver, woollens, furs and slaves the other way; cotton textiles, spices and precious stones from India and Persia in both directions. Volume was small by sea-trade standards, but the goods were high in value and the ideas high in consequence.

## The Mongol Empire

**Conquest.** In 1206 Temüjin took the title **Chinggis Khan** after uniting the Mongol tribes. The *Secret History of the Mongols*, a thirteenth-century Mongol chronicle, tells of his hard youth and his reorganisation of the tribes into units that cut across clan loyalty. His army was organised in tens, hundreds, thousands and **tumens** of ten thousand; Marco Polo later explained that the Khan "appoints an officer to every ten men, one to every hundred, one to every thousand, and one to every ten thousand, so that his own orders have to be given to ten persons only" (*The Travels*, Book I, ch. 54, Yule–Cordier edition, 1903). Mounted archers who could "ride ten days on end without lighting a fire or taking a meal", in Polo's words, living on mares' milk and dried curd, moved faster than any army of the age. Cities that submitted were spared and taxed; cities that resisted were destroyed as a warning. Between 1206 and 1279 the Mongols took northern China (the Jin), Central Asia (Khwarazm, 1219–1221), Persia and Baghdad (1258), Russia (1237–1240), and finally Song China (1279). They were stopped only at the edges: by the Mamluks at Ain Jalut (1260), by the sea at Japan (1274, 1281) and by the jungles of Vietnam and Java.

**Organisation.** After Chinggis died in 1227 his descendants ruled jointly and then separately. The Franciscan John of Plano Carpini, sent by the pope in 1245, witnessed the enthronement of Güyük: the chiefs "placed Cuyuc on the imperial seat, and the chiefs knelt before him" and "the whole people did likewise, except ourselves who were not his subjects" (Rockhill's translation, 1900). By the 1260s the empire had divided into four **khanates**: the **Yuan** in China under Kublai, the **Ilkhanate** in Persia, the **Chagatai** khanate in Central Asia and the **Golden Horde** over Russia. Each adapted to its subjects: Kublai ruled as a Chinese emperor (though he placed Mongols and foreigners such as Polo above Chinese officials and curtailed the examinations); the Ilkhans and the Golden Horde converted to Islam; the Chagatai stayed closest to nomadic ways. Russia's princes paid tribute and collected taxes for the Horde, and Moscow's rise began as a Mongol tax-collector.

**Effects.** The CED stresses the Mongols as a cause of connection. The **yam** post system, with stations every 25–30 miles stocked with horses, carried messengers and merchants with official passes across the continent. Under the **Pax Mongolica** merchants, missionaries and envoys travelled from Europe to China (Polo, Carpini, William of Rubruck) and from the Islamic world to China (Ibn Battuta). Technology and knowledge moved: gunpowder, printing and the compass westward; Persian astronomers to China and Chinese astronomy to Maragha; medical texts; crops. Artisans were deported wholesale from conquered cities to serve the khans. Chinese paper money reached Persia (where it failed) and Chinese painting styles reached Persian manuscripts. The cost was enormous: cities such as Nishapur, Merv and Baghdad were depopulated, irrigation in Persia and Iraq collapsed, and the population of northern China fell sharply. And the same roads that carried silk carried the plague: the Black Death moved west along Mongol routes in the 1340s and helped end the Pax.

## Causation chains to practise

- **Cause → effect:** Mongol unification of the steppe → security on the Silk Roads → more merchants and envoys → transfer of gunpowder, printing and the compass to the west → later European exploration (Unit 4).
- **Cause → effect:** Mongol destruction of Baghdad (1258) → end of the Abbasid Caliphate → Cairo replaces Baghdad as the centre of Arabic learning (Unit 1).
- **Cause → effect:** Mongol routes → movement of merchants, rats and fleas → Black Death (week 7).

## Primary source to read closely

**Marco Polo on the Mongol army (Yule–Cordier edition, 1903).** "Well, he appoints an officer to every ten men, one to every hundred, one to every thousand, and one to every ten thousand, so that his own orders have to be given to ten persons only, and each of these ten persons has to pass the orders only to other ten, and so on; no one having to give orders to more than ten. ... And in case of great urgency they will ride ten days on end without lighting a fire or taking a meal."

*Sourcing:* Polo served Kublai and admired Mongol efficiency; he explains the decimal system as a marvel to European readers whose armies were feudal levies. *Use:* strong evidence for organisation and mobility; weaker for numbers, which he inflates.

## Common mistakes

- **"The Mongols destroyed everything."** They destroyed cities that resisted and then built the most connected Eurasia yet seen; both are true.
- **"The Silk Road was one road."** It was a web of routes, and most goods changed hands many times.
- **"Mongols ruled all of Asia the same way."** Each khanate adapted to its subjects: Chinese emperor, Muslim sultan, steppe khan.
- **"The Mongols invented paper money and the compass."** Both were Chinese; the Mongols spread them.
