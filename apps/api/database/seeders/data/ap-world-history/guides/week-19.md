# Week 19 · Rationales for Imperialism and State Expansion

**Topics:** 6.1 Rationales for Imperialism from 1750 to 1900 · 6.2 State Expansion from 1750 to 1900
**Themes:** GOV (Governance), CDI (Cultural Developments and Interactions), ECN (Economic Systems), TEC (Technology and Innovation)
**Reasoning processes:** Causation (why industrial states expanded, and what ideas they used to justify it); comparison (settler colonies, direct rule, and expansion by non-European states)
**Spiral:** Industrialization (5.3–5.5) supplied the steamships, rifles, quinine and telegraphs that made expansion cheap; the Enlightenment and nationalism (5.1–5.2) supplied the language of "civilization" and national greatness; the land-based empires of unit 3 (Ottoman, Qing, Mughal) are the states that imperialism now pressed on.

## What you need to be able to do

- **6.1** Explain how ideologies contributed to the development of imperialism from 1750 to 1900: nationalism, Social Darwinism, the "civilizing mission", religious motives and the economic logic of industrial capitalism.
- **6.2** Compare the processes by which states expanded: European states taking control of territory where private companies had traded (India, the Dutch East Indies, the Congo); settler colonies (Australia, New Zealand, Algeria, the United States); the partition of Africa after the Berlin Conference; and expansion by the United States, Russia and Japan.

In plain language: know *why* industrial states wanted colonies (and what they said in public about it), know *how* they got them (company to crown, settler conquest, diplomatic partition, outright war), and be ready with one named example in Africa, in Asia, in the Pacific and in the Americas.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Imperialism | A state extending its power over other peoples and territories, by conquest, settlement, or economic and political control. |
| New imperialism | The burst of formal empire-building from about 1870 to 1914, above all the partition of Africa and Southeast Asia. |
| Social Darwinism | The misuse of Darwin's biology to rank human societies and claim that "fitter" nations were entitled to rule or replace others. |
| Civilizing mission | The claim that colonial rule brought Christianity, law, schools and railways to peoples who could not provide them; in French, *mission civilisatrice*. |
| Chartered company | A private company (the English and Dutch East India Companies, the Royal Niger Company) licensed by a state to trade and govern. |
| Direct rule | Colonial officials from the metropole govern at every level (French West Africa). |
| Indirect rule | Local rulers kept in office under a colonial resident (British Nigeria, the princely states of India). |
| Settler colony | A colony where large numbers of migrants from the metropole took land to farm and stay (Australia, New Zealand, Algeria, Kenya's highlands). |
| Berlin Conference (1884–85) | European powers set rules for claiming African territory ("effective occupation") and recognised Leopold II's Congo Free State. |
| Scramble for Africa | The partition of almost the whole continent among seven European states between about 1880 and 1900. |
| Sepoy | An Indian soldier in the East India Company's army; the 1857 rebellion began among sepoys. |
| Raj | British Crown rule over India, 1858–1947, after the Company was abolished. |
| Meiji expansion | Japan's acquisition of the Ryukyus, Hokkaido, Taiwan (1895) and Korea (1910) as a modern imperial state. |

## Why industrial states expanded: five rationales that worked together

Historians argue about which cause mattered most; the CED asks you to explain how several *ideologies* contributed. Keep five in mind and notice that they reinforced one another.

**1. Economic logic.** Factories needed raw materials that temperate Europe could not grow: cotton, rubber, palm oil, tin, copper, jute. They also needed markets, and bankers needed somewhere to invest surplus capital at higher returns than at home. The British economist J. A. Hobson, a critic of empire, put the economic argument sharply in *Imperialism: A Study* (1902): "It is this economic condition of affairs that forms the taproot of Imperialism." He concluded that "Imperialism is the endeavour of the great controllers of industry to broaden the channel for the flow of their surplus wealth by seeking foreign markets and foreign investments." Lenin's 1917 pamphlet on imperialism pushed the argument further, calling it the "highest stage" of capitalism; describe his argument in your own words rather than quoting a modern translation.

**2. Nationalism and great-power rivalry.** After German and Italian unification (1871), a colony became a mark of national standing. France sought colonies partly to recover prestige after losing Alsace-Lorraine; Germany's Bismarck, who cared little for colonies, still hosted the Berlin Conference and claimed Togo, Cameroon, Namibia and Tanganyika in 1884–85; Italy took Eritrea and Somaliland and tried, and failed, to take Ethiopia (1896).

**3. Social Darwinism and racial ideology.** Writers such as Herbert Spencer applied "survival of the fittest" to peoples and nations, and pseudo-scientific racism ranked humanity into supposedly fixed types. These ideas explained conquest as natural and even kind. Rudyard Kipling's poem "The White Man's Burden" (1899), written to urge the United States to keep the Philippines, is the classic statement of the duty side of the idea: "Take up the White Man's burden— / Send forth the best ye breed— / Go bind your sons to exile / To serve your captives' need". Its description of colonised peoples as "Your new-caught, sullen peoples, / Half-devil and half-child" shows the racial hierarchy underneath.

**4. The civilizing mission and religion.** Missionary societies, Protestant and Catholic, expanded enormously after 1800; David Livingstone's travels in Africa (1840s–1873) linked missions, anti-slavery and exploration in the British imagination. French politicians spoke of a *mission civilisatrice* to spread French language, law and science. Anti-slavery was a real motive and a convenient one: the Berlin Act of 1885 committed the powers to suppress the slave trade in the Congo basin while handing the basin to Leopold II, whose rubber regime became one of the worst atrocities of the age (week 20).

**5. Strategy and technology.** Coaling stations, cable landings and canals (Suez, 1869) had to be defended, which meant occupying the land around them: Britain took Egypt in 1882 largely to secure the route to India. Technology made all of this cheap: quinine against malaria, steam gunboats up the Niger and Yangzi, breech-loading rifles and the Maxim gun (1884), and telegraph cables that let London govern Calcutta in hours.

Critics existed from the start. Mark Twain, writing against the American war in the Philippines in "To the Person Sitting in Darkness" (1901), mocked the whole business as a game: "There is more money in it, more territory, more sovereignty and other kinds of emolument, than there is in any other game that is played."

## How states expanded: four patterns

**From company to crown.** In South Asia the English East India Company had grown from a trading body into a territorial ruler after Plassey (1757), governing Bengal and then most of the subcontinent through its own army of sepoys. The rebellion of 1857–58 convinced Parliament that a company could not be trusted with an empire; in 1858 the Crown took direct control (the Raj), and in 1876 Victoria was proclaimed Empress of India. The Dutch followed the same path earlier: the VOC went bankrupt in 1799 and the Netherlands took over Java, later imposing the "cultivation system" of forced export crops (1830). In West Africa the Royal Niger Company's charter was revoked in 1900 and Nigeria became a protectorate.

**Settler colonies.** Britain sent convicts to New South Wales from 1788 and free settlers to Australia and New Zealand through the 1800s; the Treaty of Waitangi (1840) with Maori chiefs was followed by land wars (1845–1872). France conquered Algeria from 1830 and made it legally part of France, settling a million Europeans there by 1900. The United States' westward expansion, Argentina's "Conquest of the Desert" (1870s–80s) and Russia's settlement of Siberia and the steppe belong in the same category: expansion over land against indigenous peoples, by states that saw themselves as filling an empty space.

**Partition by diplomacy and conquest.** In 1870 Europeans controlled about a tenth of Africa, mostly coastal; by 1914 only Ethiopia and Liberia were independent. The Berlin Conference did not draw the map, but it set the rule that a claim required "effective occupation" and notification to the other powers, which started a race to sign treaties with African rulers and station troops. Britain's holdings ran from Egypt (1882) and the Sudan (1898) to the Cape; France took most of the west and north; Belgium's king got the Congo; Portugal kept Angola and Mozambique; Germany and Italy took what remained. In Southeast Asia, France built Indochina (1862–1893), Britain took Burma (1886) and the Malay states, and Siam (Thailand) alone kept its independence as a buffer.

**Non-European imperial states.** Expansion was not only European. The United States bought Alaska (1867), annexed Hawaii (1898) and, after the war with Spain in 1898, took Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines, where it fought a long war against Filipino independence forces (1899–1902). Russia pushed across Central Asia (Tashkent 1865, Samarkand 1868) and into the Caucasus and the Amur basin. Japan, having transformed itself after the Meiji Restoration of 1868 (week 16), took the Ryukyu Islands (1879), defeated China and took Taiwan (1895), defeated Russia (1905) and annexed Korea (1910). The Meiji Constitution of 1889 (translated into English by Ito Miyoji the same year) made the emperor "the head of the Empire, combining in Himself the rights of sovereignty", the centralised state that organised this expansion.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating "new imperialism" as the first imperialism.** Europeans had held colonies since the 1500s (units 4 and 5). What was new after 1870 was the speed, the scale and the fact that industrial technology made conquest of the African and Asian interiors possible.
- **Using one cause.** An essay that says "imperialism was caused by greed" will not earn the complexity point. Show how economic, nationalist, racial, religious and strategic motives worked together, and that critics such as Hobson and Twain existed at the time.
- **Forgetting the non-European empires.** The United States, Russia and Japan were imperial states too; the CED names them in 6.2.
- **Confusing settler colonies with colonies of rule.** Algeria and Australia were settled by migrants who took the land; Nigeria and India were ruled by a thin layer of officials over large indigenous populations. The two produced very different conflicts.
