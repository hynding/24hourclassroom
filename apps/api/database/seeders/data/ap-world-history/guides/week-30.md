# Week 30 · Technology, Disease, and the Environment in the Global Age

**Topics:** 9.1 Advances in Technology and Exchange · 9.2 Technological Advances and Limitations: Disease · 9.3 Technological Advances: Debates About the Environment
**Themes:** TEC (Technology and Innovation), ENV (Humans and the Environment), SIO (Social Interactions and Organization), ECN (Economic Systems)
**Reasoning processes:** Causation (how new technologies shrank distance and what they caused); continuity and change (disease and environment before and after 1900)
**Spiral:** The industrial revolutions of unit 5 (5.3, 5.5) began the burning of fossil fuels and the transport revolution that this week's technologies extend; the Cold War's military research (8.3) produced the computer, the satellite and the internet; the Green Revolution (8.6) is the agricultural side of the same story; the influenza pandemic of 1918 (7.3) is the comparison for HIV and COVID-19; the migration of 6.6 returns at a global scale.

## What you need to be able to do

- **9.1** Explain how technology shaped economic development and exchange after 1900: the internet and computing, mobile telephony, communication satellites, jet aircraft and container shipping, and their effects on the speed and scale of exchange; energy technologies (oil, nuclear, renewables); the Green Revolution and genetically modified crops.
- **9.2** Explain the causes and effects of disease since 1900: diseases of poverty (malaria, tuberculosis, cholera) and their persistence; new epidemics (the 1918 influenza, HIV/AIDS, Ebola, COVID-19); medical advances (antibiotics, vaccines, the eradication of smallpox, polio nearly so); diseases of longer life and affluence (heart disease, diabetes, Alzheimer's); the uneven global distribution of health.
- **9.3** Explain the causes and effects of environmental change since 1900 and the debates about it: population growth from under 2 billion to 8 billion, deforestation, desertification, air and water pollution, the depletion of fisheries, and climate change from greenhouse gases; the responses (the Montreal Protocol, Kyoto, Paris, the Green Belt Movement, Greenpeace) and the arguments between development and conservation.

In plain language: be able to name five technologies that made the world smaller and say what each changed; know which diseases were beaten, which persist and which are new, and why health is unequal; and be able to explain climate change's causes and the main debates about responding to it.

## A smaller world (9.1)

**Communication.** The transistor (1947), the integrated circuit (1958) and the microprocessor (1971) made computing cheap; personal computers spread in the 1980s. The internet began as ARPANET, a U.S. Defense Department research network (1969), was opened to commerce in the 1990s and reached about 5 billion people by the 2020s through the World Wide Web (1991), search, social media and the smartphone (2007). Mobile telephony leapt over the fixed-line networks poor countries never built: sub-Saharan Africa went from almost no telephones to hundreds of millions of mobile subscriptions in twenty years, with mobile money (M-Pesa, 2007) following. Communication satellites (from 1962) carried television and telephone across oceans. Information that had taken weeks in 1850 and seconds by telegraph in 1900 was now free, global and in every pocket.

**Transport.** Jet airliners (the Boeing 707, 1958; the 747, 1970) made intercontinental travel ordinary: about 4.5 billion passenger journeys a year by 2019. The shipping container (Malcom McLean, 1956) and the ships, cranes and ports built around it cut the cost of moving goods to almost nothing and made global supply chains possible: a shirt designed in one country, woven in a second, sewn in a third and sold in a fourth. The space race gave the world weather, navigation (GPS) and communication satellites.

**Energy and food.** Oil replaced coal as the leading fuel; OPEC's embargo of 1973 showed its political weight. Nuclear power (from 1954) promised abundance and produced Chernobyl (1986) and Fukushima (2011); wind and solar fell in cost after 2010. The Green Revolution doubled grain output in Asia and Latin America; genetically modified crops (from 1996) raised yields and provoked resistance in Europe. President Kennedy's Rice University speech of 1962 captured the age's faith in technology: "We choose to go to the Moon in this decade and do the other things, not because they are easy, but because they are hard".

|---|---|---|

## Disease (9.2)

**Victories.** Life expectancy worldwide rose from about 32 years in 1900 to over 70 by 2020. Vaccines (diphtheria, polio from 1955, measles) and antibiotics, clean water and sanitation, and the WHO (1948) did most of it. Smallpox, which had killed about 300 million people in the twentieth century, was eradicated by a global vaccination campaign (last case 1977, declared 1980), the only human disease so far ended; polio was reduced from hundreds of thousands of cases a year to a few dozen.

**Persistence.** Diseases of poverty persist where water, housing and health systems are poor: malaria still kills about 600,000 a year, mostly African children; tuberculosis over a million; cholera returned to Haiti (2010) and Yemen. The 1918 influenza pandemic killed about 50 million, more than the war it followed, spread by troopships and railways. **HIV/AIDS**, identified in 1981, spread through sexual contact, blood and needles, killed about 40 million, and fell hardest on southern Africa, where life expectancy dropped by a decade; antiretroviral drugs (1996) turned it into a manageable condition where they could be afforded. Ebola (West Africa, 2014–16) and **COVID-19** (2020–23, millions dead) showed that jet travel spreads disease as fast as goods, and that vaccines could be developed in a year and distributed unequally.

**New burdens.** As people lived longer and ate more, heart disease, strokes, diabetes, cancers and dementia became the leading causes of death, first in rich countries and then everywhere; obesity rose as tobacco use fell in the West and grew in the developing world. Health became unequal in a new way: the poor die of infections, the rich of chronic disease, and the gap in life expectancy between the richest and poorest countries remains about thirty years.

## Environment (9.3)

**Causes.** World population grew from 1.6 billion in 1900 to 8 billion in 2022, and consumption per person grew faster. Fossil fuels powered everything: carbon dioxide rose from about 280 parts per million before the industrial revolution to over 420; the planet warmed by about 1.2 °C by the 2020s. Forests fell to farms, ranches and loggers (the Amazon, Indonesia, the Congo basin); deserts spread (the Sahel); rivers and air were poisoned (the Cuyahoga fire, 1969; Delhi and Beijing smog); fisheries collapsed (Newfoundland cod, 1992); species vanished. Chernobyl (1986) and the Bhopal gas disaster (1984, India, thousands dead) showed industrial technology's risks fell hardest on the poor.

**Responses.** The environmental movement (week 29) produced national laws and international agreements. The **Montreal Protocol** (1987) phased out chlorofluorocarbons and let the ozone layer recover: the model of success, because substitutes existed and the producers were few. Climate proved harder: the **Kyoto Protocol** (1997) bound rich countries to cuts the United States never ratified; the **Paris Agreement** (2015) relied on national pledges and a target of well below 2 °C. The debates: whether developing countries should limit the emissions the rich countries had used to industrialise; whether growth and conservation can be reconciled (sustainable development, the Brundtland report of 1987); how to pay for adaptation in the countries that emitted least and suffer most (Bangladesh, the Pacific islands, the Sahel). Grassroots movements did what treaties could not: Wangari Maathai's **Green Belt Movement** in Kenya (1977) planted over 50 million trees through women's groups and won her the Nobel Peace Prize (2004); Greenpeace (1971) fought whaling and nuclear testing; Chico Mendes organised Amazon rubber tappers until his murder in 1988.

| Problem | Cause | Response | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ozone depletion | CFCs | Montreal Protocol (1987) | recovering |
| Climate change | fossil fuels, deforestation | Kyoto (1997), Paris (2015) | emissions still rising; renewables growing |
| Deforestation | agriculture, logging | Green Belt, protected areas, certification | continuing in the tropics |
| Air and water pollution | industry, cars, cities | national laws (U.S. 1970–72), WHO standards | improved in rich countries, severe in poor |
| Overfishing | industrial fleets | quotas, marine reserves | mixed |

## Common mistakes

- **Treating technology as self-generating.** The computer, the internet, the jet and GPS came from state military spending; the exam wants the Cold War connection.
- **Forgetting the diseases that persist.** Malaria and tuberculosis kill more than the new epidemics in most years.
- **Writing about climate without numbers.** 280 to 420 ppm, 1.6 to 8 billion people, 1.2 °C: use them.
- **Treating the environmental debate as settled.** The exam asks for the arguments on each side (development versus conservation, rich versus poor countries).
