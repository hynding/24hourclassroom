## What you need to be able to do

- **5.3** Explain how environmental factors contributed to industrialisation: Britain's **coal** and iron deposits, **rivers** and canals, proximity of resources to each other and to ports; and the other factors: **agricultural improvements** (enclosure, crop rotation, the seed drill) that fed a growing population and freed labour; **urbanisation**; **capital** accumulated from trade and colonies; a **legal** and political culture that protected property; and access to **colonial** raw materials and markets.
- **5.4** Explain how different modes and locations of production changed over time: the shift from the **cottage industry** (putting-out system) to the **factory**; the spread of industrialisation to **France, Germany, Belgium, the United States, Russia and Japan**; and the decline of manufacturing in **India, Egypt and the Middle East** (deindustrialisation) as their textiles lost to British machine-made cloth.
- **5.5** Explain how technology shaped economic production: the **steam engine** (Watt), **coal**, the **railway**, the **steamship** and **telegraph**; the **second industrial revolution** of **steel** (Bessemer), **chemicals**, **electricity** and **oil**; and their effect on the **interior** of continents, which became reachable and exploitable.
- **5.6** Explain the causes and effects of economic strategies of states: the **Ottoman** Tanzimat and the defensive industrialisation of **Muhammad Ali** in Egypt; **Meiji Japan**'s state-led industrialisation (and the zaibatsu); **Russia**'s state-sponsored Trans-Siberian Railway and Witte's industrialisation; and the limits of such reforms.

In plain language: why machines and factories appeared in Britain around 1780, how they spread or failed to spread, what the new technologies made possible, and what governments did when they saw that industrial power was now military power.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Industrial Revolution | The shift, beginning in Britain c. 1760–1830, from hand production in homes and workshops to machine production in factories powered by water and then coal. |
| Enclosure | The fencing of common land into private farms, raising output and pushing rural labourers toward wage work and towns. |
| Agricultural revolution | Crop rotation (turnips, clover), selective breeding, the seed drill and enclosure, which fed Britain's growing population with fewer farmers. |
| Cottage industry (putting-out) | Merchants supplying raw wool or cotton to rural households that spun and wove at home; the system factories replaced. |
| Factory | A building housing machines and the workers who tend them, with set hours and discipline; Arkwright's Cromford mill (1771) is the model. |
| Spinning jenny, water frame, mule | Hargreaves (1764), Arkwright (1769) and Crompton (1779): the machines that multiplied cotton spinning. |
| Steam engine | Newcomen's pump (1712) improved by James Watt (1769, separate condenser; rotary motion 1781), which freed factories from rivers. |
| Coal | The fossil fuel that powered steam engines and smelted iron (coke); Britain's deposits lay near iron, water and ports. |
| Railway | The Stockton and Darlington (1825) and Liverpool and Manchester (1830) lines began a network that cut transport costs and created demand for iron and coal. |
| Second industrial revolution | c. 1870–1914: steel (Bessemer, 1856), chemicals (dyes, fertilisers), electricity (generators, lighting, motors) and oil (internal combustion), led by Germany and the United States. |
| Deindustrialisation | The collapse of handicraft manufacturing in India, Egypt and the Ottoman lands as machine-made British cloth undersold local weavers. |
| Tanzimat (1839–1876) | Ottoman reorganisation: legal equality of subjects, new schools, conscription, codified law and factories, under pressure from European power. |
| Muhammad Ali | Governor of Egypt (1805–1848) who built state cotton plantations, textile mills, arsenals and a conscript army; his industries were undercut by the Treaty of Balta Liman (1838). |
| Meiji Restoration (1868) | The overthrow of the Tokugawa shogunate and the rule of a reforming oligarchy in the emperor's name, which abolished the samurai order, built railways, mills and a modern army and navy. |
| Zaibatsu | Japanese family conglomerates (Mitsui, Mitsubishi) that bought state-built factories and dominated the economy. |
| Trans-Siberian Railway | Built 1891–1916 under Sergei Witte's state-led programme of railways, tariffs and foreign loans to industrialise Russia. |

## Why Britain, and why around 1780 (5.3)

No single cause will do; the exam wants several working together.

**Environment.** Britain's **coal** lay in large, shallow seams in the Midlands, the North and South Wales, close to **iron** ore and to navigable **rivers** and a coastline no part of the island is far from. Canals (from the 1760s) linked the fields. When wood ran short for charcoal, coke-smelted iron (Darby, 1709) and coal-fired steam were already at hand. An island with mild weather, fast rivers for water wheels and no land frontier to defend had cheap energy, cheap transport and cheap security.

**Agriculture.** The eighteenth century's **enclosures**, **crop rotation** with turnips and clover (no fallow year), selective breeding and the seed drill raised yields, so that a shrinking share of the population fed a growing one. Population rose from about 6 million (1750) to 9 million (1800) to 21 million (1851). Displaced labourers went to the towns and the mills; the farms that remained bought iron tools and sold grain to feed them.

**Capital and trade.** Profits from the Atlantic economy of Unit 4, from sugar, slaving, shipping and the East India trade, were available for investment; a banking system, a national debt and a stock exchange had grown since the 1690s; colonies supplied raw **cotton** (first from the Caribbean and India, after 1800 mainly from the slave plantations of the American South) and bought finished cloth. Mercantilism had built the markets that laissez-faire later opened.

**Institutions and ideas.** Parliament protected property and patents; the Royal Society and provincial societies spread useful knowledge; Dissenters barred from universities went into trade and engineering; Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (1776) argued that "the greatest improvements in the productive powers of labour ... seem to have been the effects of the division of labour," and the pin factory in which ten workers "could make among them upwards of forty-eight thousand pins in a day" described what factories would do with machines. Britain also had a **wage** problem that made machines pay: labour was dear by European standards and coal cheap, so replacing hands with engines made sense in Lancashire before it did in Bengal.

**The first industry: cotton.** Indian calicoes had been imported through the East India Company since the seventeenth century and banned (1721) to protect wool; British cotton spinners could not keep up with weavers until the **spinning jenny** (1764), the water-powered **water frame** (1769) and the **mule** (1779) multiplied thread output. Arkwright's mills at Cromford gathered machines and workers under one roof with a bell and a clock: the **factory**. Cotton output rose more than a hundredfold between 1770 and 1850, and by then Britain exported machine-made cloth to the India from which it had imported the hand-made kind.

## Steam, iron and the new geography of production (5.5)

**Watt's engine.** Newcomen's atmospheric engine (1712) had pumped water from mines; **James Watt**'s separate condenser (patented 1769) cut its coal use by three quarters, and his rotary engine (1781) turned wheels. Steam freed the factory from the river bank and put it in the coalfield town: Manchester, Leeds, Birmingham, Glasgow. Coal consumption rose with every engine, and the smoke of the industrial city was its signature.

**Iron and railways.** Coke smelting, puddling (Cort, 1784) and rolling mills made cheap wrought iron; steam engines on rails (Trevithick 1804; Stephenson's Rocket 1829) made the **railway**. The Liverpool and Manchester line (1830) carried cotton and passengers; by 1850 Britain had 6,000 miles of track, and railways then demanded iron, coal and engineering on a scale that drove the whole economy. Railways cut the cost of moving goods overland by more than half and created a national market, then continental and colonial ones.

**Steamships and the telegraph.** Steam at sea (Atlantic crossings from 1838; the Suez Canal 1869) and the **telegraph** (1837; the Atlantic cable 1866) joined the world's markets in time as well as space: a merchant in Liverpool knew the price of cotton in New Orleans within the day. Interiors that rivers had not reached, the North American prairie, the Russian steppe, the African highlands, became reachable by rail and knowable by wire, which is why the **second industrial revolution** and the new imperialism of Unit 6 arrive together.

**The second industrial revolution (c. 1870–1914).** **Steel** (Bessemer converter 1856; open-hearth), stronger and cheaper than iron, built rails, ships, bridges and skyscrapers; **chemicals** gave synthetic dyes (Perkin 1856), fertilisers and explosives; **electricity** (Siemens's dynamo 1866; Edison's lamp 1879; electric motors and trams) lit cities and powered factories without a boiler; **oil** (Drake's well, Pennsylvania 1859) fed lamps, then the internal combustion engine (Benz 1885). Germany and the United States, with research universities and large firms, led this phase, and Britain fell behind in the industries it had not invented.

## Spread and failure to spread (5.4)

**Europe and the United States.** **Belgium**, with coal, iron and proximity to Britain, industrialised first on the continent (1820s); **France** more slowly, with a larger peasantry and less coal; the **German** states after the Zollverein customs union (1834) and above all after unification (1871), when Ruhr coal, state railways and chemical and electrical firms made Germany Europe's industrial leader by 1900; the **United States** from New England's water-powered mills (Lowell, 1820s) to railways, steel (Carnegie) and oil (Rockefeller), surpassing Britain in output by the 1890s. Each borrowed British machines and mechanics, often smuggled against British export bans, and then innovated.

**Deindustrialisation.** The cloth that Britain sold abroad was the cloth others had made by hand. **India**'s weavers, the world's largest textile exporters in 1750, lost first their export markets and then, after tariffs and railways opened the interior, their home market; Bengal's spinners were the earliest casualties. **Egypt** and the **Ottoman** lands lost their textile and metal crafts to imports once the Balta Liman treaty (1838) fixed low tariffs. These regions did not stand still; they were reorganised as suppliers of raw cotton, wheat and opium to the industrial economies (Unit 6, 6.4).

## The state steps in (5.6)

Industrial power was military power, as the Opium War (1839–1842) and the Crimean War (1853–1856) showed states that lacked it. Three responses:

**Muhammad Ali's Egypt (1805–1848).** The Albanian-born governor seized the land, made long-staple cotton a state crop for export, built textile mills, foundries and shipyards with European engineers, conscripted peasants into a modern army and set up schools and a printing press. His army took Syria and threatened Istanbul (1839); the European powers forced him back and the **Treaty of Balta Liman** (1838), imposed on the Ottoman Empire and extended to Egypt, abolished state monopolies and set a 5 percent tariff, so his factories could not compete with Lancashire. Egypt became a cotton plantation for Britain and, by 1882, a British protectorate.

**The Ottoman Tanzimat (1839–1876).** The reorganisation decrees promised equality before the law for all subjects regardless of religion, a conscript army, secular schools and law codes, provincial councils, railways and factories, and in 1876 a constitution. The reforms built a modern bureaucracy and army and a reforming elite, but the empire's debt to European banks ended in bankruptcy (1875) and European control of its revenue; Sultan Abdülhamid II suspended the constitution in 1878. Japan's reformers watched and drew lessons.

**Meiji Japan (1868–1912).** Commodore Perry's warships (1853) and unequal treaties (1858) ended Tokugawa isolation and discredited the shogunate; in 1868 a coalition of young samurai restored the emperor and ruled in his name. The **Charter Oath** (April 1868) promised deliberative assemblies, the unity of all classes in the state's affairs, freedom of occupation, the abandonment of evil customs of the past, and the search for knowledge throughout the world to strengthen imperial rule. The government abolished the domains (1871) and the samurai's stipends and swords (1876), conscripted a national army (1873), sent the Iwakura mission to study the West (1871–1873), built railways, telegraphs, a mint, model silk and cotton mills (the Tomioka silk mill, 1872), shipyards and arsenals, then sold most of the mills to private houses, the **zaibatsu**, which with state contracts and tariffs (after 1899) made Japan the only non-Western industrial power by 1900. The costs fell on peasants (a land tax paying for it all) and on women in the silk mills; the result was a state that defeated China (1895) and Russia (1905).

**Russia.** Defeat in the Crimea led to the **emancipation of the serfs** (1861) and, under Finance Minister **Sergei Witte** (1892–1903), to state-led industrialisation: the gold standard, high tariffs, French loans, and the **Trans-Siberian Railway** (1891–1916) to bind the empire and open Siberia. Coal, iron and oil output soared and St Petersburg and Moscow gained large factories and a small, concentrated working class; but the peasantry stayed poor and the autocracy unreformed, which is the setting of 1905 and 1917 (Unit 7).

**Comparison.** Britain industrialised from below, through private capital over seventy years; Japan, Russia and Egypt tried to do it from above in a generation. Japan succeeded because the state kept its sovereignty (and tariffs after 1899), had a literate population and a commercial economy from the Tokugawa period, and let private firms take over; Egypt failed because European powers took away its tariffs and then its government; Russia half succeeded, building industry on a rural society it did not reform.

## Primary sources to read closely

**Smith, The Wealth of Nations (1776), Book I, chapter I.** "One man draws out the wire; another straights it; a third cuts it; a fourth points it; a fifth grinds it at the top for receiving the head"; ten such workers "could make among them upwards of forty-eight thousand pins in a day." *Use:* the logic of the factory, stated before the steam factory existed.

**Elizabeth Bentley to the Sadler Committee (1832).** Asked when she began work in a factory, she answered "When I was six years old"; her hours "From 5 in the morning till 9 at night, when they were thronged"; and if she flagged, "Strap us." *Use:* the human cost of the early factory and the parliamentary inquiry that led to the Factory Act of 1833 (week 18).

## Common mistakes

- **"Britain industrialised because of the steam engine."** Cotton mills ran on water for a generation; coal, agriculture, capital, markets and institutions came first.
- **"Industrialisation spread evenly."** It spread to a few regions with coal and capital and destroyed handicraft industry elsewhere.
- **"Japan copied the West."** The Meiji state chose, adapted and sold off; it kept the emperor, built the zaibatsu and used Tokugawa literacy and commerce.
- **"Egypt failed because Muhammad Ali's ideas were wrong."** His programme resembled Japan's; the difference was European intervention and the loss of tariff sovereignty.
- **"The second industrial revolution was British."** Germany and the United States led in steel, chemicals and electricity.
