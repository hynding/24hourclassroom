## Africa: states built on trade and faith

Africa's societies ranged from stateless kin-based communities to large empires. The CED stresses that African states, like those elsewhere, grew where resources and trade routes met, and that religion (Islam in the north and east, Christianity in Ethiopia, local traditions almost everywhere) gave rulers legitimacy.

**Mali and the Sahel.** The savanna belt south of the Sahara, the Sahel, was the meeting place of two scarce goods: **gold** from the forests to the south and **salt** from mines in the desert. Whoever controlled the exchange grew rich. The **Mali Empire**, founded by Sundiata around 1230, taxed the trade, kept the routes safe and governed through provincial governors and a large cavalry. Its rulers were Muslim, which connected them to North African merchants and scholars; **Mansa Musa's** pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324, with a caravan of gold so large that Cairo's gold price fell for years, announced Mali to the world. Under Mali, **Timbuktu** grew into a city of mosques, scholars and manuscript copyists. Ibn Battuta, who visited in 1352–1353, admired the security of the roads, the people's devotion to prayer and their intolerance of injustice, while disapproving, as a Moroccan jurist, of women's freedom of movement and of customs that mixed Islam with older practice. Most farmers kept their own religions; Islam was the faith of the court and the towns. Mali declined in the 1400s and the **Songhai** Empire took its place.

**The Swahili coast.** From Mogadishu to Kilwa, a string of independent city-states lived by the Indian Ocean. African merchants sold gold, ivory, enslaved people and timber to Arab, Persian and Indian traders arriving on the monsoon and bought cloth, porcelain and glass. The towns' rulers and merchant elites adopted Islam by the 1100s–1200s; mosques of coral stone and the **Swahili** language, Bantu in grammar and rich in Arabic words, express the blend. Kilwa, which controlled the gold trade from the south, was described by travellers as one of the finest cities of its world. These were sea-based states on the week 2 model: no large territory, much wealth.

**Great Zimbabwe.** The gold that reached Kilwa came from the plateau between the Zambezi and Limpopo rivers, where a Shona kingdom built **Great Zimbabwe**, a capital of massive dry-stone walls housing perhaps 18,000 people at its height (c. 1250–1450). Cattle, gold and trade with the coast sustained it; its decline around 1450 is linked to exhausted land and shifting trade.

**Ethiopia.** In the northeast highlands the Christian kingdom of Ethiopia, Christian since the 300s, was reorganised under the **Solomonic dynasty** from 1270, whose kings claimed descent from Solomon and the Queen of Sheba. Earlier rulers had carved churches from solid rock at **Lalibela**; the kingdom traded through the Red Sea and alternately fought and traded with Muslim neighbours. It shows an African state built on a religion other than Islam and older than most of Europe's churches.

**The Hausa kingdoms.** In the central savanna, Hausa city-states such as **Kano** and **Katsina** lived by farming, cloth and leather crafts and trans-Saharan trade. Their rulers adopted Islam gradually in the 1300s–1400s; the cities remained independent of one another.

**Pattern.** Trade routes (trans-Saharan, Indian Ocean, Red Sea) created wealth; rulers who adopted Islam or Christianity gained literate officials, foreign allies and a universal creed; local religions persisted beneath. Only Ethiopia was a long-established territorial monarchy on the European model; most African states were networks of towns and tribute.

## Europe: faith, feudalism and the rise of kings

**Belief.** Europe in 1200 was Latin Christian in the west, Orthodox Christian in the east (Byzantium, Russia), with Muslim states in Iberia and Jewish communities in most towns, tolerated and periodically persecuted. The **Catholic Church** was the one institution that spanned the continent: it ran parishes, monasteries and the new **universities** (Paris, Bologna, Oxford), crowned kings, judged marriages and wills, and collected its own tax. **Scholastic** thinkers such as Thomas Aquinas reconciled Christian doctrine with Aristotle, whose works reached them through Arabic translations and commentaries from Muslim Spain and Sicily; this is a direct intellectual link to week 1. Religion also drove violence: crusades against Muslims in the Holy Land and Iberia, and expulsions of Jews from England (1290) and France (1306).

**Feudalism and manorialism.** Political power was local. Kings were weak; **lords** held land and private armies; **vassals** received fiefs in return for military service. Economically, the **manor** was the unit: **serfs**, bound to the land, worked the lord's fields and their own plots, paid dues and could not leave without permission, but could not be sold. This was a decentralised order built for defence in an age without strong states.

**Change by 1450.** Three things changed. First, **monarchies grew**: kings in England, France, Castile and Aragon built royal courts, taxes and professional soldiers; the **Hundred Years' War** (1337–1453) forced both England and France to raise national armies and taxes and gave each a sense of itself. Second, **limits on kings were written down**: in England, **Magna Carta** (1215) made the king promise that "no freemen shall be taken or imprisoned or disseised or exiled or in any way destroyed ... except by the lawful judgment of his peers or by the law of the land" and that no tax "shall be imposed on our kingdom, unless by common counsel of our kingdom" (Avalon Project text); parliaments and estates assemblies grew from the same need for consent to taxation. Third, **serfdom declined** in the west: population growth, towns and money rents loosened it before 1348, and the **Black Death** (week 7) then made labour scarce and bargaining power real; in eastern Europe lords tightened serfdom instead. Towns, guilds and merchant republics (Venice, Genoa, the Hanseatic towns) grew in the gaps between lords.

## Comparing African and European state-building

| | Mali / Swahili coast | Western Europe |
|---|---|---|
| Source of wealth | Long-distance trade in gold, salt, ivory | Agriculture on manors; growing towns and trade |
| Source of legitimacy | Islam plus local tradition; control of routes | Christian kingship sanctioned by the Church; feudal oaths |
| Political form | Empire over tributary provinces (Mali); independent city-states (Swahili, Hausa) | Fragmented lordships slowly centralising under kings |
| Universal institution | Islamic law and scholarship (Timbuktu) | The Catholic Church and universities |

## Primary source to read closely

**Magna Carta (1215), clauses 12 and 39 (Avalon Project text).**

> "No scutage not aid shall be imposed on our kingdom, unless by common counsel of our kingdom ... No freemen shall be taken or imprisoned or disseised or exiled or in any way destroyed, nor will we go upon him nor send upon him, except by the lawful judgment of his peers or by the law of the land."

*Historical situation:* King John, defeated in France and short of money, faced a baronial revolt; the barons forced him to seal the charter. *Audience and purpose:* the barons wrote for themselves ("freemen" meant men of property, not serfs) to protect their land and limit arbitrary taxation. *Why it matters:* it is evidence of feudal lords checking a king, not of democracy; but the principle that the king is under law and needs consent for taxes outlived its makers.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: comparison across regions.** Prompt: *Compare the role of religion in state-building in Mali and in Western Europe.* Strong answer: "In both, a universal religion gave rulers legitimacy and literate officials: Mali's mansas used Islam to connect with North African trade and scholarship, while European kings were crowned by the Church and governed with clerks trained in Church schools. But in Mali Islam was a court and merchant religion over a largely non-Muslim countryside, whereas in Europe the Church reached every village and could challenge kings directly."

**Worked example 2: causation.** Prompt: *Explain one cause of the growth of European monarchies by 1450.* Strong answer: "The Hundred Years' War required permanent taxation and standing armies; to get them, kings bargained with assemblies, built royal bureaucracies and bypassed feudal levies, so war strengthened the state." Name the cause, show the mechanism.

## Common mistakes

- **"Africa had no states before Europeans arrived."** Mali, Ethiopia, Great Zimbabwe and Kilwa all predate European contact.
- **"Mansa Musa's gold made Mali rich."** Mali was already rich; the pilgrimage displayed the wealth and spread Mali's reputation.
- **"Serfs were slaves."** Serfs were unfree but could not be sold apart from the land and had customary rights.
- **"Magna Carta created democracy."** It limited the king for the benefit of barons; later generations read wider meanings into it.
