## What you need to be able to do

- **9.4** Explain the causes and effects of economic change after 1980: free-market (neoliberal) policies under Reagan, Thatcher, Deng Xiaoping and Pinochet's Chile; the growth of multinational corporations; trade agreements and organisations (the EU, NAFTA, the WTO, ASEAN, Mercosur); the rise of the Asian tigers and China; outsourcing and supply chains; the uneven distribution of gains and the 2008 financial crisis.
- **9.5** Explain how and why people called for reform after 1900 and how states responded: human rights (the Universal Declaration, 1948), the rights of women, racial and ethnic minorities, religious minorities and LGBT people; movements against apartheid and caste discrimination; economic reform (microfinance); environmental reform; and the responses of governments, from reform to repression.
- **9.6** Explain how globalization changed culture: global popular culture (film, music, sport, television, the internet), the spread of English and of consumer brands, the revival and spread of religions (evangelical Christianity, Islam), the fusion of cultures (Bollywood, reggae, K-pop) and the reactions against cultural homogenisation.

In plain language: know why the market model won after 1980 and who gained and lost; know the main rights movements and documents since 1948 with examples of success and failure; and be able to give examples of global culture that flow in more than one direction.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Neoliberalism | Free-market policies from the late 1970s: privatisation, deregulation, lower taxes, free trade, weaker unions. |
| Washington Consensus | The IMF and World Bank's package of market reforms required of borrowing countries in the 1980s–90s (structural adjustment). |
| Deng Xiaoping's reforms (1978) | China's turn to markets: household farming, special economic zones, foreign investment, while the party kept power. |
| Asian tigers | South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong and Singapore, which industrialised through exports from the 1960s. |
| Multinational corporation | A firm producing and selling in many countries (Toyota, Shell, Apple, Walmart). |
| WTO (1995) | The World Trade Organization, successor to the GATT, setting and enforcing trade rules; China joined in 2001. |
| European Union | From the Coal and Steel Community (1951) to the single market (1993), the euro (1999) and 27 members. |
| NAFTA (1994) | The North American Free Trade Agreement among the United States, Canada and Mexico. |
| Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) | The UN's statement that all human beings are "born free and equal in dignity and rights". |
| Structural adjustment | IMF conditions on loans: cut spending, privatise, open markets; blamed for hardship in Africa and Latin America. |
| Microfinance | Small loans to the poor, pioneered by the Grameen Bank in Bangladesh (1983). |
| Cultural globalization | The worldwide spread and mixing of films, music, sport, brands, languages and religions. |

## The global economy (9.4)

**The turn to the market.** The 1970s brought inflation, oil shocks and stagnation to the Western economies and debt to the developing world. The answer, from the late 1970s, was a revival of nineteenth-century liberalism: Margaret Thatcher (Britain, 1979) and Ronald Reagan (United States, 1981) cut taxes and regulation, privatised state industries, broke strikes and let markets decide; Pinochet's Chile had tried it first (1973) under the "Chicago boys"; the IMF and World Bank imposed it on debtor countries as structural adjustment. Most consequential was **Deng Xiaoping's** China: from 1978 the communes were dissolved, peasants farmed for themselves, special economic zones invited foreign factories, and China grew at nearly 10 per cent a year for three decades while the party kept its monopoly of power. After 1991 the former Soviet bloc privatised too, often chaotically. By 2000 the market was the only model left; President Clinton, urging Congress to admit China to the WTO, argued that "By joining the W.T.O., China is not simply agreeing to import more of our products; it is agreeing to import one of democracy's most cherished values: economic freedom." The prediction's political half has not come true.

**Institutions and firms.** The GATT rounds cut tariffs for fifty years and became the **WTO** in 1995; regional blocs multiplied: the **European Union** (single market 1993, euro 1999), **NAFTA** (1994), ASEAN, Mercosur. **Multinational corporations** organised production across borders: design in California, chips from Taiwan, assembly in Shenzhen, sale everywhere; by the 2000s the largest firms had revenues larger than most states. Containers, the internet and cheap labour made outsourcing routine, moving manufacturing from the West to East Asia and services to India.

**Winners and losers.** The **Asian tigers** (South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong) had shown from the 1960s that export manufacturing under strong states could lift a poor country to wealth in a generation; China, Vietnam, Indonesia, India and others followed. World extreme poverty fell from about 40 per cent of humanity in 1980 to under 10 per cent by 2015, mostly in Asia. But inequality within countries rose: industrial regions of the United States and Europe lost factories, wages for the less skilled stagnated, and the richest 1 per cent captured a growing share. Sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Latin America stagnated under debt and structural adjustment in the 1980s (the "lost decade"). Financial deregulation produced crises: Mexico (1994), East Asia (1997), Russia (1998), and the **global financial crisis of 2008**, when American mortgage debt brought down banks worldwide and governments, remembering 1929, spent to prevent a depression. The backlash against globalization (week 32) grew from here.

| Economy | Policy | Result |
|---|---|---|
| United States and Britain (1980s) | tax cuts, privatisation, deregulation, weaker unions | growth and inequality; deindustrialisation |
| China (from 1978) | market reforms under one-party rule; export zones | 800 million out of poverty; world's second economy |
| Asian tigers (from 1960s) | state-guided export industrialization | rich within a generation |
| Chile (from 1973) | free-market shock under dictatorship | growth after crisis; inequality |
| Latin America and Africa (1980s) | debt and structural adjustment | lost decade; later recovery uneven |
| Former Soviet bloc (1990s) | rapid privatisation | collapse then recovery; oligarchs |

## Calls for reform (9.5)

**Rights.** The UN's **Universal Declaration of Human Rights** (1948), drafted under Eleanor Roosevelt, declared that all people are born free and equal in dignity and rights; covenants (1966), the Helsinki process (1975), Amnesty International (1961) and Human Rights Watch made rights a global language that governments had to answer. Women's movements won legal equality across much of the world, the UN Decade for Women (1975–85), the convention against discrimination (CEDAW, 1979) and the Beijing conference (1995); gaps in pay, violence and representation remain, and some states resisted. Racial and ethnic minorities fought discrimination from the American South to apartheid South Africa (ended 1994) to India's caste system (reservations for Dalits; B. R. Ambedkar's constitution of 1950) to indigenous peoples' claims in the Americas and Australia. Religious minorities and LGBT people won protections in some countries and persecution in others; the pattern was uneven and contested.

**Economic and environmental reform.** Muhammad Yunus's Grameen Bank (1983) lent small sums to poor women; microfinance spread worldwide with mixed results. Debt-relief campaigns (Jubilee 2000) wrote off some poor-country debt. Environmental reformers won the laws and treaties of week 30. Fair trade, labour standards and corporate codes answered the sweatshop scandals of the 1990s.

**Responses.** States reformed (civil rights acts, apartheid's end, women's suffrage in Switzerland in 1971 and Saudi Arabia in 2015), co-opted (official women's organisations) or repressed (Tiananmen 1989, the persecution of dissidents in many states). International law gained tribunals for Yugoslavia and Rwanda and the International Criminal Court (2002), and lost enforcement wherever great powers refused.

## Global culture (9.6)

**Flows.** Hollywood, American television, Coca-Cola, McDonald's and blue jeans spread with American power; English became the language of business, science and the internet. But culture flowed in every direction: Bollywood reached Africa and the Middle East; Japanese anime and games and Korean pop and television conquered Asia and then the world; reggae from Jamaica, salsa, Afrobeat and hip-hop crossed every border; football's World Cup and the Olympics became the largest shared events in history; Nollywood became the world's second-largest film industry by volume. Migrants carried food, music and faith: curry in Britain, Turkish kebabs in Germany, Mexican food everywhere.

**Religion.** Far from fading, religion globalised: evangelical and Pentecostal Christianity grew fastest in Latin America, Africa and Asia; Islamic revival movements spread from Egypt and Iran to Southeast Asia and the diaspora; the Catholic Church elected a Polish pope (1978) and an Argentine one (2013); Hindu nationalism rose in India; new religious movements and secularism grew in Europe. Religion was both a carrier of global culture and a reaction against it.

**Reactions.** Fear of homogenisation (Americanisation, McDonaldisation) produced cultural protection (French film quotas, language laws), revivals of local tradition and, at the extreme, the violent rejection taken up in week 32. The exam wants examples of both flow and resistance, and of fusion: culture in the global age is remixed, not replaced.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating globalization as Americanisation only.** K-pop, Bollywood, Nollywood and anime are on the exam.
- **Confusing the tigers with China.** The tigers industrialised under American-allied states from the 1960s; China under communist rule from 1978.
- **Forgetting the losers.** Deindustrialised regions, structural-adjustment Africa and the 2008 crisis explain the backlash.
- **Writing about rights without documents and dates.** 1948, 1966, 1979, 1995, 2002.
