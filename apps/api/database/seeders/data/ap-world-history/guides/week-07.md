# Week 07 · What Connection Did: Cultures, Crops, Cities and Plague

**Topics:** 2.5 Cultural Consequences of Connectivity · 2.6 Environmental Consequences of Connectivity · 2.7 Comparison of Economic Exchange
**Themes:** CDI, ENV, ECN, TEC, SIO (Cultural Developments and Interactions; Humans and the Environment; Economic Systems; Technology and Innovation; Social Interactions and Organization)
**Reasoning process:** Causation (the effects of the three networks) and comparison (the unit's capstone: the networks side by side)
**Built from:** Giovanni Boccaccio, *The Decameron*, Introduction to the First Day (trans. J. M. Rigg, 1903); the travels of Ibn Battuta, Marco Polo and Margery Kempe described in our own words; the week 5 and week 6 guides
**Spiral:** Weeks 5 and 6 built the three networks. This week asks what they did to the people who lived along them: the religions and literatures they carried, the crops and diseases they moved, the cities they made and unmade. Unit 1's states and faiths are the material; the Black Death is the hinge to Unit 3.

## What you need to be able to do

- **2.5** Explain the intellectual and cultural effects of the networks: the diffusion of religions (Islam into Southeast Asia and the Sahel, Buddhism into Southeast Asia, Neo-Confucianism in East Asia), of literary and artistic traditions, and of scientific and technological knowledge (gunpowder, printing, paper, the compass, Greek and Islamic learning reaching Europe); the rise and fall of cities (Hangzhou, Baghdad, Constantinople, Timbuktu, Samarkand, Kashgar); and the travellers (Ibn Battuta, Marco Polo, Margery Kempe) whose accounts are our evidence.
- **2.6** Explain the environmental effects: the diffusion of crops (bananas across Africa, Champa rice into China, citrus and sugar into the Mediterranean, cotton) and of disease, above all the Black Death of the 1340s.
- **2.7** Compare the causes and effects of the Silk Roads, the Indian Ocean and the trans-Saharan networks: similar causes (demand, technology, state support), similar effects (cities, religion, cultural exchange, disease), and the differences in goods and reach.

In plain language: connection changed what people believed, what they ate, where they lived and what killed them. Be able to give an example of each and compare the three networks.

## Cultural consequences (2.5)

**Religions travelled with merchants.** The same diasporic communities that carried pepper carried faith. Islam reached the ports of Southeast Asia and the Swahili coast by trade, not conquest; Sufi teachers made it local by tolerating existing custom, a process the CED calls **syncretism**. In West Africa, Mali's rulers were Muslim but their subjects mixed Islam with older practice, which is why Ibn Battuta, visiting in 1352, both praised the people's devotion in prayer and complained about customs he thought un-Islamic. Buddhism had reached Southeast Asia and China on the same routes centuries earlier; in this period Theravada Buddhism spread in mainland Southeast Asia while, in East Asia, **Neo-Confucianism** moved from Song China into Korea, Japan and Vietnam through the Chinese written language and the examination model. Hinduism and Buddhism shaped the court cultures of Srivijaya, Majapahit and Angkor.

**Knowledge travelled too.** Chinese paper, printing, gunpowder and the compass reached the Islamic world and Europe (week 5); Islamic mathematics, astronomy, medicine and Greek philosophy in Arabic reached Europe through Spain and Sicily, feeding the universities of week 3. Persian became the literary language from Anatolia to India; Arabic the language of law and science across Dar al-Islam. Artistic styles blended: Chinese motifs appear in Persian manuscript painting under the Ilkhans; Islamic geometry appears in Indian and African mosques built of local materials, such as the mud-brick Great Mosque of Djenné.

**Cities rose and fell.** Connection made cities. **Hangzhou** grew to perhaps a million people on the Grand Canal and the sea trade; **Samarkand** and **Kashgar** flourished on the Silk Roads; **Timbuktu** and **Djenné** on the trans-Saharan routes; **Kilwa**, **Calicut** and **Malacca** on the ocean. Connection also unmade them: **Baghdad** never recovered from the Mongol sack of 1258 and the collapse of Mesopotamian irrigation; **Constantinople** shrank as Venetian and Genoese merchants took its trade and the Ottomans took its hinterland, until it fell in 1453; the Mongol conquests emptied Merv and Nishapur; and the plague halved the population of Cairo and Florence.

**Travellers are our evidence.** Three are named by the CED. **Ibn Battuta** (1304–1368 or 1369) left Tangier for the hajj in 1325 and did not return for twenty-four years: Mecca, Persia, the Swahili coast, Anatolia, the Golden Horde, Delhi (where he served as a judge), the Maldives, Ceylon, Sumatra, China, then Spain and Mali. His *Rihla*, dictated to a scholar in Fez, is the fullest picture of Dar al-Islam as one world. **Marco Polo** (1254–1324) travelled with his father and uncle to Kublai's court, served the Yuan for seventeen years and dictated his book in a Genoese prison; it told Europe of paper money, coal and cities larger than any it knew. **Margery Kempe** (c. 1373–after 1438), an English townswoman and mystic, dictated an account of her pilgrimages to Jerusalem, Rome and Santiago de Compostela; it shows that long-distance travel was possible for a woman of means and that Christian pilgrimage used the same routes and ships as trade.

## Environmental consequences (2.6)

**Crops moved and changed landscapes.** The CED names four diffusions. **Bananas**, domesticated in Southeast Asia, crossed the Indian Ocean to East Africa and spread through the forest zones of Africa, where they supported denser settlement and the Bantu-speaking peoples' expansion. **Champa rice**, from the kingdom of Champa in Vietnam, reached Song China around 1000; because it ripened in sixty days and tolerated drought, farmers could harvest twice a year, which fed the population boom behind the Song commercial revolution (week 1). **Citrus fruits**, **sugar**, cotton and new irrigation techniques moved west with Muslim farmers into the Mediterranean (Sicily, Spain, Egypt), part of what historians call the medieval agricultural exchange within Dar al-Islam; sugar in particular would become the crop of the Atlantic plantation (Unit 4). Each diffusion changed the environment: terraces, canals, cleared forest, new pests.

**Disease moved with people.** The **Black Death** was the price of the Pax Mongolica. Bubonic plague, carried by fleas on rodents, spread from Central Asia along Mongol routes in the 1330s and 1340s, reaching the Black Sea by 1346, Constantinople, Alexandria and Sicily in 1347, and the rest of Europe and the Islamic Mediterranean by 1350. Ibn Battuta, returning west in 1348, found the plague ahead of him in Syria and Egypt and described, in a passage we summarise rather than quote, the daily death tolls in Damascus and Cairo. Boccaccio, in Florence, wrote the most famous European account (below). Perhaps a third of the population of Europe and the Middle East died; China's losses, though disputed, were heavy. Consequences: labour shortages that raised wages and weakened serfdom in Western Europe, religious movements of penance and persecution (Jewish communities were massacred in the Rhineland), the end of the Pax Mongolica as trade collapsed, and in the longer run the social changes behind the Renaissance and the Ottoman rise.

## Comparing the networks (2.7)

The CED's last topic in Unit 2 is a comparison. Use week 6's table and add the causes and effects.

| | Silk Roads | Indian Ocean | Trans-Saharan |
|---|---|---|---|
| Similar causes | Demand for luxuries; state support (Mongols); commercial practices and credit | Demand for spices and cloth; port states; Islamic law and credit; monsoon knowledge | Demand for gold and salt; Mali's protection; camel technology |
| Similar effects | Cities (Samarkand, Kashgar); Islam and Buddhism spread; technology transfer; plague | Cities (Kilwa, Calicut, Malacca); Islam spread; diasporic communities; crops (bananas, Champa rice) | Cities (Timbuktu, Djenné); Islam spread; learning; the gold that fed Europe's coinage |
| Key difference | Overland luxuries; Mongol unity | Bulk goods possible; no single power | Two necessities (gold and salt); enslaved people a major cargo |

An AP comparison asks you to state a similarity or difference *and* explain why it existed. Why did all three spread Islam? Because Muslim merchants dominated all three and settled where they traded. Why did only the ocean carry bulk? Because ships carry more than camels. Why did the Silk Roads decline after 1350 while the ocean did not? Because the Mongol peace broke and the plague struck, while the monsoon kept blowing.

## Primary source to read closely

**Boccaccio, *The Decameron*, Introduction to the First Day (trans. J. M. Rigg, 1903).** "I say, then, that the years of the beatific incarnation of the Son of God had reached the tale of one thousand three hundred and forty-eight when in the illustrious city of Florence" the deadly pestilence arrived. It "first betrayed itself by the emergence of certain tumours in the groin or the armpits, some of which grew as large as a common apple, others as an egg, some more, some less, which the common folk called gavoccioli." Worst of all was the breaking of human ties: "brother was forsaken by brother, nephew by uncle, brother by sister, and oftentimes husband by wife; nay, what is more, and scarcely to be believed, fathers and mothers were found to abandon their own children, untended, unvisited, to their fate, as if they had been strangers." He reckoned that "upwards of a hundred thousand human beings lost their lives within the walls of the city of Florence."

*Sourcing:* Boccaccio was a Florentine writer framing a collection of stories; his figure for the dead is a round estimate, and his description serves a literary purpose (to explain why ten young people flee the city). *Use:* strong evidence for symptoms and social breakdown; treat the numbers as impression, not census. Compare with Ibn Battuta's account of Damascus, where he describes the whole population, Muslim, Jewish and Christian, praying together for relief: the same plague, a different social response.

## Historical thinking worked example

*Prompt:* "Explain one environmental effect of increased connectivity in the period c. 1200 to c. 1450." *Weak:* "The Black Death spread." *Strong:* "Because Mongol rule made the Silk Roads safe for merchants and the yam moved people quickly across Central Asia, the plague bacterium carried by rodents and fleas spread west along those routes, reaching the Black Sea by 1346 and the Mediterranean by 1347; the result was the death of perhaps a third of the population of Europe and the Middle East, which raised wages, weakened serfdom in Western Europe and ended the Mongol-era boom in overland trade." The strong answer names the cause, the mechanism and the specific effect.

## Common mistakes

- **"Connection was always good."** It brought the plague as surely as it brought paper.
- **"The Black Death was only European."** It struck Cairo, Damascus and probably China first; Ibn Battuta saw it in Syria before Boccaccio saw it in Florence.
- **"Champa rice came from China."** It came from Vietnam into China, which is the point: diffusion.
- **"Baghdad stayed the centre of the Islamic world."** After 1258 it did not; Cairo did.
