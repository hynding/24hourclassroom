# Week 29 · Global Resistance, the End of the Cold War, and Causation

**Topics:** 8.7 Global Resistance to Established Order After 1900 · 8.8 End of the Cold War · 8.9 Causation in the Age of the Cold War and Decolonization
**Themes:** SIO (Social Interactions and Organization), GOV (Governance), CDI (Cultural Developments and Interactions), ECN (Economic Systems), TEC (Technology and Innovation)
**Reasoning processes:** Causation (the unit's capstone: why the Cold War ended and what the age's events caused); comparison (non-violent and violent resistance; movements in the two blocs and the third world)
**Spiral:** The civil rights and anti-apartheid struggles are the colour line of 6.1 and 7.5 fought at home; Gandhi's satyagraha (7.5) is the method of King and the early ANC; the Cold War structure of 8.2 and 8.3 is what ends in 1989–91; the Soviet command economy of 7.4 is what Gorbachev tried to reform; the nationalist and religious movements of 8.5 and 8.6 return as resistance to the new states.

## What you need to be able to do

- **8.7** Explain various reactions to existing power structures after 1900: non-violent movements (the Indian independence movement, the U.S. civil rights movement, the anti-apartheid struggle), youth and student protest (1968 in Paris, Prague, Mexico City and the United States), feminist and environmental movements, religious movements (Liberation Theology, the Iranian Revolution of 1979), and the turn to violence by some groups (the Shining Path, the IRA, Palestinian and other nationalist and religious terrorism).
- **8.8** Explain the causes of the end of the Cold War: the economic and political failures of the Soviet system, the cost of the arms race and Afghanistan, Reagan's pressure, Gorbachev's reforms (glasnost, perestroika), the revolutions of 1989 in eastern Europe, German reunification (1990) and the dissolution of the Soviet Union (1991).
- **8.9** Explain the relative significance of the causes of the Cold War and decolonization and of their consequences: the unit's causation review.

In plain language: know five movements against established power with a leader, method and result each; be able to explain the end of the Cold War with internal and external causes ranked; and be ready to write a causation essay for the whole unit.

## Resistance to established order (8.7)

**Non-violence.** Gandhi's satyagraha travelled. In the United States, Martin Luther King Jr. and the movement that began with the Montgomery bus boycott (1955–56) used boycotts, sit-ins, freedom rides and marches to break segregation in the South; Birmingham and the March on Washington (1963) pushed Congress to pass the Civil Rights Act (1964) and the Voting Rights Act (1965). King was assassinated in 1968; Malcolm X and the Black Power movement argued for self-defence and pride. In South Africa the ANC used defiance campaigns until the Sharpeville massacre (1960) drove it to armed struggle; Nelson Mandela was imprisoned from 1962 to 1990; the Soweto rising (1976), township revolts, international sanctions and the economic cost led F. W. de Klerk to negotiate; the first non-racial election made Mandela president in April 1994. Both movements used the Cold War: American racism was a Soviet propaganda gift, and apartheid's anti-communism bought it Western tolerance until the 1980s.

**1968 and the young.** Students in Paris (May 1968, with a general strike of ten million), Mexico City (the Tlatelolco massacre before the Olympics), West Germany, Italy, Japan and the United States (against the Vietnam War and the draft) challenged governments, universities and the older generation. In Prague, Alexander Dubcek's "socialism with a human face" loosened censorship until Soviet tanks ended it; the Brezhnev Doctrine followed. The year changed cultures more than governments.

**Women and the environment.** Second-wave feminism demanded equal pay, access to professions, contraception and abortion rights and an end to legal subordination: the Equal Pay Acts, the UN's Decade for Women (1975–85), women's suffrage in Switzerland (1971) and the spread of women into universities and parliaments. The environmental movement grew from Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring* (1962, described here) to Earth Day (1970), Greenpeace (1971) and the first UN environment conference (Stockholm, 1972); Nixon's State of the Union in 1970 called clean air and water "the common cause of all the people of this country" (week 31 returns to this).

**Religion against the order.** Liberation Theology in Latin America (Gutiérrez, Romero, murdered in 1980) set the Church against dictatorship and poverty. In Iran the Shah's modernisation, repression by SAVAK and dependence on the United States produced a revolution led by the Shia clergy: Ayatollah Khomeini returned in February 1979, an Islamic Republic was proclaimed, American hostages were held for 444 days, and Iraq's invasion (1980) began an eight-year war. President Carter answered the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan the same winter with the Carter Doctrine (January 1980): "An attempt by any outside force to gain control of the Persian Gulf region will be regarded as an assault on the vital interests of the United States of America, and such an assault will be repelled by any means necessary, including military force." Political Islam, from the Muslim Brotherhood to the Afghan mujahideen, became a force in its own right.

**Violence.** Some groups chose terror or guerrilla war against states they could not move otherwise: the IRA in Northern Ireland (1969–98), ETA in Spain, the Palestine Liberation Organization and later Hamas, the Shining Path in Peru (1980s), the Red Brigades in Italy and the Red Army Faction in West Germany, and religious terrorism culminating in al-Qaeda's attacks of 11 September 2001 (week 31). The exam asks you to compare methods and to explain why some movements turned violent (closed political systems, settler or occupying states, the failure of non-violence) and what followed.

| Movement | Where | Method | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Civil rights | United States, 1954–68 | non-violent direct action; courts; legislation | legal segregation ended; inequality persisted |
| Anti-apartheid | South Africa, 1912–94 | defiance, then armed struggle, strikes, sanctions | majority rule 1994 |
| 1968 | France, Mexico, Czechoslovakia, United States | strikes, demonstrations, reform from within | cultural change; Prague crushed |
| Feminism | global, 1960s onward | legislation, organisation, UN conferences | legal equality widely won; gaps remain |
| Iranian Revolution | Iran, 1978–79 | mass protest led by clergy | Islamic Republic; a model and a warning |
| Solidarity | Poland, 1980–89 | strikes, independent union, negotiation | first free elections in the bloc, 1989 |

## The end of the Cold War (8.8)

**Internal causes.** The Soviet command economy that had built heavy industry could not produce computers, consumer goods or enough food; growth stalled in the 1970s; perhaps a quarter of output went to arms; the satellites were in debt to Western banks; the war in Afghanistan (1979–89) cost 15,000 Soviet dead and all legitimacy; Chernobyl (1986) exposed the system's secrecy. **Mikhail Gorbachev** (general secretary from 1985) tried to save the system by reforming it: *glasnost* let the press and the public speak, *perestroika* loosened central planning, and he withdrew from Afghanistan, cut arms with Reagan (the INF Treaty, 1987) and told the satellites in 1988–89 that the Brezhnev Doctrine was dead. Reform released forces it could not control.

**External pressure.** Reagan's buildup (the 600-ship navy, the Strategic Defense Initiative of 1983, aid to the Afghan mujahideen and Nicaraguan contras) raised the cost of competition and, in his 1983 speech to the National Association of Evangelicals, named "the aggressive impulses of an evil empire"; at the Brandenburg Gate in June 1987 he challenged: "Mr. Gorbachev, open this gate! Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall!" Pope John Paul II's visits to Poland (from 1979), Western broadcasting and the Helsinki human-rights process encouraged dissidents; Solidarity survived martial law (1981–83).

**1989–91.** Poland's round-table talks produced semi-free elections (June 1989) that Solidarity swept; Hungary opened its border with Austria (summer 1989), letting East Germans out; mass demonstrations in Leipzig and the East German government's confusion opened the Berlin Wall on 9 November; Czechoslovakia's Velvet Revolution (November) and Romania's violent one (December) followed; Germany reunified in October 1990; the Warsaw Pact dissolved in 1991. In the Soviet Union, nationalism in the Baltic republics, Ukraine and the Caucasus and Yeltsin's Russia outran Gorbachev; hardliners staged a coup in August 1991 that collapsed in three days; the republics declared independence and the USSR ceased to exist on 25 December 1991. The Cold War ended without a war between the superpowers, which deterrence had made unthinkable and which the Soviet system could no longer afford.

## Causation in the age of the Cold War and decolonization (8.9)

Causes to rank: the outcome of the Second World War (structural); ideology and security (the Cold War's origins); nationalism and imperial exhaustion (decolonization); economics (the Soviet failure; the costs of empire); individuals (Gandhi, Mao, Gorbachev, Reagan); technology (the bomb, which prevented direct war and shaped everything else). Consequences: a divided then reunited Europe; some 25 million dead in proxy wars; about 100 new states; the superpower confrontation replaced by American predominance and the globalization of unit 9; and the movements of 8.7, which outlived the Cold War that framed them.

## Common mistakes

- **Crediting Reagan alone, or Gorbachev alone.** The exam wants internal decay, external pressure and the choices of leaders and peoples weighed together.
- **Treating 1989 as spontaneous.** Solidarity, Hungary's border decision and Gorbachev's refusal to intervene made it possible; Tiananmen (June 1989) shows the alternative.
- **Writing 8.7 as a list of good causes.** Compare methods and explain results; include religious and violent movements.
- **Forgetting that resistance also targeted the new states.** The Iranian Revolution overthrew a modernising monarchy; the Shining Path fought a democracy.
