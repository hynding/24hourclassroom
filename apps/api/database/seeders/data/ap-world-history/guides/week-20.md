## What you need to be able to do

- **6.3** Explain how and why internal and external factors shaped responses to state expansion from 1750 to 1900: direct armed resistance, the creation of new states on the edges of empires, and religiously inspired movements; and explain why most failed against industrial armies while a few, such as Ethiopia, succeeded.

In plain language: know at least six named responses across four continents, be able to sort them into the three CED categories, and explain the difference between the outcomes at Adwa (1896) and at Omdurman (1898).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Direct resistance | Armed opposition to conquest or to colonial rule, from field battles to guerrilla war. |
| Peripheral state | A new state formed on the edge of an expanding empire by people pushed out of it or reorganising to resist it. |
| Religious revitalization movement | A movement that answered conquest with prophecy and ritual promising a restored world (the Ghost Dance, the Xhosa cattle killing, the Mahdist movement). |
| Túpac Amaru II | Leader of a 1780–81 Andean uprising against Spanish taxes and forced labour; executed in Cuzco in 1781. |
| Sepoy Rebellion (1857–58) | Uprising of Indian soldiers and civilians against the East India Company that ended Company rule. |
| Samory Touré | Founder of the Wassoulou Empire in West Africa who fought the French from 1882 to 1898. |
| Yaa Asantewaa | Asante queen mother who led the 1900 war against Britain over the Golden Stool. |
| Anglo-Zulu War (1879) | Britain's invasion of the Zulu kingdom; Zulu victory at Isandlwana, then defeat at Ulundi. |
| Mahdist state | Sudanese state (1885–98) founded by Muhammad Ahmad, the Mahdi, after taking Khartoum; destroyed at Omdurman. |
| Ghost Dance (1889–90) | Lakota and other Plains peoples' religious movement promising the return of the bison and the dead; ended with the Wounded Knee massacre. |
| Xhosa cattle killing (1856–57) | Prophecy-driven slaughter of cattle in the eastern Cape that caused famine and broke Xhosa independence. |
| Adwa (1896) | Ethiopian victory over Italy under Menelik II; the one decisive African military success against the scramble. |
| Cherokee Nation | A Native American state with a written constitution (1827) whose legal resistance failed against removal in the 1830s. |

## Pattern 1: direct armed resistance

Across every continent the first response to conquest was to fight. The results depended on timing (before or after the machine gun), on access to modern weapons, and on whether the resisters could unite rival groups.

**The Americas.** In the Andes, **Túpac Amaru II**, a descendant of the Inca line, led tens of thousands in 1780–81 against the Bourbon reforms' heavier taxes and the *mita* labour draft; the revolt was crushed and he was executed in Cuzco, but it shook Spanish rule a generation before the wars of independence. On the North American Plains, Lakota, Cheyenne and Arapaho resistance peaked with the destruction of Custer's command at the Little Bighorn (1876) and ended with reservations and the massacre at Wounded Knee (1890). In Argentina and Chile, the "Conquest of the Desert" (1878–85) and the occupation of Araucanía broke Mapuche and Patagonian independence.

**Africa.** In Algeria, **Abd al-Qadir** led a fifteen-year resistance to the French (1832–47). On the Gold Coast, the Asante fought Britain through the century; in 1900 **Yaa Asantewaa**, queen mother of Ejisu, led the War of the Golden Stool after the governor demanded the stool itself, the symbol of the Asante nation. In West Africa, **Samory Touré** built the Wassoulou Empire with a disciplined army, bought and then manufactured rifles, and fought the French from 1882 until his capture in 1898. In southern Africa, the Zulu kingdom founded under Shaka destroyed a British column at **Isandlwana** (January 1879) with spear-armed regiments before being defeated at Ulundi six months later. In East Africa, the **Maji Maji** rising (1905–07) against German cotton quotas in Tanganyika belongs to the next decade but follows the same pattern.

**Asia and the Pacific.** The **Sepoy Rebellion** of 1857–58 began among Company soldiers at Meerut, angered by rumours that new cartridges were greased with cow and pig fat, and spread to peasants and dispossessed princes across northern India; it was suppressed with great violence and ended Company rule (week 19). In Vietnam, the Can Vuong ("Aid the King") movement of 1885–89 fought the French in the name of the emperor. In New Zealand, Maori resistance in the land wars (1845–72) included fortified *pa* that held off British artillery. In the Philippines, the independence forces that had fought Spain fought the United States from 1899 to 1902.

**Why most failed.** Industrial weapons (week 19), the telegraph and steamship, and the imperial powers' ability to recruit colonial troops (Senegalese *tirailleurs*, Indian sepoys, Egyptian regiments) gave them an edge no single African or Asian polity could match after about 1880; divisions among local states let the invaders find allies. Resistance that came earlier, or that had modern arms and unity, did better.

**Why Ethiopia succeeded.** **Menelik II** bought tens of thousands of modern rifles and artillery from France, Russia and even Italy itself, united Ethiopia's regional lords behind a defence of the Christian empire, and in March 1896 destroyed the Italian army at **Adwa**. Italy recognised Ethiopian independence in the Treaty of Addis Ababa. Compare Omdurman two years later, where the Mahdist army, armed mostly with spears and old rifles, lost about 10,000 men to Maxim guns in a morning.

## Pattern 2: new states on the periphery

Expansion sometimes created states rather than destroying them. Peoples displaced or threatened by empires reorganised, often borrowing the empire's own tools.

- The **Cherokee Nation** in the southeastern United States adopted a written language (Sequoyah's syllabary), a newspaper and a constitution (1827) modelled on the American one, and took its case to the Supreme Court, which ruled in its favour in *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832). The state of Georgia and President Jackson ignored the ruling and the Cherokee were removed west on the Trail of Tears (1838–39), which shows the limits of legal resistance.
- The **Zulu kingdom** was itself a product of the upheavals of early nineteenth-century southern Africa (the *mfecane*): Shaka's regimental army created a centralised state that then confronted Boer settlers and the British.
- The **Sokoto Caliphate** (founded 1804 by Usman dan Fodio's jihad) was the largest state in West Africa in the nineteenth century; when Britain conquered it in 1903 Lugard kept its emirs in place as the basis of indirect rule.
- The **Mahdist state** in Sudan (1885–98) and **Samory's Wassoulou Empire** were states built to resist; so, in a different way, were the Boer republics (Transvaal, Orange Free State) formed by Dutch-descended settlers trekking away from British rule at the Cape.

## Pattern 3: religiously inspired movements

When armies failed, some peoples turned to prophecy. The **Ghost Dance** spread from the Paiute prophet Wovoka in 1889: dancers believed the dead would return, the bison come back and the settlers vanish. Its spread among the Lakota alarmed the U.S. Army; the killing of Sitting Bull and the massacre of about 250 Lakota at **Wounded Knee** in December 1890 ended it. The Bureau of American Ethnology's report on the movement by James Mooney (1896) is the fullest contemporary account; it treats the dance as a religion of despair rather than a war plan.

In the eastern Cape in 1856–57 the prophetess Nongqawuse told the **Xhosa** that if they killed their cattle and destroyed their grain, their ancestors would rise and drive the British into the sea. Up to 400,000 cattle were killed; tens of thousands starved, and the famine completed the British conquest of Xhosaland more thoroughly than any battle.

In Sudan, **Muhammad Ahmad** declared himself the Mahdi (the guided one) in 1881, defeated Egyptian and British-led forces, took Khartoum in 1885 and founded a state that lasted until Omdurman. In India the 1857 rising drew on fears for both Hindu and Muslim religion; in China the Boxer movement of 1899–1901 (week 21) combined martial-arts ritual with the belief that its members were immune to bullets.

Religious movements expressed, in their own idiom, the same thing the armies had: a refusal to accept conquest as permanent.

## Common mistakes

- **"Africans and Asians did not resist."** They resisted everywhere; the CED's point is to explain the forms resistance took and why outcomes differed.
- **Treating religious movements as irrational.** The Ghost Dance and the cattle killing were responses to the collapse of the bison economy and of Xhosa cattle herds (lung-sickness had already killed many); prophecy made sense of catastrophe.
- **Forgetting the Americas.** Túpac Amaru II and the Cherokee are CED examples; the unit is global.
- **Explaining Adwa by luck.** Menelik's rifles, artillery, diplomacy and unity were deliberate; the contrast with Omdurman is about arms and organisation.
