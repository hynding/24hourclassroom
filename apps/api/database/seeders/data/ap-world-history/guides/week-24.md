## What you need to be able to do

- **7.3** Explain how governments used a variety of methods to conduct war: total war (mobilising economies and populations), new technologies (machine guns, artillery, poison gas, tanks, aircraft, submarines), propaganda, the use of colonial troops and labour, and the war's human cost.
- **7.4** Explain how different governments responded to economic crisis after 1900: the Great Depression's causes and spread, the New Deal in the United States, fascist corporatism in Italy and Germany, the Soviet Five-Year Plans and collectivisation, and the Depression's effects on colonial and Latin American export economies.

In plain language: know what "total war" means with three examples, know which technologies made 1914–18 so deadly, know how the war reached Africa and Asia, and be able to compare three political answers to the Depression.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Total war | A war in which whole economies and populations are mobilised and civilians become targets and workers for the war effort. |
| Trench warfare | The stalemate on the Western Front (1914–18) in which defensive firepower made attacks ruinously costly. |
| Unrestricted submarine warfare | Germany's sinking of merchant ships without warning (1915, resumed 1917), which brought the United States into the war. |
| Zimmermann telegram (1917) | Germany's offer of alliance to Mexico, intercepted by Britain and published in the United States. |
| Propaganda | State-managed information to sustain morale, recruit, and demonise the enemy. |
| Colonial troops | Soldiers from empires: about 1.4 million Indians, hundreds of thousands of Africans, Vietnamese and others. |
| Gold standard | The fixing of currencies to gold, restored after the war and abandoned in the Depression. |
| Great Depression | The worldwide economic collapse that began with the U.S. stock-market crash of October 1929. |
| New Deal | Franklin Roosevelt's programme of relief, public works and regulation in the United States (1933–39). |
| Corporatism | The fascist organisation of economy by state-controlled bodies of employers and workers, with strikes banned. |
| Five-Year Plans | Stalin's programmes of forced industrialization from 1928. |
| Collectivisation | The forced merger of Soviet peasant farms into collective farms (1929–33), with famine in Ukraine and Kazakhstan. |

## Total war (7.3)

**Why the war was so deadly.** The industrial technologies of unit 5 reached the battlefield: magazine rifles and **machine guns** that fired hundreds of rounds a minute, quick-firing **artillery** that caused most deaths, barbed wire, **poison gas** (first used at Ypres, 1915), **tanks** (1916), **aircraft** for reconnaissance and then bombing, and **submarines** that attacked merchant shipping. Defensive firepower outran the means of attack, so the Western Front froze into trenches from the Channel to Switzerland; the Somme (1916) cost Britain about 57,000 casualties on its first day, and Verdun (1916) about 700,000 casualties in all. Railways moved millions; the telegraph and telephone coordinated them. About 9–10 million soldiers and several million civilians died; the influenza pandemic of 1918–19 killed tens of millions more.

**Mobilising whole societies.** Governments conscripted men, rationed food, took over railways and factories, borrowed and printed money, and set prices (Germany's war economy under Rathenau; Britain's Ministry of Munitions). Women entered factories, transport and farms in millions, a change that fed the extension of the vote (Britain 1918, Germany 1919, the United States 1920). **Propaganda** ministries used posters, film and censorship to sustain the effort and paint the enemy as a barbarian; Britain's control of the transatlantic cables shaped what Americans read. Wilfred Owen, an officer killed a week before the armistice, answered the recruiting rhetoric in "Dulce et Decorum Est" (published 1920): "My friend, you would not tell with such high zest / To children ardent for some desperate glory, / The old Lie: Dulce et decorum est / Pro patria mori."

**A world war.** Empires fought with their colonies. About 1.4 million Indians served, in France, Mesopotamia and East Africa; France recruited several hundred thousand West and North Africans (the *tirailleurs sénégalais*) and some 100,000 Vietnamese workers; Britain's African campaigns against German colonies used about a million African carriers, of whom perhaps 100,000 died; Chinese labourers dug trenches in France. Japan seized Germany's Pacific islands and Shandong; the Ottomans fought in the Caucasus, Gallipoli (1915) and Mesopotamia; Britain promised Arabs independence and Jews a national home in Palestine (week 25). The war ended empires in Europe and shook them elsewhere: soldiers who had fought for the British or French came home with claims.

**The United States enters.** Germany's **unrestricted submarine warfare** (the *Lusitania*, 1915; resumed in February 1917) and the **Zimmermann telegram**, in which Germany proposed that Mexico "make war together, make peace together" and reconquer "lost territory in Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona", brought Wilson to Congress on 2 April 1917: "The world must be made safe for democracy. Its peace must be planted upon the tested foundations of political liberty." American money, food and from 1918 two million soldiers tipped the balance; the armistice came on 11 November 1918.

## The interwar economy (7.4)

**A fragile recovery.** The war left Europe in debt to the United States, Germany owing reparations, and the gold standard restored at pre-war rates that overvalued the pound. Germany's hyperinflation of 1923 wiped out savings; American loans (the Dawes Plan, 1924) propped up German payments to Britain and France, which repaid Washington: a circle that depended on New York. Keynes had warned in *The Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919) that the settlement was economically unworkable, recalling the pre-war world in which "the inhabitant of London could order by telephone, sipping his morning tea in bed, the various products of the whole earth".

**Crash and Depression.** The U.S. stock market fell in October 1929; American lending stopped; banks failed; U.S. imports collapsed. The Smoot-Hawley tariff (1930) and retaliation shrank world trade by about two-thirds in value by 1933. Unemployment reached about a quarter of the workforce in the United States and Germany. Countries that left gold early (Britain 1931) recovered sooner; those that clung to it (France) suffered longer. **Export economies** were hit hardest of all: prices of coffee, sugar, rubber, tin and cotton fell by half or more, so Brazil burned coffee, Chile's nitrate towns emptied, and colonial peasants in Java, India and West Africa could not pay taxes or debts. Latin American governments turned toward **import-substitution industrialization**, and the Depression discredited the export model that unit 6 described.

**Three answers.**

| Response | Where | Method | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Liberal reform: the New Deal | United States (Roosevelt, 1933–39) | relief, public works (CCC, WPA, TVA), banking and securities regulation, Social Security (1935), labour rights; FDR's first inaugural: "the only thing we have to fear is fear itself" | recovery partial until war spending; democracy preserved |
| Fascist corporatism and rearmament | Italy (Mussolini from 1922), Germany (Hitler from 1933) | state-directed economy through corporations of employers and workers, strikes banned, public works (autobahns), rearmament, autarky | unemployment fell; economy harnessed to war |
| Communist command economy | Soviet Union (Stalin from 1928) | Five-Year Plans setting targets for steel, coal, machinery; collectivisation of agriculture; forced labour (Gulag) | heavy industry built fast; famine of 1932–33 killed millions in Ukraine and Kazakhstan |

Japan, hit by the collapse of silk exports, turned to military expansion in Manchuria (1931) as its answer; Turkey under Atatürk and Mexico under Cárdenas (oil nationalised in 1938) used the state to build national economies. The common thread: everywhere the state took a larger role, and the liberal faith in free trade and the gold standard died.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the war as European only.** Colonial troops, African campaigns, Japan in the Pacific and the Ottoman fronts are on the exam.
- **Confusing causes of entry with causes of war.** The Zimmermann telegram explains American entry in 1917, not the outbreak in 1914.
- **Making the Depression American only.** Its spread through loans, trade and commodity prices is the point; export economies suffered most.
- **Grouping the Soviet and fascist economies as the same.** Both were state-directed, but fascism preserved private ownership under state control; Stalin abolished it.
