## Gunpowder and the shape of empire

The decisive new fact after 1450 was the cannon. Gunpowder was Chinese; its use in guns spread west along the Mongol routes (Unit 2); by the fifteenth century Ottoman and European foundries cast bronze guns that could breach any medieval wall. The consequences for state-building were uniform across Eurasia. Walled cities and castles stopped protecting local lords, so the power that could afford cannon and the infantry to use them absorbed the rest. Armies of musketeers needed drilling, pay and supply, so rulers needed revenue, bureaucracies and roads. And a ruler who controlled the arsenal could discipline his own nobility. Historians call the Ottomans, Safavids and Mughals the **gunpowder empires**, but the Ming, the Qing and Russia grew the same way.

The ambassador Busbecq, who spent eight years at Suleiman's court, described in letters home (published 1581) what made the Ottoman army formidable: the janissaries' discipline and sobriety in camp, their obedience, the absence of noble birth as a qualification for command, and the sultan's habit of promoting men from the humblest origins to the highest offices on merit. He contrasted this pointedly with the quarrelling, luxurious nobility of Christian armies and warned that Europe would lose if it did not learn. We describe his letters rather than quote them, but the point they make is the point of the topic: the gunpowder army was also a social revolution.

## Six empires in order

**Ottomans (c. 1300–1922).** A frontier Turkic state in Anatolia grew by raiding Byzantium and absorbing other Turkic principalities. **Mehmed II** took **Constantinople in 1453** with cannon cast by a Hungarian engineer; it became Istanbul, the capital. Selim I conquered Syria, Egypt and the Hejaz (1516–1517), making the sultan guardian of Mecca and Medina. **Suleiman** took Belgrade, Rhodes and most of Hungary, besieged Vienna (1529) and fought Portugal in the Indian Ocean and the Habsburgs in the Mediterranean. The engine was the **janissary** corps, recruited by the **devshirme**, and the sipahi cavalry paid by land grants (timars). Expansion slowed after 1570 (Lepanto, 1571) and ended with the failed second siege of Vienna (1683).

**Safavids (1501–1736).** A Sufi order turned militant under **Shah Ismail**, whose **Qizilbash** cavalry took Tabriz in 1501. Ismail declared **Twelver Shia Islam** the religion of Persia, converting a Sunni population by force and making the Safavid–Ottoman frontier a religious as well as political one. Defeat by Ottoman guns at Chaldiran (1514) taught the lesson of gunpowder; **Shah Abbas I** built an army of musketeers and artillery staffed by enslaved Christians from the Caucasus (on the Ottoman model), checked the Qizilbash, recovered lost provinces and made **Isfahan** one of the world's great cities.

**Mughals (1526–1857).** **Babur**, a Timurid prince driven out of Central Asia by the Uzbeks, took Kabul and then, at **Panipat** (1526), defeated the Delhi Sultanate's much larger army with field cannon, matchlocks and Mongol-style cavalry tactics. His memoir, the *Baburnama*, records the battle and his unflattering first impressions of India. **Akbar** (1556–1605) conquered Gujarat, Bengal and Rajasthan, won over the Rajput warrior clans by marriage and office, and built a tax system (week 9). **Aurangzeb** pushed into the Deccan, reaching the empire's greatest extent and exhausting it; after his death (1707) the Marathas, regional governors and then the British dismantled it.

**Ming and Qing (1368–1644; 1644–1912).** The Ming expelled the Mongols, rebuilt the Great Wall and sent Zheng He's fleets (Unit 2) before turning inward. Fiscal crisis, peasant revolt and famine in the 1630s opened the way for the **Manchus**, a people of the northeast who had built a Chinese-style state with a banner army. In **1644** they took Beijing and proclaimed the **Qing**. **Kangxi** (1661–1722) crushed the last Ming loyalists and Taiwan, fought Russia to a treaty (Nerchinsk, 1689) and broke the Dzungar Mongols; his successors annexed Tibet, Mongolia and Xinjiang, doubling the empire's area by 1760. Qing expansion used cannon and musketeers alongside banner cavalry, and was justified in Confucian terms: a new dynasty with the Mandate of Heaven.

**Russia (Muscovy to empire).** Moscow's princes had risen as the Golden Horde's tax collectors (Unit 2); Ivan III stopped paying tribute (1480). **Ivan IV** took the title tsar (1547) and, with artillery, conquered the khanates of **Kazan** (1552) and **Astrakhan** (1556), opening the Volga. Fur traders and **Cossacks** then crossed the Urals and Siberia, reaching the Pacific by 1639; forts, tribute in furs (yasak) and firearms subdued the small peoples of the taiga. Peter the Great (1682–1725) won a Baltic coast from Sweden and built St Petersburg; Catherine II later took the Black Sea coast. Russia was the one empire that expanded across a continent without serious rivals until it met the Qing.

**Songhai (c. 1464–1591).** On the Niger bend, **Sunni Ali** took Timbuktu and Djenné from Mali's successors with a river fleet and cavalry; **Askia Muhammad** (1493–1528) organised provinces, a Muslim court and the pilgrimage, making Songhai the largest state in West African history. It fell to gunpowder it did not have: in **1591** a Moroccan force of musketeers crossed the Sahara and destroyed the Songhai army at **Tondibi**. The CED uses Songhai to show both the reach of Islamic state-building in Africa and the fate of an empire on the wrong side of the gunpowder divide.

## Rivalry between empires

Empires grew against each other. The **Ottoman–Safavid** rivalry was Sunni against Shia as well as Turk against Persian: Chaldiran (1514), the contest for Iraq and the Caucasus, a border that stabilised in 1639 and still roughly divides Turkey from Iran. The **Safavid–Mughal** rivalry was over Kandahar and the Afghan passes. The **Qing–Russian** meeting in the Amur valley produced the first treaty China made with a European power on equal terms (Nerchinsk, 1689). The **Ottoman–Habsburg** struggle in Hungary and the Mediterranean lasted two centuries. Rivalry drove military innovation, consumed revenue and explains why no single empire dominated Eurasia as the Mongols had.

## Primary sources, described

**Busbecq's Turkish letters (1554–1562).** A Flemish scholar sent by the Habsburg emperor to negotiate over Hungary, Busbecq reported the Ottoman camp with mixed alarm and admiration. He described janissaries living on bread, salt and a little meat, forbidden wine, quartered in silence, and the sultan's officers chosen for ability without regard to birth, so that a shepherd's son could rise to the highest rank. He warned that the Ottomans had unity, discipline and a full treasury, while Christendom had quarrels, luxury and empty purses. *Sourcing:* a hostile foreigner, writing to persuade his own side to reform, who therefore flatters the enemy's institutions; reliable on what he saw, polemical in what he concludes.

**Babur's memoirs.** Written in Chaghatai Turkish, the *Baburnama* describes Panipat: Babur's small army, its wagons chained together as a barrier with matchlockmen and cannon behind them, and the flanking charges of his cavalry that broke the Lodi sultan's elephants and infantry. He also records his dislike of India's heat, dust and lack of gardens, and his plans for Persian-style gardens and buildings, which his descendants built. *Use:* evidence for gunpowder tactics and for the Central Asian, Persianate culture the Mughals brought.

## Common mistakes

- **"Gunpowder empires" means the empires invented gunpowder.** China did; the empires adopted it.
- **Confusing the Safavids and the Ottomans.** Safavids: Persia, Shia, Isfahan. Ottomans: Anatolia and the Balkans, Sunni, Istanbul.
- **"The Manchus were Chinese."** They were a northeastern people who conquered China and ruled it as a separate elite with Chinese institutions.
- **"Russia expanded by sea."** Across Siberia it was rivers, forts and furs; the sea came with Peter.
- **Forgetting Songhai** when the question asks about land-based empires; it is the African example.
