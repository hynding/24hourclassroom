# Week 01 · East Asia and Dar al-Islam, c. 1200–1450

**Topics:** 1.1 Developments in East Asia from c. 1200 to c. 1450 · 1.2 Developments in Dar al-Islam from c. 1200 to c. 1450
**Themes:** GOV, CDI, ECN, TEC (Governance; Cultural Developments and Interactions; Economic Systems; Technology and Innovation)
**Reasoning process:** Continuity and change (how Song China and the Islamic world kept older traditions while innovating), with comparison between the two regions
**Built from:** Marco Polo, *The Travels of Marco Polo* (Yule–Cordier edition, 1903); the observations of Ibn Battuta and Ibn Khaldun summarised in our own words
**Spiral:** This is where the course begins. Everything in Unit 2 (the Silk Roads, the Mongols, the Indian Ocean) depends on the two worlds described here: a commercialised China and a politically divided but culturally confident Islamic world.

## What you need to be able to do

- **1.1** Explain the systems of government in Song China (the civil service examination, the scholar-gentry, a bureaucracy built on Confucian learning), how Buddhism, Confucianism and Neo-Confucianism shaped society, and the economic developments (Champa rice, the Grand Canal, iron and steel, paper money, porcelain and textiles for export) that made Song China the most commercialised society in the world. Describe how Korea, Japan and Vietnam borrowed from China and where they kept their own ways.
- **1.2** Explain how the Islamic world changed after the Abbasid Caliphate weakened: new Turkic-led states (the Seljuks, the Mamluk Sultanate of Egypt, the Delhi Sultanate), the transfer of knowledge (preservation of Greek learning, advances in mathematics and medicine, the House of Wisdom's legacy), and how Islam spread by conquest, by merchants and by Sufi missionaries into Africa, South Asia and Southeast Asia.

In plain language: know how each region was governed, what it believed, how its economy worked, and what changed between 1200 and 1450.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Song dynasty (960–1279) | Chinese dynasty known for a merit-based bureaucracy and a commercial boom; lost the north to the Jurchen Jin in 1127 and fell to the Mongols in 1279. |
| Civil service examination | Tests on the Confucian classics that selected officials; in theory open to talent, in practice favouring families who could afford years of study. |
| Scholar-gentry | The landowning, examination-educated class that staffed the bureaucracy and dominated local society. |
| Neo-Confucianism | A Song-era revival of Confucian thought (Zhu Xi) that absorbed Buddhist and Daoist ideas about the cosmos while reasserting social hierarchy and filial piety. |
| Filial piety | The Confucian duty of children to honour and obey parents, the model for all obedience in society. |
| Foot binding | The elite practice of binding girls' feet, which spread under the Song and signalled a narrowing of women's roles. |
| Champa rice | A fast-ripening, drought-resistant rice from Vietnam that allowed two harvests a year and fed a growing population. |
| Grand Canal | The waterway linking the Yangzi and Yellow River regions, moving grain and goods between south and north. |
| Tributary system | China's diplomatic order: neighbouring rulers sent gifts and ritual submission; China returned gifts and trading rights. |
| Abbasid Caliphate (750–1258) | The Baghdad-based caliphate whose political power had largely collapsed by 1200 and which the Mongols destroyed in 1258. |
| Seljuk Turks | Turkic Muslim rulers who took real power in the Abbasid lands in the 1000s while leaving the caliph as a figurehead. |
| Mamluk Sultanate (1250–1517) | Egypt and Syria ruled by former enslaved soldiers who defeated the Mongols at Ain Jalut (1260). |
| Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526) | Turkic Muslim rule over northern India; a Muslim ruling minority over a Hindu majority. |
| Sufism | Mystical Islam; its brotherhoods and travelling teachers carried the faith into Africa and Asia. |
| Ibn Battuta | Moroccan jurist who travelled across Dar al-Islam, India and China (1325–1354) and dictated an account of it. |

## Song China: a Confucian state with a commercial engine

**Governance.** The Song emperors ruled through a bureaucracy chosen by **civil service examinations**. Candidates memorised the Confucian classics and wrote essays; those who passed the highest examinations entered a career of posts across the empire, moved regularly so that they could not build local power bases. The system was old (it reached back to the Han and was expanded under the Tang), but the Song enlarged it enormously, printed the classics cheaply and made it the main road to status. The result was the **scholar-gentry**: a class whose power rested on education and land rather than birth alone. The examinations were, in principle, open to any man; in practice a poor family could rarely spare a son for the years of study required, so the system reproduced elite advantage while still bringing in new talent.

**Belief and society.** Confucian ideas, revived as **Neo-Confucianism** by scholars such as Zhu Xi (1130–1200), set the moral vocabulary: a well-ordered state rested on well-ordered families, and a well-ordered family rested on **filial piety**, the obedience of children to parents, wives to husbands and subjects to rulers. Buddhism, which had come from India along the Silk Roads centuries earlier, remained enormously popular in its Chan (Zen) and Pure Land forms; Neo-Confucians borrowed its questions about the nature of the cosmos even while they criticised monasteries as unproductive. Women's legal and social position narrowed under the Song: **foot binding** spread among elite families, and widow remarriage was discouraged, though women kept dowry rights and managed households and textile work.

**Economy.** Song China was the most commercialised large society in the world. **Champa rice**, introduced from Vietnam, ripened fast enough to allow two crops a year; the population roughly doubled to perhaps 100 million. The **Grand Canal** moved grain north and manufactured goods south. Iron and steel production reached levels Europe would not match until the 1700s; artisans made porcelain and silk for export; the state and private merchants issued **paper money**; coal fired furnaces; and shipbuilders built large ocean-going junks with watertight compartments and the magnetic compass. Historians call this **proto-industrialisation**: rural households producing goods for distant markets, without factories. Marco Polo, who saw the Yuan successor state in the 1270s–1290s, wrote that the Great Khan "makes them take of the bark of a certain tree, in fact of the Mulberry Tree ... and this they make into something resembling sheets of paper" and that "nobody, however important he may think himself, dares to refuse them on pain of death" (*The Travels of Marco Polo*, Book II, ch. 24, Yule–Cordier edition, 1903). Paper currency was a Song invention that the Mongols inherited.

**The neighbours.** Korea (Goryeo), Japan (under the Kamakura shogunate from 1185) and Vietnam (Dai Viet) all borrowed Chinese writing, Buddhism and Confucian political ideas, and all adapted them. Korea ran its own examinations but kept a hereditary aristocracy in charge; Japan's emperor reigned while military lords (the shogun and the samurai) ruled, and the Chinese examination system never took root; Vietnam adopted Confucian bureaucracy yet kept a larger role for women and resisted Chinese political control. Over all of them hung the **tributary system**, in which foreign rulers sent missions and gifts to the Chinese court and received recognition and trade in return.

## Dar al-Islam: a divided house with one culture

**Politics.** By 1200 the **Abbasid Caliphate** in Baghdad was a shadow. Real power lay with regional dynasties, most of them led by Turkic peoples who had entered the Islamic world as enslaved soldiers (mamluks) or as nomadic migrants. The **Seljuk Turks** had ruled Persia and Iraq since the 1050s and kept the caliph as a religious figurehead; in Egypt a corps of enslaved Turkic and Circassian soldiers seized power in 1250 and founded the **Mamluk Sultanate**, which stopped the Mongols at Ain Jalut in 1260 and ruled until 1517; in northern India the **Delhi Sultanate** (from 1206) planted a Muslim ruling class over a Hindu majority. In 1258 the Mongols under Hulagu sacked Baghdad and killed the last Abbasid caliph. The political unity of the early caliphate was gone, but the religious, legal and commercial unity of Dar al-Islam, "the house of Islam", remained: a traveller like **Ibn Battuta** could cross from Morocco to India finding the same sharia courts, the same Arabic, the same networks of scholars and merchants, and he repeatedly found employment as a judge because the law he knew was the law everywhere.

**Knowledge.** Islamic scholars had translated Greek philosophy, mathematics and medicine into Arabic centuries earlier at Baghdad's House of Wisdom, and this period continued that work. **Nasir al-Din al-Tusi** (1201–1274) built an observatory at Maragha under Mongol patronage and produced astronomical models later used in Europe; mathematicians extended algebra and trigonometry; physicians ran hospitals in Cairo and Damascus; **Ibn Khaldun** (1332–1406) wrote a history whose introduction analysed how dynasties rise through group solidarity and decline in luxury, an early social science. Paper-making, learned from China, made books cheap enough for great libraries, and madrasas (religious colleges) funded by charitable endowments trained jurists across the Islamic world. In Cairo and Damascus, Ibn Battuta described hospitals, colleges and charitable foundations paying for everything from pilgrims' travel to the repair of broken crockery.

**Expansion.** Islam spread in three ways. By **conquest**: the Delhi Sultanate's armies, and Turkic migration into Anatolia. By **trade**: Muslim merchants dominated the Indian Ocean and the trans-Saharan routes, and rulers in West Africa (Mali) and the Swahili coast adopted Islam partly to join that commercial and literate world. By **Sufi missionaries**: mystics organised in brotherhoods who travelled, taught, blended Islam with local practice and won converts in places armies never reached, including Southeast Asia. Women's status varied: in the Islamic heartland the Quran gave women property and inheritance rights, but seclusion and veiling grew among elites; among nomadic Turks and in Mali, observers such as Ibn Battuta were startled by women's freedom of movement.

## Comparing the two regions

| | Song China | Dar al-Islam |
|---|---|---|
| Political form | One centralised empire (then Yuan after 1279) | Many states; shared religion and law, no shared ruler |
| Route to elite status | Examination on Confucian classics | Religious scholarship, military service, trade |
| Intellectual focus | Neo-Confucian ethics and statecraft; printing | Preservation and extension of Greek science; law; mathematics; medicine |
| Economic strength | Agriculture (Champa rice), manufacturing, paper money | Long-distance trade, banking instruments, urban crafts |
| How culture spread | Tributary relations and borrowing by neighbours | Conquest, merchants, Sufi missionaries |

## Primary source to read closely

**Marco Polo on the Great Khan's paper money (Yule–Cordier edition, 1903).**

> "All these pieces of paper are issued with as much solemnity and authority as if they were of pure gold or silver; and on every piece a variety of officials, whose duty it is, have to write their names, and to put their seals. ... And nobody, however important he may think himself, dares to refuse them on pain of death. And indeed everybody takes them readily, for wheresoever a person may go throughout the Great Kaan's dominions he shall find these pieces of paper current."

*Historical situation:* Polo, a Venetian merchant, served the Mongol Yuan court in the 1270s–1290s. *Audience and purpose:* European readers who had never seen state paper money; Polo wanted to astonish. *Point of view:* an admiring outsider impressed by scale and authority. *Why it matters:* it shows a Song innovation continuing under Mongol rule, and a state powerful enough to enforce a currency with no metal content.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: continuity and change.** Prompt: *Explain one way Song China continued earlier Chinese traditions and one way it changed them.* Strong answer: "The Song continued the examination system and Confucian ideals of government inherited from the Tang, but expanded them so that examination graduates rather than aristocrats dominated office, and Neo-Confucianism absorbed Buddhist metaphysics while reasserting hierarchy." Name the continuity, name the change, and say what made the change.

**Worked example 2: a comparison claim.** Prompt: *Compare how Song China and the Islamic world maintained cultural unity.* Weak: "Both had strong cultures." Strong: "Song China's unity rested on a single state and an examination system that spread one curriculum, whereas Dar al-Islam, politically fragmented after the Abbasid decline, held together through shared sharia law, Arabic scholarship and merchant and Sufi networks."

## Common mistakes

- **"The Abbasid Caliphate fell in 1258, so Islamic civilisation declined."** Political power had already passed to Turkic states; Islamic scholarship, trade and conversion expanded after 1258.
- **"The examination system was fully meritocratic."** It was open in principle; wealth decided who could prepare.
- **"Japan copied China."** Japan borrowed writing, Buddhism and court ritual but never adopted the examination bureaucracy and developed samurai rule.
- **"Paper money was a Mongol invention."** It was Song; the Yuan continued and over-issued it.
