# Week 32 · Resistance to Globalization, Global Institutions, and Causation

**Topics:** 9.7 Resistance to Globalization · 9.8 Institutions Developing in a Globalized World · 9.9 Causation in Globalization
**Themes:** GOV (Governance), SIO (Social Interactions and Organization), CDI (Cultural Developments and Interactions), ECN (Economic Systems)
**Reasoning processes:** Causation (the unit's capstone: what caused globalization and what it caused); comparison (forms of resistance; institutions that work and institutions that do not)
**Spiral:** The anti-globalization movement inherits the resistance movements of 8.7; terrorism's modern forms began in the Cold War's proxy wars (8.3, 8.8); the UN of 8.1 and the WTO of 9.4 are the institutions under strain; nationalism (5.2) returns as populism; the economic losers of 9.4 are the voters of 9.7; the religious revival of 9.6 supplies both reform and violence.

## What you need to be able to do

- **9.7** Explain the various responses to economic and cultural globalization: anti-globalization protest (Seattle 1999, the World Social Forum, Occupy), economic nationalism and populism (Brexit 2016, tariffs, anti-immigrant parties), religious fundamentalism, and terrorism (al-Qaeda, 11 September 2001, ISIS); and the reasons behind each.
- **9.8** Explain how and why new international institutions and non-governmental organisations developed after 1945 and what they do: the United Nations and its agencies (peacekeeping, the WHO, UNICEF, UNHCR), the IMF, World Bank and WTO, regional bodies (the EU, the African Union, ASEAN), NGOs (Amnesty, Médecins Sans Frontières, the Red Cross, Greenpeace, Oxfam), the Millennium and Sustainable Development Goals, and the limits of these bodies.
- **9.9** Explain the extent to which globalization since 1900 has been a continuation of earlier patterns or a new era: the causation review for the unit and the course.

In plain language: know four kinds of resistance to globalization with an example and a cause each; know what the main global institutions do and where they fail; and be ready to argue whether the world after 1990 is new or a faster version of 1900.

|---|---|
| Anti-globalization movement | Protests against free-trade institutions and corporate power: the Seattle WTO meeting (1999), Genoa (2001), the World Social Forum (2001), Occupy (2011). |
| Populism | Politics claiming to defend the people against elites, often nationalist and anti-immigrant; Brexit (2016) and tariff wars are its economic forms. |
| Fundamentalism | Religious movements demanding a return to scriptural law and rejecting secular modernity, in Christianity, Islam, Judaism and Hinduism. |

| War on terror | The American response: Afghanistan (2001), Iraq (2003), surveillance and detention. |

| Nationalism (returned) | The reassertion of national sovereignty against global institutions and migration after 2008. |

## Resistance to globalization (9.7)

**From the left.** The Seattle protests at the WTO meeting of November 1999 brought together unions, environmentalists, farmers and students against trade rules they saw as serving corporations; Genoa (2001) and the annual World Social Forum in Porto Alegre followed; Occupy Wall Street (2011) protested inequality after the 2008 crisis with the slogan of the 99 per cent. Campaigns against sweatshops, for debt relief (Jubilee 2000) and for fair trade changed corporate codes and some policies. Latin America's "pink tide" (Chávez, Lula, Morales) rejected the Washington Consensus in the 2000s.

**From the right.** The losers of 9.4 and the migrations of the global age produced economic nationalism and populism: Britain's vote to leave the European Union (2016), the American turn to tariffs against China (from 2018), anti-immigrant parties across Europe, India's Hindu nationalism, and strongmen promising sovereignty against global elites. The financial crisis of 2008 and the migration crisis of 2015 were the triggers; deindustrialisation and inequality the causes.

**From religion.** Fundamentalist movements rejected secular modernity: the Iranian Revolution (1979), the Taliban (Afghanistan, 1996), Hindu nationalism, Christian fundamentalism in the United States, ultra-Orthodox politics in Israel. Most were political, some violent.

**Terrorism.** Al-Qaeda grew from the Afghan war against the Soviets; its attacks on 11 September 2001 killed nearly 3,000 people in New York and Washington. President Bush told Congress nine days later: "Either you are with us, or you are with the terrorists." The United States invaded Afghanistan (2001) and Iraq (2003); the Iraq war's chaos bred the Islamic State (ISIS), which held territory in Iraq and Syria in 2014–17 and inspired attacks worldwide. Earlier terrorism (the IRA, ETA, the PLO) had been nationalist and limited; the new terrorism was transnational, religious and aimed at mass casualties, and it used the tools of globalization: air travel, the internet, global finance. States answered with wars, surveillance and new restrictions on movement; the balance between security and liberty became a global argument.

| Resistance | Example | Cause | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anti-globalization left | Seattle 1999; Occupy 2011 | inequality; corporate power; debt | fair-trade and debt-relief gains; agenda for later populism |
| Economic nationalism | Brexit 2016; U.S. tariffs 2018 | deindustrialisation; migration; 2008 | retreat from integration; trade wars |
| Religious fundamentalism | Iran 1979; Taliban 1996 | rejection of secular modernity and foreign influence | theocracies; culture wars |
| Transnational terrorism | 9/11; ISIS | Afghan war legacy; Middle East conflicts; ideology | wars in Afghanistan and Iraq; surveillance states |

## Institutions (9.8)

**The United Nations.** From 51 members in 1945 to 193; the Security Council's veto still decides what it can do. Peacekeeping (first in 1948) kept ceasefires in Cyprus, the Sinai, Namibia (1989–90) and Mozambique, and failed in Rwanda (1994) and Srebrenica (1995) when members would not act. The agencies did the quiet work: the WHO (smallpox, polio, COVID), UNICEF (vaccines, schools), the UNHCR (refugees, over 100 million displaced by the 2020s), the FAO and World Food Programme. The **Millennium Development Goals** (2000) set targets for 2015 and the world met the poverty target early, mostly because of China; the Sustainable Development Goals (2015) added climate and inequality.

**Economic institutions.** The IMF lends with conditions; the World Bank funds development; the WTO sets and adjudicates trade rules; together they are the global economy's government, and the target of 9.7's protests. The G7 and G20 coordinate the largest economies; central banks acted together in 2008.

**Regional bodies.** The **European Union** is the deepest: a single market, a common currency for twenty members, a parliament and court, and since 2016 one departure. The African Union (2002), ASEAN, Mercosur and the Organization of American States are looser. All face the same question: how much sovereignty states will surrender.

**Non-governmental organisations.** The Red Cross (1863) is the oldest; Amnesty International (1961), Médecins Sans Frontières (1971), Oxfam, Greenpeace, Human Rights Watch and thousands more deliver aid, document abuses, lobby governments and shape opinion. They are globalization's conscience and sometimes its substitute for absent states; their accountability is to donors, not voters.

**Limits.** The ICC (2002) lacks the United States, China, Russia and India; climate agreements lack enforcement; peacekeeping needs consent; the Security Council is frozen whenever a permanent member objects. Institutions reflect the power of states; they do not replace it.

## Causation in globalization (9.9)

**Causes of globalization since 1900:** the technologies of 9.1 (container, jet, internet); the political choices of 8.1 and 9.4 (Bretton Woods, the GATT and WTO, neoliberalism, Deng's China); the end of the Cold War; the demand of consumers and the strategies of firms; migration and diaspora. **Consequences:** poverty's fall and inequality's rise (9.4); global culture and its fusions (9.6); disease and environment as planetary problems (9.2, 9.3); the institutions of 9.8 and the resistance of 9.7.

**Continuity or new era?** The world of 1900 was already global: Keynes's Londoner, the telegraph, migration of 50 million Europeans, the gold standard, empires. The world since 1990 differs in scale (everyone, not elites, is connected), speed (instant), direction (culture and capital flowing from Asia as well as to it), and the absence of formal empire. The exam's last question is whether this is the same process at a higher speed or something new; a good answer holds both.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating resistance as one thing.** Seattle, Brexit, the Taliban and al-Qaeda have different causes and aims; compare them.
- **Dismissing institutions because they fail.** Smallpox, Namibia, the ozone layer and the poverty goal are successes; name both.
- **Forgetting that terrorism used globalization's tools.** Air travel, finance and the internet are part of the explanation.
- **Writing 9.9 without 1900.** The comparison with the first globalization is what makes the argument.
