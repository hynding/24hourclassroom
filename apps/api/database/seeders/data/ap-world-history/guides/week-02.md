## South Asia: three religions, many states

**Political map.** The **Delhi Sultanate** (week 1) ruled the north from 1206, a Turkic Muslim elite over Hindu peasants, merchants and princes. Its attempts to push south met the **Vijayanagara Empire**, founded in 1336 by brothers who had served the sultans, briefly converted to Islam, and then returned to Hinduism to build a southern kingdom. In the northwest the **Rajput** warrior clans ruled small Hindu kingdoms. India had no single state; it had a layered political world in which religion, caste and region all mattered.

**Religious change.** Three movements challenged established hierarchies. The **Bhakti movement**, which spread from the south through this period, taught that love for a chosen god (Shiva, Vishnu, Krishna) mattered more than ritual, Brahmin mediation or caste; its poets, including women, sang in Tamil, Kannada, Hindi and other local languages. **Sufism** (week 1) made a parallel offer within Islam: direct experience of God through devotion, music and a revered teacher. Bhakti and Sufi teachers often borrowed from each other, and some, like the fifteenth-century weaver-poet Kabir, mocked both Brahmins and mullahs. **Buddhism** meanwhile had nearly vanished from India, its monasteries destroyed or absorbed, though it flourished in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia. Hinduism remained the majority faith; Islam gained converts through Sufis, through the attractions of a ruling faith, and among groups low in the caste order, but in most of India conversion was slow and partial.

**Why this matters for the exam.** The CED asks how religions "shaped societies": Bhakti and Sufism are the named examples of movements that reached ordinary people and challenged elites, and the Delhi Sultanate and Vijayanagara are the named examples of states built on religious difference.

## Southeast Asia: sea-based and land-based states

Southeast Asia sits between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea, and its states fell into two types.

**Sea-based states** lived by controlling trade routes. **Srivijaya**, a Buddhist kingdom on Sumatra, had taxed shipping through the Strait of Malacca since the 600s and was a centre of Buddhist learning; by the 1300s it had declined under competition. **Majapahit**, a Hindu-Buddhist empire centred on Java from 1293, dominated the spice trade of the islands. Around 1400 the **Sultanate of Malacca** rose on the Malay peninsula; its ruler's conversion to Islam attracted Muslim merchants from Gujarat, Arabia and China's Muslim communities, and Malacca became the great entrepôt where Indian Ocean and Chinese shipping met. Marco Polo, passing Sumatra in the 1290s, noted that "the Island hath great abundance of treasure, with costly spices, lign-aloes and spikenard and many others that never come into our parts" (*The Travels of Marco Polo*, Book III, ch. 9, Yule–Cordier edition, 1903).

**Land-based states** lived by rice. The **Khmer Empire** at Angkor ruled Cambodia from 802; its kings built vast reservoirs and canals to grow rice for a city that may have held several hundred thousand people, and they built **Angkor Wat** (early 1100s) for Vishnu before the court turned to Theravada Buddhism. By 1431 drought, floods and Thai attacks had emptied Angkor. The **Sinhala** kingdoms of Sri Lanka were Theravada Buddhist monarchies with irrigation tanks and a long tradition of monastic learning.

**Pattern.** Indian religions (Hinduism, Buddhism) and Indian ideas of kingship had shaped Southeast Asia for centuries; after 1200 Islam arrived by sea along the same routes, carried by merchants and Sufis, not armies. Sea-based states converted first because their rulers wanted Muslim traders; inland rice states stayed Buddhist.

## The Americas: states without Afro-Eurasian contact

The Americas had no horses, iron, wheels in practical use, or contact with the other hemisphere, and yet produced states as large and organised as any in the world. The exam wants two in detail.

**The Mexica (Aztecs).** Migrants into the Valley of Mexico, the Mexica founded **Tenochtitlan** on an island in Lake Texcoco in 1325 and in 1428 formed the **Triple Alliance** with Texcoco and Tlacopan. Their empire was a **tribute system**: conquered city-states kept their own rulers but sent cotton cloth, maize, cacao, feathers, jade and captives to Tenochtitlan. Painted tribute lists, later copied into the Codex Mendoza for Spanish officials, record the goods each province owed. The city was fed by **chinampas**, raised fields built in the shallow lake, and linked to the shore by causeways and an aqueduct; Spanish soldiers who saw it in 1519 wrote that its market held tens of thousands of people and that the city seemed like something from a book of romance. Society was ranked: nobles, priests, warriors, commoners organised in **calpulli** (ward communities), and enslaved people; **pochteca** merchants traded far beyond the empire. Religion required **human sacrifice**, mostly of war captives, to sustain the sun, which gave warfare a religious purpose and left subject peoples resentful, a weakness the Spanish would exploit.

**The Inca.** From **Cusco** in the Andes, Pachacuti (from about 1438) and his successors conquered a territory stretching some 4,000 kilometres along the mountains. Where the Aztecs collected tribute in goods, the Inca demanded **labour**: the **mit'a** obliged each community (**ayllu**) to send workers in turn to build roads, terraces and storehouses, to farm state and temple lands, to serve in the army and to mine. The state redistributed stored food and cloth in return and in famine. Some 40,000 kilometres of **roads** with bridges and way-stations carried runners and armies; **quipu** knotted cords recorded census and tax data without writing. The Inca ruler was worshipped as a son of the sun; royal mummies kept their palaces and estates, which pushed each new ruler to conquer new lands. Conquered peoples were resettled and taught Quechua; local gods were added to the state cult.

**Other American societies.** The **Maya** city-states of Yucatán (Mayapan and others) continued after the classic collapse, trading salt, cacao and cotton by canoe. In North America, **Cahokia** on the Mississippi (peak c. 1050–1200) built huge earthen mounds and ruled a maize-growing chiefdom; the **Ancestral Puebloans** of **Chaco Canyon** and Mesa Verde built stone great houses and irrigation works in the arid Southwest before drought pushed them to disperse in the 1100s–1200s.

## Comparing Aztec and Inca

| | Mexica (Aztec) | Inca |
|---|---|---|
| Core | Tenochtitlan, Valley of Mexico | Cusco, Andes |
| Form of control | Tribute in goods from subject city-states that kept their own rulers | Direct administration, resettlement, Quechua, roads and storehouses |
| Economic base | Chinampas; markets; pochteca merchants; tribute | Terrace farming; state storehouses; mit'a labour; no markets or money to speak of |
| Record-keeping | Pictographic codices | Quipu |
| Religion and state | Sun god fed by human sacrifice; war as sacred duty | Sun god; ruler divine; royal mummies; local gods absorbed |
| Weakness | Resentful tributaries, ready to ally with outsiders | Civil war over succession, disease arriving before the Spanish |

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: comparison with a reason.** Prompt: *Compare how the Aztec and Inca states extracted resources from conquered peoples.* Strong answer: "The Aztecs took tribute in goods and left subject rulers in place, because their empire was a web of city-states around a market economy; the Inca took labour through the mit'a and administered directly, because the Andes had little market exchange and the state itself stored and redistributed goods." Say *how* they differed and *why*.

**Worked example 2: causation for religious change.** Prompt: *Explain why Islam spread in Southeast Asia after 1200.* Strong answer: "Muslim merchants dominated Indian Ocean trade, so port rulers such as Malacca's adopted Islam to attract them; Sufi teachers made conversion easy by accommodating local custom; and Islam offered connection to a wider world of law and learning. Inland rice kingdoms, less dependent on trade, remained Buddhist." Multiple causes, ranked, with the contrast that proves the point.

## Common mistakes

- **"Islam conquered Southeast Asia."** It arrived by trade and preaching; no Muslim army crossed the Indian Ocean before 1500.
- **"The Inca used money and markets like the Aztecs."** The Inca economy was state-run through labour and storehouses.
- **"Bhakti was a rejection of Hinduism."** It was a reform within Hinduism that reached across caste.
- **"The Americas were simple societies."** Tenochtitlan was one of the largest cities in the world in 1500; the Inca road system was longer than Rome's.
