## What you need to be able to do

- **8.4** Explain the causes and effects of the spread of communism after 1900: the Chinese revolution of 1949, Mao's land reform, the Great Leap Forward and the Cultural Revolution; Cuba (1959); Vietnam; the appeal of communism in agrarian societies and the land redistribution it promised.
- **8.5** Compare the processes by which peoples gained independence after 1945: negotiated independence (India 1947, Ghana 1957, most of French West Africa 1960) and armed struggle (Algeria 1954–62, Vietnam 1946–54, Kenya 1952–60, Angola); the role of nationalist leaders, the Cold War and the United Nations; partition in India and Palestine.

In plain language: know three communist revolutions and what Mao did after 1949; know which colonies negotiated and which fought, with dates and leaders; and know how at least five new states tried to govern and develop, with their successes and failures.

## The spread of communism (8.4)

**China.** The CCP survived the Long March, grew in the war against Japan by organising peasants in the north, and won the civil war of 1946–49 because the Guomindang was corrupt, inflation-ridden and hated. Mao's state redistributed land to peasants (killing perhaps a million landlords), nationalised industry and copied the Soviet Five-Year Plan with Soviet aid. In 1958 the **Great Leap Forward** merged villages into communes and set the peasants to making steel in backyard furnaces; grain output collapsed and the famine of 1959–61 killed somewhere between 20 and 45 million. Mao, pushed aside, struck back in 1966 with the **Cultural Revolution**: students as Red Guards attacked teachers, officials and the "four olds"; schools closed; perhaps a million died; the chaos lasted until Mao's death in 1976. China split with the Soviet Union after 1960 and opened to the United States in 1972 (Nixon's visit). Deng Xiaoping's market reforms after 1978 are week 30.

**Cuba.** Fidel Castro's guerrillas overthrew Batista in January 1959; land reform and nationalisation of American property, the Bay of Pigs invasion (1961) and the embargo pushed Cuba into the Soviet camp; the missile crisis followed. Cuba sent doctors and soldiers abroad, most notably to Angola (1975–91).

**Vietnam.** Ho Chi Minh's Viet Minh fought France from 1946 and won at Dien Bien Phu (1954); the Geneva accords divided the country at the 17th parallel pending elections that never came. Eisenhower explained the stakes to reporters in April 1954 with "what you would call the 'falling domino' principle": "You have a row of dominoes set up, you knock over the first one, and what will happen to the last one is the certainty that it will go over very quickly." The United States backed South Vietnam, sent combat troops from 1965 (over 500,000 by 1968), and withdrew in 1973 under Nixon's doctrine that allies must provide "the manpower for its defense"; the North took Saigon in April 1975. Communism also came to North Korea (1948), Laos and Cambodia (1975), Ethiopia (1974), Angola and Mozambique (1975), Afghanistan (1978).

**Why it spread.** In agrarian societies with landlords and foreign domination, communism promised land, national independence and rapid industrialization; its party organisation gave peasant armies discipline; the Soviet Union (and then China) supplied arms; and the West's colonial record discredited its alternative. Where it won, it delivered land reform, literacy and heavy industry, and also famine, purges and one-party rule.

## Decolonization (8.5)

**Why then.** The war bankrupted Britain and France, destroyed the myth of European superiority in Asia, raised nationalist expectations (the Atlantic Charter) and brought two anti-colonial superpowers to the top. Colonial soldiers came home; the UN gave nationalists a forum; the cost of holding colonies by force rose above their value.

**Negotiated.** India: the Quit India movement (1942), the Royal Indian Navy mutiny (1946), Labour's decision to leave; Jinnah's Muslim League demanded Pakistan; Mountbatten partitioned the subcontinent in August 1947 at the cost of perhaps a million lives and 15 million refugees; Gandhi was assassinated by a Hindu nationalist in January 1948. Ghana: Kwame Nkrumah's Convention People's Party won elections and Britain transferred power in 1957, the first sub-Saharan colony to go; Nigeria (1960), Tanganyika (1961), Uganda (1962) followed. French West and Equatorial Africa: de Gaulle's referendum of 1958 and independence for fourteen states in 1960, with close ties to France kept. Indonesia: four years of fighting and Dutch withdrawal under American pressure (1949).

**Armed.** Algeria: the FLN's war from 1954, with torture, a million French settlers, the fall of the Fourth Republic (1958) and de Gaulle's grant of independence in 1962; about 300,000 to a million dead. Vietnam (above). Kenya: the Mau Mau rising (1952–60) among the Kikuyu, suppressed with detention camps; independence under Jomo Kenyatta in 1963. Angola and Mozambique: wars against Portugal from 1961 until its revolution of 1974. Rhodesia: white settler rule until 1980 (Zimbabwe). South Africa's apartheid lasted until 1994 (week 29).

## Newly independent states (8.6)

**India.** Nehru (1947–64) built a parliamentary democracy with a federal constitution (1950), planned heavy industry, dams ("the temples of modern India"), non-alignment abroad and secularism at home; the Green Revolution from the mid-1960s ended famine. Pakistan, divided into two wings a thousand miles apart, fell under military rule; East Pakistan broke away as Bangladesh in 1971 after a war with Indian intervention.

**Egypt and the Middle East.** Nasser overthrew the monarchy (1952), nationalised the Suez Canal (1956) to pay for the Aswan Dam, survived the Anglo-French-Israeli invasion when Eisenhower and Khrushchev both demanded withdrawal, and became the hero of Arab nationalism; he lost the Six-Day War of 1967. Israel: the UN partition plan (1947), independence (May 1948), war with its neighbours, and about 700,000 Palestinian refugees (the Nakba); wars in 1956, 1967 and 1973. Iran: the CIA-backed coup against Mossadegh (1953) restored the Shah after he nationalised oil.

**Africa.** Ghana's Nkrumah pursued Pan-Africanism and state industry and was overthrown in 1966. The Congo gained independence from Belgium in 1960 with almost no trained administrators; secession, the murder of Lumumba (1961) and Mobutu's dictatorship (1965–97) followed. Tanzania's Nyerere tried ujamaa village socialism (1967), honest but economically failed. Borders drawn at Berlin stayed, holding peoples together or apart; one-party states and military coups became common by the 1970s.

**Common patterns.** State-led development and import substitution; the Cold War as a source of aid and interference; the Green Revolution's gains and its dependence on fertiliser and credit; migration to the former imperial centres (South Asians and West Indians to Britain, Algerians to France, Indonesians to the Netherlands); religious and ethnic conflict within inherited borders.

## Common mistakes

- **Making decolonization a gift.** The empires left because they were pushed and because holding on cost too much; nationalist movements and wars did the pushing.
- **Treating Mao's China as a Soviet copy.** The peasant base, the Great Leap and the Cultural Revolution were Mao's own; the Sino-Soviet split was real.
- **Forgetting Suez.** It marks the end of Britain and France as independent great powers and the Cold War's intrusion into decolonization.
- **Confusing negotiated and armed cases.** Know which was which and why: settlers (Algeria, Kenya, Rhodesia) usually meant war.
