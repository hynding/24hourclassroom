## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mercantilism | The doctrine that a state's wealth is its stock of bullion, so it should export more than it imports, hold colonies as sources and markets, and exclude rivals. |
| Joint-stock company | A company whose capital is raised by selling shares, spreading risk; the VOC and English East India Company. |
| Triangular trade | Manufactured goods from Europe to Africa; enslaved people from Africa to the Americas (the Middle Passage); sugar, tobacco and silver from the Americas to Europe. |
| Middle Passage | The Atlantic crossing of enslaved Africans; mortality of 10–20 per cent. |
| Chattel slavery | Slavery in which the enslaved person is property, hereditary and permanent; the form of the Americas. |
| Encomienda | Spanish grant of the labour and tribute of Indigenous people to a colonist; abolished in law by 1542 but persisting. |
| Hacienda | A large Spanish-American estate worked by Indigenous and mixed-race labourers bound by debt. |
| Mit'a (colonial) | The Inca labour draft redirected by Spain to Potosí's silver mines and the mercury mines of Huancavelica. |
| Indentured servitude | A contract to work for a fixed term (four to seven years) in return for passage; the labour of early Virginia and the Caribbean before slavery. |
| Maroons | Communities of escaped slaves in Jamaica, Suriname, Brazil (Palmares) and elsewhere that fought colonial forces. |
| Pueblo Revolt (1680) | Uprising led by Popé that drove the Spanish from New Mexico for twelve years. |
| Metacom's (King Philip's) War (1675–1676) | Wampanoag-led war against New England settlers; the deadliest conflict in colonial New England. |
| Fronde (1648–1653) | Noble and parlementary revolts against the French crown during Louis XIV's minority. |
| Ana Nzinga | Queen of Ndongo and Matamba (d. 1663) who fought and negotiated with the Portuguese for decades. |
| Sakoku | Tokugawa Japan's restriction of foreign contact: Christianity banned, Japanese forbidden to leave, trade confined to Nagasaki with the Dutch and Chinese. |

## Paying for empire (4.5)

**Silver.** The mines of **Potosí** (1545) and Mexico produced most of the world's silver for two centuries. It left America in three streams: to Spain, where it paid for Habsburg wars and then leaked to the rest of Europe to buy Asian goods; across the Pacific on the **Manila galleons** to China; and around Africa in Portuguese, Dutch and English ships, also to Asia. **China**, taxing in silver, absorbed perhaps half of it (Unit 3). The effects were a **price revolution** in Europe (prices rose several-fold as silver flooded in), the enrichment of merchant cities (Antwerp, Amsterdam, London), and the first truly global trade, in which Chinese silk was paid for in Peruvian metal through a Mexican port.

**Mercantilism and companies.** European states understood trade as a contest for a fixed quantity of bullion. **Mercantilist** policy, as Thomas Mun argued for the English East India Company in the 1620s, aimed to sell more to foreigners than was bought from them, to keep colonies trading only with the mother country, and to build fleets; tariffs, navigation acts and monopolies followed. The **joint-stock company** let hundreds of investors share the risk of a voyage and the profits of a monopoly; the VOC paid dividends for two centuries. The result was a new class of **merchant elites** in Amsterdam, London and the colonial ports, and a state that depended on them.

**Plantations and the slave trade.** Sugar, then tobacco, rice, indigo and coffee, grew on **plantations**: large estates worked by gangs under coercion. Indigenous labour died or fled; indentured Europeans were too few and went free; so planters turned to **enslaved Africans**, whom the Portuguese had been buying on the West African coast since the 1440s. Between 1500 and 1750 some six million Africans were shipped across the Atlantic, rising to twelve million by the trade's end; Brazil and the Caribbean took most. The **triangular trade** carried cloth, guns, iron and rum to Africa, people to the Americas, and sugar and tobacco to Europe. Slavery in the Americas was **chattel**: permanent, hereditary and racial, unlike the household and military slavery of the Islamic world. Equiano, enslaved as a child in the 1750s, recorded the slave ship: "The closeness of the place, and the heat of the climate, added to the number in the ship, which was so crouded that each had scarcely room to turn himself, almost suffocated us. ... The shrieks of the women, and the groans of the dying" (1789). **Effects on Africa:** coastal states (Dahomey, Asante, Kongo's rivals) grew by selling captives for guns; the interior was raided; the export of mostly men skewed sex ratios and encouraged polygyny; population growth stalled in the worst-hit regions; and European goods undercut African crafts.

**Coerced labour systems compared.** The **encomienda** granted Indigenous labour to conquistadors and was curtailed by the crown after Las Casas's protests; the **repartimiento** and the colonial **mit'a** drafted villages for mines; the **hacienda** bound workers by debt; **indentured servants** worked fixed terms in English colonies; **chattel slavery** became the plantation norm. In Russia, **serfdom** tightened at the same time (Unit 3). The CED's point is continuity and change: old forms (mit'a, Islamic and African slavery) were adapted; new forms (plantation chattel slavery, indenture) were created; and the scale was unprecedented.

## Resistance (4.6)

**Against European empires.** The **Pueblo Revolt** of 1680: Popé united the Pueblo peoples of New Mexico against Spanish missions, forced labour and the suppression of their religion; the Spanish were expelled for twelve years and returned on easier terms. **Metacom's War** (1675–1676): the Wampanoag leader Metacom (King Philip) led a coalition against New England's expanding settlements; a tenth of the colonial male population died, and Indigenous power in southern New England was destroyed. **Ana Nzinga**, queen of Ndongo and then Matamba in Angola, fought the Portuguese slave traders and their African allies for forty years, allied with the Dutch, and negotiated a treaty in 1657; she is the CED's example of African state resistance. **Maroon** communities (Palmares in Brazil, lasting most of the seventeenth century; the Jamaican Maroons, who won a treaty in 1739) and revolts on ships and plantations were slavery's constant companion.

**Against the land empires.** The **Marathas**, Hindu warriors of the western Deccan under Shivaji, fought Aurangzeb's Mughal armies from the 1660s and by 1750 ruled much of India (Unit 3). The **Cossack revolts** of Stenka Razin (1670–1671) and later Pugachev (1773–1775) raised the Volga and Don frontiers against the tsar's serfdom and taxes. The **Fronde** (1648–1653) saw French nobles and courts rebel against royal ministers during Louis XIV's childhood; its failure, and his memory of it, produced Versailles (Unit 3). The pattern: expanding states provoked the people they taxed, drafted and converted, and the revolts, even when they failed, shaped how states ruled afterwards.

**Restricting contact.** Some states answered European expansion by limiting it. **Tokugawa Japan** expelled missionaries (1614), crushed the Christian Shimabara rebellion (1637–1638), forbade Japanese to go abroad and confined foreign trade to Dutch and Chinese ships at Nagasaki, a policy later called **sakoku**; edicts of the 1630s prescribed death for returning Japanese and for priests. The Ming and Qing confined Europeans to Macau and Canton. These restrictions were acts of state power, not weakness: Japan and China chose their terms of contact for two centuries.

## Primary source to read closely

**Equiano, *The Interesting Narrative* (1789), chapter 2.** "The first object which saluted my eyes when I arrived on the coast was the sea, and a slave-ship, which was then riding at anchor, and waiting for its cargo." Below decks: "The closeness of the place, and the heat of the climate, added to the number in the ship, which was so crouded that each had scarcely room to turn himself, almost suffocated us. ... The shrieks of the women, and the groans of the dying". He asked other captives "if we were not to be eaten by those white men with horrible looks, red faces, and long hair", and "now wished for the last friend, Death, to relieve me".

*Sourcing:* written in London in 1789 by a freed man active in the abolition campaign; the narrative was meant to move British readers to end the trade, which it helped to do in 1807. Some scholars debate whether Equiano was born in Africa or South Carolina; either way his account of the Middle Passage matches ship records. *Use:* the experience of the trade from the enslaved side, the only such first-hand account on this scale from the eighteenth century.

## Common mistakes

- **"Slavery was new in Africa."** Slavery and the slave trade were old; the Atlantic trade's scale, racial basis and plantation purpose were new.
- **"Mercantilism means free trade."** The opposite: monopoly, tariffs and bullion.
- **"Japan closed itself completely."** It traded with the Dutch, Chinese, Koreans and Ryukyus on its own terms.
- **"Resistance always failed."** The Pueblo expelled Spain for twelve years; the Maroons won treaties; the Marathas replaced the Mughals.
