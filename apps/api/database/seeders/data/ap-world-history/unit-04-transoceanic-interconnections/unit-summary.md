# Unit 4 · Transoceanic Interconnections, 1450–1750 · The night-before sheet

**Exam weight:** about 12–15% of the AP exam · **CED topics:** 4.1 Technological Innovations · 4.2 Exploration: Causes and Events · 4.3 Columbian Exchange · 4.4 Maritime Empires Established · 4.5 Maritime Empires Maintained and Developed · 4.6 Internal and External Challenges to State Power · 4.7 Changing Social Hierarchies · 4.8 Continuity and Change
**Themes:** TEC, ENV, ECN, GOV, SIO, CDI · **Reasoning processes:** Causation and continuity and change above all; comparison for 4.7
**Built from:** Columbus's letter to Luis de Sant Angel (1493; American History Leaflets, 1892); the Treaty of Tordesillas (1494; Davenport, 1917); Bartolomé de las Casas, *A Brief Account of the Destruction of the Indies* (1552; English edition, 1689); Olaudah Equiano, *The Interesting Narrative* (1789); the described journals of da Gama and Bernal Díaz, the Tokugawa edicts, Thomas Mun, the casta paintings, the queue edict and the Ottoman reception of the Sephardim

## The ideas that generate every question

1. **Borrowed technology plus state money opened the oceans.** The compass and astrolabe (Chinese, Muslim), the lateen sail (Indian Ocean), the caravel and later the fluyt, and knowledge of the wind wheels (volta do mar) were assembled by Portuguese, Spanish, Dutch, French and English crowns looking for Asian goods without Ottoman and Venetian middlemen. The Ming had the ships and stopped sailing (1433); Europe had the motive and kept going.
2. **The Columbian Exchange is the largest environmental event in the course.** Smallpox, measles and influenza killed most of the American population within a century; wheat, sugar, horses, cattle and pigs remade American landscapes; maize, potatoes, cassava and manioc fed population growth in Europe, Africa and China; Afro-Eurasian diseases moved west while syphilis probably moved east. Nothing in 4.4–4.7 happens without this.
3. **Two kinds of empire.** Portugal built a trading-post empire in the Indian Ocean (Goa, Malacca, Hormuz, the cartaz) and never replaced Asian merchants; Spain built a territorial empire in the Americas on silver and coerced labour. The Dutch, French and English copied both models with joint-stock companies (VOC 1602, EIC 1600) and plantation colonies.
4. **Silver tied the world together.** Potosí and Zacatecas silver went east to Europe and west on the Manila galleon to China, where the single-whip tax made silver the money of a quarter of humanity. Asia remained the economic centre; what changed was the number of routes by which silver reached it.
5. **Labour was reorganised, not invented.** Encomienda, the colonial mit'a, haciendas and debt peonage, indenture, and then chattel slavery: Africans replaced Indigenous workers as disease and the New Laws (1542) cut the supply. The Atlantic slave trade carried about 12 million people over the whole period; coastal African states grew by selling captives for guns.
6. **Mercantilism explains the policies.** Bullion as wealth, favourable balance of trade, monopolies, navigation acts, chartered companies and captive colonial markets. Mun defended it; Adam Smith (Unit 5) attacked it.
7. **Resistance came from inside and outside.** Pueblo Revolt (1680), Metacom's War (1675–76), Ana Nzinga, Maroons, the Fronde, Cossack and peasant revolts in Russia, Maratha and Sikh resistance to the Mughals, and Tokugawa and Qing limits on European contact.
8. **New elites and a new racial order.** Merchants and financiers in Europe, creoles in Spanish America, merchants under the samurai in Japan; the casta system in the Americas; Manchu bannermen over Han officials; Spain expelling Jews while the Ottomans received them; hidden Christians in Japan.

## Sources you have read

- **Columbus to Sant Angel (1493):** the Taíno "have no iron, nor steel, nor weapons"; are "so unsuspicious and so generous"; he promises gold "and as many slaves as they choose to send for." Use for motives (God, gold, glory), for how the first contact was framed, and as a source to question: a man selling a return voyage to his sponsors.
- **Treaty of Tordesillas (1494):** a line "three hundred and seventy leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands"; lands to the east "pertain forever to the said King of Portugal," to the west to Castile. Use for state rivalry, papal authority, and the absence of any non-European voice.
- **Las Casas, *Brief Account* (1552):** of Hispaniola's "Three Millions of Persons" there remained "scarce Three Hundred"; men "sent to the Mines," women "to Manure and Till the ground." Use for demographic collapse and the encomienda, with the caution that he exaggerated numbers to persuade the crown.
- **Equiano, *Interesting Narrative* (1789):** the ship "so crouded that each had scarcely room to turn himself"; "the shrieks of the women, and the groans of the dying." Use for the Middle Passage and for the limits of a memoir written for an abolitionist audience decades later.
- **Described, not quoted:** da Gama at Calicut (the Zamorin unimpressed by his gifts); Bernal Díaz on Tenochtitlan; the Tokugawa edicts (no Japanese abroad, no Portuguese ships, Christianity banned); Thomas Mun's defence of the East India trade; the casta paintings; the Qing queue edict; Bayezid II and the Sephardim.

## Sentences that earn points

- "The transoceanic voyages were possible because Europeans adopted navigational technology from Muslim and Chinese sources and were motivated by a desire for Asian goods that the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople had made costlier."
- "Disease, not weapons, explains the conquest of the Americas: the demographic collapse removed the labour force, which in turn created the demand for enslaved Africans."
- "Portugal's trading-post empire differed from Spain's territorial empire because the Indian Ocean already had wealthy states and dense trade that Portugal could tax but not replace, whereas the Americas, depopulated by disease, could be settled and mined."
- "Mercantilist states chartered joint-stock companies so that private capital, not the treasury, bore the risk of distant trade, while the crown took its share through monopolies and customs."
- "Although new elites based on commerce rose in Europe, the Americas and Japan, older hierarchies of birth and religion persisted and in the Americas a new hierarchy of ancestry was created."

## Mistakes the exam punishes

- Saying Europeans "invented" the compass, astrolabe or lateen sail. They adopted them.
- Treating the Portuguese as rulers of the Indian Ocean. They taxed a fraction of its trade; Gujaratis, Arabs and Chinese carried most of it.
- Forgetting that most American silver ended in China, and that Manila was a Spanish colony trading with Chinese merchants.
- Writing "slavery began" in this period. Chattel racial slavery on a transatlantic scale was new; coerced labour was not.
- Calling the casta system fixed law. It was a hierarchy of appearance and reputation that could shift with wealth and marriage.
- Mixing up the VOC (Dutch, Indian Ocean, 1602) and the EIC (English, 1600); and the Pueblo Revolt (1680, New Mexico, Popé) with Metacom's War (1675–76, New England).
- Leaving Asia out of a continuity-and-change answer. Tokugawa, Qing, Mughal and Ottoman choices about European trade are half the story of 4.6 and 4.8.

## The unit exam

Forty-two items, about 72 minutes, every topic from 4.1 to 4.8 with review of Units 1 to 3. Four source-based multiple-choice sets (Columbus's letter, Las Casas on Hispaniola, Equiano on the Middle Passage, and a continuity-and-change table), stand-alone multiple choice, select-all, true/false and fill-in-the-blank items, two SAQs (one on the Treaty of Tordesillas, one with no stimulus on the Columbian Exchange and labour) and one LEQ on the extent to which the transoceanic voyages changed the world economy. For the SAQs, label each part and give one specific example with a reason. For the LEQ, take a position on extent, give context before the thesis, and bring in at least one region outside Europe and the Americas.
