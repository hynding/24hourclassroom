# AP World History: Modern · Exam Prep Guide

**Exam:** Thursday 6 May 2027, morning session · **Format:** fully digital in the Bluebook app, two sections, 3 hours 15 minutes · **Built from:** the College Board AP World History: Modern Course and Exam Description and the AP Central pages on the exam and on the 2027 history exam updates (accessed 2026-10-16).

This guide is for weeks 33 and 34. It tells you exactly what the paper looks like, how each part is scored, which skills every question tests, how to spend your time, and how to use the thirty-two weeks of guides, quizzes and unit exams behind you. Read it once now, then keep it open while you sit the practice exam in week 34.

## The format

| Section | Items | Time | Weight | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I, Part A · Multiple choice | 55 questions, four options each | 55 minutes | 40% | Sets of 3 or 4 questions on one source: a primary text, a historian's interpretation, an image, a map, a chart or a table. No penalty for guessing. |
| I, Part B · Short answer (SAQ) | 3 questions, all required | 40 minutes | 20% | Each has three parts, (a), (b) and (c), worth 1 point each. SAQ 1 uses a secondary text, SAQ 2 a primary text, SAQ 3 a non-text source (map, image, chart). They are drawn from different periods of the course. |
| II · Document-based question (DBQ) | 1 question, 7 documents | 60 minutes, including a 15-minute reading period | 25% | Scored out of 7. |
| II · Long essay question (LEQ) | 1 question, required | 40 minutes | 15% | Scored out of 6. |

Because the exam is digital, you type every essay. You can highlight and annotate sources in Bluebook and move between questions within a section, and your answers are submitted automatically when time runs out. Practise typing timed essays this spring, not handwriting them.

### What changed for 2027

According to AP Central (the AP World History: Modern exam page and the AP History Exam Updates page, accessed 2026-10-16), the May 2027 exam changes the free-response questions but not the course:

- **Every SAQ now has a source.** Earlier exams required two SAQs with sources and let you choose between two without. From 2027 you answer three required questions, each built on a source, in a fixed pattern: secondary text, primary text, non-text.
- **The DBQ may draw on the whole course.** The documents still centre on one period, but the prompt may ask you to connect them to developments before or after it, and your outside evidence may come from anywhere in 1200 to the present.
- **The LEQ is a single required prompt.** The old choice among three prompts from different periods is gone. One broad prompt, with a short introductory statement, is set for everyone; it will ask for a reasoning process (comparison, causation, or continuity and change) across a wide span of the course.
- The multiple-choice section is unchanged: 55 questions in sets, all source-based.

Every unit exam in this course already used these shapes, and the practice exam in week 34 follows them exactly.

## The skills, the reasoning processes and the themes

Every question on the exam tests one or more of six **historical thinking skills**. Learn their names because the rubrics are written in them.

| Skill | What it asks you to do | Where it shows up |
|---|---|---|
| 1 · Developments and processes | Identify and explain a historical development or process | Every multiple-choice set; SAQ parts that say 'identify' or 'describe' |
| 2 · Sourcing and situation | Explain a source's point of view, purpose, historical situation and audience, and how they affect its meaning | Multiple-choice items on primary sources; the DBQ's sourcing point |
| 3 · Claims and evidence in sources | Identify a source's argument and the evidence it uses; compare arguments | Items on historians' interpretations; SAQ 1 |
| 4 · Contextualization | Place a development in a broader setting | One point on the DBQ and the LEQ; many multiple-choice items |
| 5 · Making connections | Use comparison, causation, and continuity and change to link developments | Multiple-choice and SAQ items; the reasoning point on the essays |
| 6 · Argumentation | Make a defensible claim and support it with evidence and reasoning; show complexity | The DBQ and the LEQ |

The three **reasoning processes** are comparison, causation, and continuity and change over time. Every LEQ and most DBQs name one in the prompt ('evaluate the extent to which', 'compare', 'explain the causes'). Say which one you are using and let it structure the essay.

The six **course themes** run through every unit. The multiple-choice section is built so that every theme appears; a DBQ or LEQ is usually written around one or two.

| Theme | Code | The question it asks | Example from this course |
|---|---|---|---|
| Humans and the Environment | ENV | How did people and environments shape each other? | The Columbian exchange; the industrial revolution's coal; climate change (9.3) |
| Cultural Developments and Interactions | CDI | How did ideas, beliefs and arts move and change? | Islam's spread; the Enlightenment; global culture (9.6) |
| Governance | GOV | How did states form, rule, expand and fall? | The Mongols; absolutism; empire (6.2); the Cold War (8.2) |
| Economic Systems | ECN | How did people produce, exchange and consume? | The Silk Roads; the Atlantic system; the global economy (9.4) |
| Social Interactions and Organization | SIO | How were societies arranged by class, gender, race and kin? | Caste; coerced labour; the rights movements (9.5) |
| Technology and Innovation | TEC | How did new tools change the world? | Gunpowder; the steam engine; the internet (9.1) |

## The task verbs

The College Board uses a fixed set of verbs, and each means something exact. Doing more than the verb asks wastes time; doing less loses the point.

- **Identify:** name it. One sentence; no explanation needed.
- **Describe:** give its main features. Two or three sentences that say what it was.
- **Explain:** give the how or the why, with a reason or a mechanism. 'Because' belongs in the sentence.
- **Compare:** state a similarity or a difference and account for it.
- **Evaluate the extent:** take a position on how much, and defend it; 'to a great extent because ... although ...' is the shape.
- **Support, modify or refute:** choose one, and give evidence that does what you chose.

## Multiple-choice technique for source sets

1. Read the source's attribution line first: who, when, where, to whom. Half the questions in a set turn on it.
2. Read the questions before the passage so that you read for what is asked.
3. Treat every item as a skill: 'the author's purpose' is sourcing; 'best explains the context' is contextualization; 'a historian would most likely use this to argue' is claims and evidence; 'most similar to' is comparison.
4. Eliminate the two options that are wrong in date or place; then choose between the remaining two by asking which the source itself supports.
5. Never spend more than a minute on an item. Flag it, guess, and return.
6. In data tables, read the column headings and units before the numbers, and look for the trend the question names, not every trend in the table.

## Short-answer technique: ACE

Every SAQ part is worth one point and takes about four minutes. Write three or four sentences per part, labelled (a), (b), (c). The pattern is **ACE**:

- **A**nswer the verb directly in the first sentence.
- **C**ite a specific piece of evidence: a name, a date, a place, a document, a number.
- **E**xplain how the evidence answers the question, with 'because' or 'which meant that'.

A part that names evidence without explaining it, or explains without naming anything, usually earns zero. Do not write an introduction or a thesis; the SAQ is not an essay.

SAQ 1 will give you a historian's interpretation and ask you to describe its argument, support or challenge it with evidence, and explain a development that relates to it. SAQ 2 will give you a primary source and ask about its content, its sourcing (purpose, audience, point of view) and its context. SAQ 3 will give you a map, image or chart and ask you to read it and connect it to what you know.

## The DBQ rubric, point by point (7 points)

You have 60 minutes: 15 to read and plan, 45 to write. The documents arrive in the order the exam writers chose; group them by what they say about the prompt, not by their order.

| Point | What earns it | What loses it |
|---|---|---|
| A · Thesis (1) | A historically defensible claim that answers the prompt and establishes a line of reasoning, in the introduction or conclusion | Restating the prompt; a claim with no reasoning ('there were many causes') |
| B · Contextualization (1) | Describing a broader setting before, during or after the period, in more than a phrase | A passing mention ('after the industrial revolution') |
| C · Evidence from the documents (2) | 1 point for accurately describing the content of at least three documents; 2 points for using at least four documents to support an argument | Quoting without explaining; summarising documents one after another without an argument |
| D · Evidence beyond the documents (1) | One specific piece of outside evidence, used in an argument, different from what you used for context | Vague references; evidence already in a document |
| E · Sourcing (1) | For at least two documents, explaining how or why the point of view, purpose, situation or audience matters to the argument | Describing the source's background without saying why it matters |
| F · Complexity (1) | A sophisticated understanding sustained through the essay: qualifying the claim, weighing a counter-argument, connecting to another period, theme or region, or using all seven documents purposefully | A single sentence of 'however' at the end |

The thesis and contextualization are the cheapest points; secure them in the first paragraph. Then write two or three body paragraphs, each with a group of documents, each document's content tied to the claim, and sourcing for at least two. Put outside evidence in the paragraph where it fits. Plan the complexity point before you start writing: it cannot be bolted on.

## The LEQ rubric, point by point (6 points)

You have 40 minutes. The prompt will be broad (a span of several units) and will name a reasoning process.

| Point | What earns it | What loses it |
|---|---|---|
| A · Thesis (1) | A defensible claim with a line of reasoning | Restating the prompt |
| B · Contextualization (1) | A broader setting described, not just named | A phrase |
| C · Evidence (2) | 1 point for two specific, relevant examples; 2 points for using them to support an argument | Generalities ('trade increased'); evidence listed without connection to the claim |
| D · Reasoning (1) | Comparison, causation, or continuity and change used to frame or structure the argument | Narration in date order |
| E · Complexity (1) | A sophisticated understanding sustained through the essay | One sentence of qualification |

The LEQ rewards range: evidence from two regions or two periods, and a thesis that says 'to a great extent ... although ...' or 'more because of X than Y'. Since the 2027 prompt spans much of the course, the strongest essays use the unit summaries' 'what to review from earlier units' sections to pull evidence from several units.

## Worked example: thesis and contextualization

*Prompt (LEQ): Evaluate the extent to which the First World War changed the relationship between European states and their colonies in the period 1914 to 1945.*

A weak thesis: 'The First World War changed the relationship between Europe and its colonies in many ways.' It restates the prompt and gives no reasoning.

A strong thesis: 'The First World War changed the relationship between Europe and its colonies to a great extent, because the war's demands for colonial soldiers and labour, Wilson's promise of self-determination and the mandate system raised expectations the empires then refused to meet, turning loyal subjects into nationalists; yet the empires themselves survived and even grew until a second war broke them.' It takes a position on extent, gives causes and admits a limit.

Contextualization for the same prompt: 'By 1914 the European empires of the late nineteenth century ruled most of Africa and much of Asia through a mixture of settlers, indirect rule and export economies, justified by the civilising mission and secured by industrial weapons; colonial elites educated in European schools had begun to form congresses and newspapers but asked for reform within empire, not independence.' Three sentences, before the period, describing rather than naming.

Evidence that would follow: the Fourteen Points and the Covenant's Article 22 (unit 7, week 25), in which Wilson's call for a general association of nations to guarantee the independence of 'great and small states alike' became a mandate system that reassigned Germany's and the Ottomans' colonies to the victors; Amritsar (1919); Gandhi's campaigns; the Wafd in Egypt; the May Fourth movement; the Rif war; the Atlantic Charter (1941). Reasoning: continuity and change. Complexity: the contrast between the dominions (which gained autonomy at Westminster in 1931) and the colonies (which did not), or between expectations and institutions.

## Two-week review plan (weeks 33 and 34)

The plan assumes about an hour a day. Every unit summary is a one-page sheet; every unit exam can be re-sat in the app. The practice exam in week 34 should be sat in one block, timed, before you look at the answers.

| Day | Do |
|---|---|
| Week 33, Monday | Read this guide. Re-read the unit 1 and 2 summaries. Re-sit the unit 1 exam's multiple choice. |
| Tuesday | Units 3 and 4 summaries. Re-sit the unit 3 exam. Write one timed SAQ (choose any from a week quiz, 12 minutes). |
| Wednesday | Units 5 and 6 summaries. Re-sit the unit 5 exam. List the five causes of the industrial revolution and the five rationales for empire from memory. |
| Thursday | Units 7 and 8 summaries. Write one timed LEQ (40 minutes) from a unit exam, then score it against the rubric. |
| Friday | Unit 9 summary. Re-sit the unit 9 exam. Build a one-page timeline of 1200 to the present with twenty dates you choose. |
| Saturday | Rest, or review flashcards for the two units whose exams went worst. |
| Week 34, Monday | Sit the practice exam, Section I (55 multiple choice, 55 minutes; then 3 SAQs, 40 minutes). Do not check answers yet. |
| Tuesday | Sit the practice exam, Section II (DBQ, 60 minutes; LEQ, 40 minutes). Then check Section I and read every rationale for the items you missed. |
| Wednesday | Score your essays against the rubrics in this guide. Rewrite your thesis and contextualization paragraphs for both. |
| Thursday | Re-read the summaries of the units where you lost most marks. Review the task verbs. |
| Friday | Flashcards only, fast: every unit's deck in the app, skipping what you know. |
| The night before the exam | Nothing new. Read the 'ideas that generate every question' section of each summary, go to sleep early. |

## Themes across the course: the connections the LEQ wants

Since the 2027 LEQ spans much of the course, practise making one-sentence links across units. Here are nine; make your own for each theme.

- **Trade networks** (units 2, 4, 6, 9): the Silk Roads, the Indian Ocean and trans-Saharan routes became the Atlantic system, which became the imperial world economy, which became the container-and-internet economy; each stage carried more, faster, and reorganised more lives.
- **Empires** (units 1, 3, 6, 8): the Mongols, the gunpowder empires and the maritime empires ruled by different means; the industrial empires of unit 6 ruled by machine guns and railways; decolonization in unit 8 ended the last of them.
- **Labour** (units 4, 5, 6, 9): coerced labour (slavery, the mita, serfdom) gave way to indentured labour and then wage labour; the global factory of unit 9 moved it again.
- **Revolution** (units 5, 7, 8): the Atlantic revolutions' ideas of rights and nation; the communist revolutions' claim to complete them; the anti-colonial revolutions' use of both.
- **Religion** (units 1, 3, 6, 9): the spread of Islam and Buddhism; the religious legitimacy of early modern rulers; missionaries in empire; the revivals and fundamentalisms of the global age.
- **Disease** (units 2, 4, 9): the Black Death on the Silk Roads, smallpox in the Columbian exchange, influenza in 1918, HIV and COVID-19 by jet.
- **Technology** (units 2, 4, 5, 9): the compass and the lateen sail, the caravel and the cannon, the steam engine and the telegraph, the satellite and the internet; each shrank the world and shifted power to whoever had it first.
- **Environment** (units 4, 6, 9): the Columbian exchange, the coal of industrialisation and the plantations of empire, the carbon of the global age.
- **Resistance** (units 4, 6, 8, 9): maroon communities and the Haitian Revolution; the Sepoy Rebellion and the Boxers; Gandhi and the ANC; Seattle and al-Qaeda. Compare methods, conditions and results.

## The night before and on the day

Bring nothing but what Bluebook asks for and a charged device. Read every attribution line. Write the thesis first and the conclusion last. Label SAQ parts (a), (b), (c). Watch the clock: 55 minutes for 55 items means one minute each; 40 minutes for three SAQs means thirteen each; in the DBQ spend the full fifteen minutes reading and grouping. Leave nothing blank; there is no penalty for a wrong answer and a partial essay can still earn thesis and context points.
