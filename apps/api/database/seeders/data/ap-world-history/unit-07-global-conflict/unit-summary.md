# Unit 7 · Global Conflict, 1900–present · The night-before sheet

**Exam weight:** about 8–10% of the AP exam · **CED topics:** 7.1 Shifting Power After 1900 · 7.2 Causes of World War I · 7.3 Conducting World War I · 7.4 The Economy in the Interwar Period · 7.5 Unresolved Tensions After World War I · 7.6 Causes of World War II · 7.7 Conducting World War II · 7.8 Mass Atrocities After 1900 · 7.9 Causation in Global Conflict
**Themes:** GOV, TEC, ECN, SIO, CDI · **Reasoning processes:** Causation (the unit's capstone), comparison, continuity and change
**Built from:** the Zimmermann telegram and Wilson's war message (1917); Owen, "Dulce et Decorum Est" (1920); Keynes, *The Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919); the Fourteen Points (1918), the Covenant of the League (1919) and the Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928); Gandhi, *Hind Swaraj* (1910); Roosevelt's war message and the Atlantic Charter (1941); Truman's Hiroshima statement (1945); Jackson's Nuremberg opening (1945)

## The ideas that generate every question

1. **The old regimes fell to a mix of internal weakness and external pressure.** Mexico (1910), Qing China (1911), the Ottoman Empire (1908, 1918) and Russia (1905, 1917) each had a regime that had failed to reform, foreign domination or defeat that exposed it, and peasants and workers with grievances. The outcomes differed: an agrarian-nationalist constitution, a warlord republic, a secular nation-state, a one-party communist state.
2. **1914 was a crisis that escaped control; 1939 was a war someone chose.** The First World War came from militarism, alliances, imperial rivalry and nationalism acting through mobilisation timetables after Sarajevo. The Second came from an unsustainable peace, the Depression, fascist and militarist ideology, the failure of collective security and appeasement, with Hitler's invasion of Poland as a deliberate act.
3. **Both wars were total wars, the second more so.** Conscription, state-run economies, propaganda, women in industry, colonial troops and labour, and attacks on civilians through blockade (1914–18) and strategic bombing, genocide and famine (1939–45). The technologies escalated from machine gun and gas to carrier, bomber and atomic bomb; deaths from about 15 million to about 70 million.
4. **The interwar economy broke and the state stepped in everywhere.** Reparations, war debt and a restored gold standard made a fragile recovery that the crash of 1929 destroyed; trade fell by two-thirds and export economies collapsed. The New Deal, fascist corporatism and the Five-Year Plans all expanded the state; they differed in ownership and politics.
5. **The peace enlarged the empires and armed their subjects with an idea.** Mandates under Article 22 gave Britain and France the Arab lands and German Africa, but self-determination, colonial service and the disappointments of 1919 produced the mass nationalism of Gandhi, May Fourth, Atatürk and the Pan-African Congress. The Atlantic Charter repeated the promise in 1941.
6. **Genocide requires a state, an ideology and a war.** The Armenian genocide (1915–17), the Holocaust (1941–45), Cambodia (1975–79), Rwanda (1994) and Bosnia (1992–95) share a government in crisis, a group defined as an internal enemy, prior exclusion and propaganda, and the indifference of outsiders. Nuremberg and the Genocide Convention created law without enforcement.
7. **Causes must be ranked.** 7.9 asks for structural (industrial technology, nationalism, imperialism, imperial collapse), intermediate (Versailles, the Depression, ideology) and immediate (Sarajevo, Poland, Pearl Harbor) causes, with a reason for the ranking.

## The two wars compared

| | 1914–1918 | 1939–1945 |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term causes | militarism, alliances, imperialism, nationalism | Versailles, Depression, fascism and militarism, League failure |
| Trigger | Sarajevo and the July Crisis | invasion of Poland (Europe); Marco Polo Bridge 1937 and Pearl Harbor (Asia) |
| Decisive technology | machine gun, artillery, gas, submarine | tank, carrier, bomber, radar, atomic bomb |
| Civilians | blockade, some bombing, Armenian genocide | strategic bombing, Holocaust, occupation, famine |
| Non-European participation | Indian, African, Vietnamese troops and labour; Japan; Ottoman fronts | 2.5 million Indians; Africans; China's eight-year war; the Pacific |
| End | armistice; Versailles; League | unconditional surrender; occupation; United Nations |
| Deaths | about 10 million soldiers, several million civilians | about 70 million, majority civilians |

## Three answers to the Depression

| | New Deal (United States) | Fascist corporatism (Italy, Germany) | Five-Year Plans (Soviet Union) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ownership | private, regulated | private, state-directed, strikes banned | state |
| Method | relief, public works, Social Security, banking regulation | public works, rearmament, autarky | planned heavy industry, collectivisation, forced labour |
| Politics | democracy preserved | one-party dictatorship | one-party dictatorship |
| Result | partial recovery until war spending | unemployment cut, economy aimed at war | heavy industry built; famine of 1932–33 |

## Interwar nationalism at a glance

India: Rowlatt Act and Amritsar (1919), Non-Cooperation (1920–22), Salt March (1930), Government of India Act (1935). China: May Fourth (1919), GMD reorganised (1923), CCP (1921), Northern Expedition (1926–28), Shanghai massacre (1927), Long March (1934–35). Turkey: Atatürk's republic (1923), caliphate abolished (1924). Arab lands: Iraq revolt (1920), Syria (1925), Palestine (1936–39), Iraq independent (1932). Africa and the diaspora: ANC (1912), Pan-African Congress (1919), Garvey's UNIA. Korea: March First Movement (1919). Egypt: revolution (1919), nominal independence (1922).

## Sample SAQ sketch

*Using the Covenant's Article 22, (a) describe how it characterises the mandated peoples; (b) explain one way the mandate system continued nineteenth-century imperialism; (c) explain one reaction to the settlement of 1919 in Asia or Africa.* (a) Peoples "not yet able to stand by themselves", whose tutelage is a "sacred trust of civilisation" for "advanced nations". (b) The mandatories were the existing empires, governing by the old methods and the old civilising-mission language; the British and French empires reached their greatest extent. (c) The award of Shandong to Japan produced the May Fourth Movement; or the Egyptian revolution of 1919; or the Iraqi revolt of 1920.

## Sample LEQ sketch

*Evaluate the extent to which the causes of the Second World War were similar to the causes of the First.* Thesis: both wars grew from the same structures of nationalism, militarism and imperial rivalry, but the Second had causes the First lacked: an unsustainable settlement, a world depression, and ideological regimes that chose conquest, so the similarity is real but the differences explain why 1939 was deliberate where 1914 was a crisis out of control. Context: industrialization and the new imperialism. Evidence: the naval race and alliance blocs; Sarajevo and the July Crisis; Article 231; the Nazi vote and the Depression; Manchuria, Ethiopia, Munich, the Nazi-Soviet Pact. Complexity: the thirty-year-crisis view that treats the two as one war; the role of the United States absent in 1919 and present in 1941.

## The unit exam

Forty-five items, about 75 minutes, every topic from 7.1 to 7.9 plus review of units 5 and 6. Four source-based multiple-choice sets (Wilson's Fourteen Points against Article 231 of Versailles, Keynes on the pre-war economy, the Atlantic Charter against the Kellogg-Briand Pact, and a table of war deaths), stand-alone multiple choice, select-all, true/false and fill-in-the-blank items, one SAQ on a described propaganda poster, and one LEQ on causation. For the SAQ, describe what the source shows before explaining it. For the LEQ, rank causes and give a reason for the ranking, contextualize before the thesis, and bring in evidence from both wars.
