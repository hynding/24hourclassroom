## The ideas that generate every question

1. **Technology shrank the world and changed what people could do.** Jet aircraft, containers (1956), satellites (Sputnik 1957, Telstar 1962), the computer, the internet (public from the 1990s) and the mobile phone cut the cost of moving goods, people and information toward zero. The Green Revolution's seeds and fertilisers fed a population that grew from 1.6 billion (1900) to 8 billion (2022). Energy from oil and gas, then nuclear and renewables, powered it all.
2. **Medicine lengthened life, and connection spread disease.** Antibiotics, vaccines (polio 1955, smallpox eradicated 1980), oral rehydration and clean water doubled life expectancy in the poor world; the 1918 influenza, HIV/AIDS (from the 1980s), Ebola and COVID-19 (2020) travelled along the same routes as trade. Diseases of affluence (heart disease, diabetes, Alzheimer's) replaced infection as the leading killers where people lived long enough.
3. **Growth cost the planet.** Deforestation, soil loss, water shortage, species extinction, and above all carbon dioxide from fossil fuels warming the climate. The debate runs from Earth Day (1970) and the Stockholm conference (1972) through Rio (1992), Kyoto (1997) and Paris (2015): who should cut, who should pay, and whether growth and limits can be reconciled. Nixon's 1970 message called clean air and water a national question; by 1992 it was global.
4. **The economy turned to the market after 1979 and East Asia rose.** Thatcher, Reagan, Deng and the IMF's structural adjustment replaced the regulated post-war order; the WTO (1995), the EU single market and NAFTA (1994) lowered barriers; multinationals split production across borders. Japan, the four tigers, then China (reforms 1978, WTO 2001) and India grew fastest; extreme poverty fell from one-third of humanity (1990) to one-tenth (2015); inequality within countries rose; the crises of 1997 and 2008 spread through connected finance.
5. **Rights movements remade societies.** The UDHR (1948) set the standard; decolonization, the American civil rights movement, the end of apartheid (1994), the women's movements (suffrage, work, reproduction, the 1995 Beijing conference), caste and indigenous rights, and gay rights all claimed it. Governments answered with laws, quotas and courts, or with repression; NGOs and microfinance worked around them.
6. **Culture went global in both directions.** Hollywood, English, pop music, football and fast food spread from the West; Bollywood, Nollywood, anime, K-pop, reggae and world cuisine flowed back and sideways. Religion revived (evangelical Christianity, Islamic revival, Hindu nationalism) partly in reaction. Fusion is the rule; pure Americanisation is the exam's favourite trap.
7. **Resistance took four forms.** The anti-globalization left (Seattle 1999, the World Social Forum, Occupy) attacked inequality and corporate power; populist nationalism (Brexit 2016, tariff wars) attacked migration and lost sovereignty; religious fundamentalism (Iran 1979, the Taliban) attacked secular modernity; terrorism (al-Qaeda, 9/11, ISIS) attacked Western power with globalization's own tools. Each is a response to a different loss.
8. **Institutions grew, and hit their limits.** The UN and its agencies (WHO, UNICEF, UNHCR), peacekeeping (Namibia and Mozambique; Rwanda and Srebrenica), the IMF, World Bank and WTO, the EU, the African Union and ASEAN, NGOs from the Red Cross to Greenpeace, the Millennium and Sustainable Development Goals. Their limits: the veto, no forces or funds of their own, weighted voting, withdrawal by nationalist governments, and great powers ignoring rulings.
9. **The causation question.** Technology made globalization possible; policy (Bretton Woods, GATT, the market turn, China's opening, the Cold War's end) chose it; firms seeking cheaper labour drove it. Its effects produced its opponents. Whether it is a continuation of the nineteenth-century world economy at greater speed or a new era is the unit's essay question; a strong answer takes both sides and judges.

## Sample SAQ sketch

*Using Kennedy's Rice University speech (1962), (a) describe the reason Kennedy gives for going to the Moon; (b) explain one Cold War context for the speech; (c) explain one later consequence of the space programme for globalization.* (a) Because it is hard, and the challenge will organise and measure the nation's best energies and skills; also because the United States will not let space be controlled by a hostile power. (b) Sputnik (1957) and Gagarin's flight (1961) had made the Soviet Union appear ahead; the Cuban missile crisis came that autumn; prestige in the Third World was at stake. (c) Satellites built for the race carried television, telephone and later GPS and the internet across oceans; weather and climate science depend on them; the photograph of Earth from space fed the environmental movement.

## Sample LEQ sketch

*Evaluate the extent to which economic globalization after 1980 benefited the world's population.* Thesis: it benefited most of humanity, above all in Asia, where extreme poverty fell from a third to a tenth, while concentrating losses on particular groups and on the environment, so that its benefits were broad but its costs were specific and politically explosive. Context: the post-war regulated order, the stagnation of the 1970s, the market turn. Evidence: China's 800 million lifted from poverty; the tigers; falling child mortality; against them, deindustrialisation in the West, the 1997 and 2008 crises, rising inequality, carbon emissions. Reasoning: causation and comparison of regions. Complexity: the same process produced both gains and the backlash (9.7); or the benefits depended on state policy (China's planning, Korea's industrial policy), not on free markets alone; or the environmental cost is not yet fully paid.

## What to review from earlier units

- **Unit 6:** the nineteenth-century world economy (steamships, telegraphs, mass migration, the gold standard) is the baseline for the continuity-or-change question in 9.9; imperialism's export economies explain the commodity dependence of 9.4; the migration patterns of 6.7 reappear in reverse (to the old metropoles).
- **Unit 7:** the 1930s (7.4) show what the post-1945 institutions were built to prevent and what the tariff wars of 2018–19 revived; the world wars' technologies (radar, jets, computing, nuclear power) seeded 9.1; the Atlantic Charter's self-government and the UDHR share a lineage.
- **Unit 8:** the UN, IMF and World Bank were born in 8.1; the space race and the internet are Cold War children (8.3); decolonization produced the 'developing world' whose growth and debt fill 9.4; the resistance movements of 8.7 became the anti-globalization movement; the Afghan war (8.3) produced al-Qaeda; the Cold War's end (8.8) opened the market to the whole planet.
- **Unit 5:** the nationalism of 5.2 is the populism of 9.7 with a new enemy; the industrial revolution's coal (5.3) began the carbon story of 9.3.

## The unit exam

45 questions, about 75 minutes: four source-based multiple-choice sets (Clinton and Bush on the global order; Kennedy at Rice; the UDHR described; a table of growth, poverty and emissions), stand-alone multiple choice with review from units 6–8, select-all, true/false, fill-in-the-blank, two short-answer questions (one on a described photograph) and one long essay on causation. Bring the four forms of resistance, the causes and effects of globalization, and a judgement on continuity versus change.
