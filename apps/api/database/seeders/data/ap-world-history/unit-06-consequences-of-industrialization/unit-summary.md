# Unit 6 · Consequences of Industrialization, 1750–1900 · The night-before sheet

**Exam weight:** about 12–15% of the AP exam · **CED topics:** 6.1 Rationales for Imperialism · 6.2 State Expansion · 6.3 Indigenous Responses to State Expansion · 6.4 Global Economic Development · 6.5 Economic Imperialism · 6.6 Causes of Migration · 6.7 Effects of Migration · 6.8 Causation in the Imperial Age
**Themes:** GOV, ECN, ENV, SIO, CDI, TEC · **Reasoning processes:** Causation (the unit's capstone), comparison, continuity and change
**Built from:** Kipling, "The White Man's Burden" (1899); Hobson, *Imperialism* (1902); Twain, "To the Person Sitting in Darkness" (1901); the Treaty of Nanking (1842); the Meiji Constitution (1889); the Monroe Doctrine (1823) and the Roosevelt Corollary (1904); the Chinese Exclusion Act (1882)

## The ideas that generate every question

1. **Industrialization caused imperialism, and imperialism fed industrialization.** Factories needed raw materials, markets and outlets for capital; steam, quinine, the Maxim gun and the telegraph made conquest cheap; the colonies then grew cotton, rubber and tea for the factories.
2. **Several rationales worked together.** Economic interest (Hobson's "taproot"), nationalism and prestige after 1871, Social Darwinism and racial hierarchy, the civilizing mission and missions, and strategic security (Suez). Critics (Hobson, Twain) existed at the time.
3. **Expansion took four forms.** Company to crown (India 1858, Java 1799, Nigeria 1900); settler colonies (Australia, New Zealand, Algeria, the U.S. West, Argentina); partition by diplomacy and conquest (Berlin 1884–85, the scramble); and non-European empires (the United States, Russia, Japan).
4. **Peoples responded in three ways.** Armed resistance (1857, Zulu, Samory, Yaa Asantewaa), new states on the periphery (Cherokee, Sokoto, Zulu, Mahdist), and religious movements (Ghost Dance, Xhosa cattle killing, Mahdi, Boxers). Almost all failed against industrial arms; Ethiopia at Adwa (1896) is the exception that proves the rule.
5. **Control without annexation.** China lost sovereignty by treaty (Nanjing 1842, extraterritoriality, spheres of influence, the Boxer Protocol); Latin America by debt and investment (British railways in Argentina, Peru's guano, the Roosevelt Corollary). Economic imperialism is the CED's term.
6. **The export economy remade land and labour.** Monoculture, famine under export policy, deforestation and crop transfers (tea to Assam, rubber to Malaya); and a world division between industrial exporters and raw-material producers.
7. **People moved on an unprecedented scale.** Fifty million Europeans to the Americas; over a million indentured Indians; millions of Chinese; convicts to Australia. Effects: changed gender roles and remittances at home, enclaves and mixed cultures abroad, and nativist exclusion (Chinese Exclusion Act 1882, White Australia 1901).

## Export economies and economic imperialism (6.4, 6.5)

| Region | Export | Buyer or lever |
|---|---|---|
| India | cotton, jute, tea, opium | Britain; famines 1876–78, 1896–1900 with grain still exported; Naoroji's drain theory (1901) |
| Egypt | cotton | debt → British occupation 1882 |
| Java | sugar, coffee (cultivation system) | Netherlands |
| Congo | rubber, ivory | Leopold's forced labour |
| Malaya | tin, rubber (Kew transfer 1876) | Britain |
| West Africa | palm oil, cocoa (African smallholders) | Britain, France, Germany |
| Argentina | beef, wheat (refrigeration 1870s) | British railways, banks: "informal empire" |
| Peru, Chile | guano, nitrates | Peru's default 1876; War of the Pacific 1879–84 |
| Cuba, Central America | sugar, bananas | United States; United Fruit (1899) |

**China:** opium → Lin Zexu 1839 → First Opium War → Treaty of Nanjing 1842 (Hong Kong "in perpetuity", five ports, indemnity, fixed tariff; then extraterritoriality) → Taiping 1850–64 → Second Opium War 1856–60 → spheres of influence after 1895 → Open Door 1899–1900 → Boxers and the Protocol 1901 (450 million taels). Never a colony.

**Latin America:** Monroe Doctrine 1823 (European systems "dangerous to our peace and safety") → British loans and railways → Roosevelt Corollary 1904 ("international police power") → customs receiverships, marine landings, Panama Canal 1903–14.

## Migration (6.6, 6.7)

| Flow | Scale | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Europe → Americas, 1846–1940 | 50–55 million | free; Irish famine 1845–52; Italians 1876–1914 |
| India → Caribbean, Mauritius, Natal, Fiji, Malaya, 1834–1917 | 1.3 million | indentured (after abolition 1833–38; ended 1917) |
| China → Southeast Asia, Americas, Australia | several million | free and coolie trade |
| Britain → Australia, 1788–1868 | 160,000 convicts | coerced |
| Japan → Hawaii, Americas, from 1885 | hundreds of thousands | contract and free |

Causes: population growth, famine and poverty, labour demand of export economies, steamships and railways. Effects: women's roles and remittances in sending regions; enclaves (Chinatowns, Little Italies, Indian East Africa) and mixed cultures; migrant-built railways (Central Pacific, Uganda Railway); nativism and restriction (Chinese Exclusion Act 1882, Canada's head tax 1885, White Australia 1901, Gentlemen's Agreement 1907).

## Chains to have ready

- **Causation:** industrial demand + surplus capital + nationalist rivalry + racial ideology + new technology + weak old empires → expansion → export economies → debt and treaties → migration → enclaves and exclusion → educated elites and diasporas → twentieth-century nationalism.
- **Comparison:** settler colony vs colony of rule (Algeria vs Nigeria); formal colony vs semi-colony vs dependent republic (India vs China vs Argentina); Adwa vs Omdurman; indenture vs slavery; Chinese Exclusion vs White Australia.
- **Continuity and change:** empire itself continues from unit 4; what changes is depth (interiors conquered), speed (Africa in thirty years), the technology gap, and the scale of migration.

## Sample SAQ and LEQ sketches

**SAQ (source-based).** Given Kipling's first stanza: (a) identify the ideology (civilizing mission with racial hierarchy); (b) explain the historical situation (the U.S. debate over the Philippines, 1899); (c) explain one contemporary challenge (Hobson's economic critique or Twain's satire). One specific sentence per part.

**LEQ.** *Evaluate the extent to which economic motives drove imperial expansion, 1750–1900.* Thesis: economic motives were the underlying driver but worked through nationalism, ideology and technology, which explain timing and form. Context: industrialization and the earlier maritime empires. Evidence: Egypt 1882, the Congo, Java, Berlin, Kipling, Omdurman, the Philippines, Japan in Korea. Complexity: colonies that lost money (German Africa) were held for prestige; the United States, Russia and Japan expanded for strategic as well as economic reasons.

## Sourcing reminders

- **Kipling (1899):** a poet urging the United States to keep the Philippines; duty on the surface, race underneath.
- **Hobson (1902):** a liberal critic writing after the Boer War; economic explanation, hostile to financiers.
- **Twain (1901):** an anti-imperialist satirist; mocks the "game" of civilization.
- **Treaty of Nanjing (1842):** dictated aboard a warship; read it as the product of force.
- **Roosevelt (1904):** a president addressing Congress amid European debt collection in the Caribbean; "civilized nation" is the civilizing mission in American dress.
- **Chinese Exclusion Act (1882):** a statute whose preamble states the nativist justification.

## What to review from earlier units

Unit 4's maritime empires and the Atlantic slave trade (the pattern indenture replaced); unit 4's challenges to state power (the Pueblo Revolt as an earlier religious resistance); unit 3's land-based empires (what the Company and the Opium Wars dismantled); unit 5's industrial revolution, its technologies and its financial innovations (the engine of the whole unit) and its nationalism (the rivalry that drove the scramble).

## The unit exam

Forty-five items, about 75 minutes, every topic from 6.1 to 6.8 plus review of units 4 and 5. Four source-based multiple-choice sets (Hobson on the economics of empire, the Meiji Constitution, Twain's satire, and a table of foreign investment and exports), stand-alone multiple choice, select-all, true/false and fill-in-the-blank items, one SAQ on a described photograph of the Uganda Railway, and one LEQ on continuity and change in the relationship between industrial and non-industrial regions. For the SAQ, describe what the source shows before explaining it. For the LEQ, take a position on extent, contextualize before the thesis, and show both continuity and change.
