# Unit 7 Summary · Qualifying Claims and Building Sentences

Three weeks, three writers and one idea: precision. Montaigne showed how a writer limits a claim so that it can be defended; the Declaration of Sentiments showed how the arrangement of clauses carries an argument; Hazlitt showed how punctuation and grammar make an argument clear and give it force. On the exam, these skills appear in reading questions about what a phrase or a sentence “primarily serves to” do, and in a large share of the writing questions on drafts.

## The skills, as this unit used them

## Week 23 · “Of Cannibals” (Michel de Montaigne, 1580, translated by Charles Cotton, 1685)

**Key moves.** Montaigne opens with King Pyrrhus, who expected “barbarians” and found order, and draws his method from it: we are to judge “by the eye of reason, and not from common report.” He qualifies his evidence (a “plain ignorant fellow” is more likely to tell the truth than a traveller who embellishes) and his own knowledge (“I cannot be sure”). His thesis carries its limit inside it: he finds nothing barbarous in the nation “by anything that I can gather,” except that everyone calls barbarous whatever is not his own custom. Then the central concession: he grants “the barbarous horror of so cruel an action” and turns the charge on Europeans who, seeing their neighbours' faults so clearly, are “so blind to our own.”

**What to remember.** A qualifier is strongest when it matches the evidence. Montaigne's final claim keeps a standard (reason) while denying Europe the right to use it as a weapon: barbarous by one measure, but not by comparison with ourselves.

## Week 24 · Declaration of Sentiments (Elizabeth Cady Stanton and the Seneca Falls Convention, 1848)

**Key moves.** The changed truth: “all men and women are created equal.” Then grievance after grievance as parallel independent clauses beginning “He has,” the grammar of an indictment. One grievance opens with a participial phrase (“Having deprived her of this first right of a citizen”) so that the order of the sentence becomes the order of cause and effect. “He has made her, if married, in the eye of the law, civilly dead” delays its verdict with interrupters that also qualify. The final demand is periodic: the reasons accumulate before “we insist.”

**What to remember.** Coordination presents ideas as equals; subordination makes one idea background, cause, condition or concession. The main clause, and especially the end of the sentence, carries the emphasis. Concede in the dependent clause; claim in the main clause.

## Week 25 · “On Familiar Style” (William Hazlitt, Table-Talk, 1822)

**Key moves.** Hazlitt defines plain style by contrast, in two parallel *not... but* clauses balanced on a semicolon. A colon hinges his antithesis between the easy pompous word and the hard exact one. Parentheses make “(I know)” a quick shrug before he turns a charge into a boast. After an elaborate arch analogy he drops into three plain “I hate” clauses that practise the economy they praise. Words, he says, are like money, “not the worse for being common.”

**What to remember.** Punctuation makes promises: a semicolon, a related clause of equal weight; a colon, the thing just promised; dashes, a loud interruption; parentheses, a quiet one. Grammar errors matter because they make the reader stumble or misread.

## Essay moves practised

- **Revising an absolute claim.** Ask who, how often and how sure; adjust one limit at a time; stop before the claim dissolves.
- **The qualified thesis.** *Although [the strongest opposing point], [your claim], because [your reason].*
- **Concede, then answer.** State the opposing view fairly, grant what is true, answer with a reason or evidence.
- **Sentence combining.** Decide which idea matters most, name the relationship, choose the structure that states it.
- **Proofreading under time.** Thesis, topic sentences, splices and vague pronouns, in two minutes.

## Common traps

- Dismissing a counterargument without a reason (“they are wrong”).
- Conceding a point and never answering it.
- Stacking hedges until the claim says nothing.
- Putting your own claim in an *although* clause.
- Joining clauses with *however* and a comma.
- Writing *Stanton uses a periodic sentence* without explaining what the delay does.
- Reading Montaigne as defending cannibalism.

## What the unit exam looks like

Forty-five questions in about 90 minutes. Four passage sets: a new passage from “Of Cannibals” (the three Brazilians at Rouen and Montaigne's ironic last sentence); the close of the Declaration of Sentiments and the opening of the Seneca Falls resolutions; a passage you have not studied, from Charles Lamb's essay “Imperfect Sympathies” (1821), in which Lamb mocks his own hedging mind and sets it against a comic, stereotyped portrait of a Scottish mind with “no border-land” between yes and no (the stereotype is Lamb's prejudice, read here only for its picture of certainty); and a revision set on a student's opinion piece. Then an AP-style **argument essay** on Montaigne's wish that everyone “write what he knows, and as much as he knows, but no more,” and thirteen questions across the unit, including two short responses.

To prepare: reread the close readings in the three guides, practise naming what a punctuation mark promises, rewrite three absolute claims of your own into qualified ones, and draft one timed argument paragraph with a concession in a dependent clause.
