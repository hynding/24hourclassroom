# Week 31 · Qualifying a Claim with Madison's Federalist No. 10

**CED skill:** 4.C Qualify a claim using modifiers, counterarguments, or alternative perspectives
**Read:** James Madison (as Publius), *The Federalist* No. 10 (1787), in *The Federalist Papers*, [Project Gutenberg #1404](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1404)
**Spiral:** Last week named the moves (3.C); this week you make them. The quiz also returns to the rhetorical situation and audience (1.A, 1.B), methods of development (5.C), tone (7.A) and transitions (6.B).

## What you need to be able to do

- **4.C** Revise your own claims so that they say exactly what you can defend: narrow their scope, measure their certainty, state their conditions, and adjust them in light of an objection or another perspective.
- Write a **counterargument paragraph** that states an objection fairly, concedes what is true in it, rebuts it with a reason, and ties back to the thesis.
- On revision questions, choose the version of a sentence that qualifies a claim without reversing it or hedging it into nothing.

In plain language: by Friday you should be able to take a sentence like *Phones ruin every student's focus* and turn it into one a skeptical reader cannot easily knock down, and you should be able to write the paragraph in which you face that reader's best objection.

## What this week is for

Last week you watched Mill give the other side its strongest case and answer it in a sentence. That is the most dramatic form of qualification, but it is not the most common. Most qualification happens inside the claim itself, in a word or a clause. A writer who says *most* instead of *all*, *likely* instead of *certainly*, or *for students who work after school* instead of *for students* has already answered an objection before anyone raises it.

The CED names three ways to qualify: **modifiers**, **counterarguments** and **alternative perspectives**. This week practises all three, with a model from 1787 who was writing for readers inclined to disagree with him.

## The three tools

**1. Modifiers of scope and certainty.** Scope modifiers answer *how many*: *some, many, most, few, in rural districts, among first-year teachers.* Certainty modifiers answer *how sure*: *probably, likely, may, appears to, in all likelihood.* Use them where your evidence stops. If you have one study of one school, you cannot claim *all schools*; you can claim *schools like this one*.

**2. Conditions.** A conditional clause names the circumstances under which a claim holds: *if the program is funded, when teachers are trained, unless enrolment falls.* Conditions let a claim stay firm inside its limits. *Later start times improve attendance* is easy to attack; *later start times improve attendance when bus schedules are changed to match* is harder.

**3. Counterarguments and alternative perspectives.** A counterargument opposes your claim; you answer it, and the answer often changes the claim slightly. An alternative perspective does not oppose so much as see the issue from somewhere else: a parent's view, a budget officer's, a view from ten years on. Taking one into account often leads to a refined claim rather than a rebuttal.

| Original claim | Problem | Qualified claim |
|---|---|---|
| *Phones ruin every student's focus.* | absolute scope | *For many students, a phone on the desk makes sustained focus harder.* |
| *A four-day week will save the district money.* | unstated condition | *A four-day week would save the district money if busing and building costs fall as projected.* |
| *Everyone agrees the library needs longer hours.* | false consensus | *Students who work after school have the strongest case for longer library hours.* |

## The text in context

The proposed Constitution was signed in September 1787 and sent to the states for ratification. In New York the debate was fierce. Writers who signed themselves with Roman names attacked the plan; one of the most powerful arguments against it was that a republic could survive only in a small territory where citizens knew one another and shared interests. A large republic, they warned, would be governed by distant men who would ignore local concerns.

Alexander Hamilton, James Madison and John Jay answered in eighty-five essays signed **Publius**, most of them first printed in New York newspapers. No. 10, by Madison, appeared on November 22, 1787. Its **exigence** was the small-republic argument. Its **audience** was New York voters, many of whom admired their own state constitution and feared a national government. Its **purpose** was to turn the danger they feared most, faction, into an argument for the Union.

That audience explains the essay's style. Madison cannot overstate. Every reader of No. 10 knew of disorders in the states, and many blamed them on the state governments they also admired. A writer who denied the disorders would lose them; a writer who blamed the state constitutions would insult them. Madison qualifies his way between the two.

## Close reading

### 1. Praise, then a limit

> The valuable improvements made by the American constitutions on the popular models, both ancient and modern, cannot certainly be too much admired; but it would be an unwarrantable partiality, to contend that they have as effectually obviated the danger on this side, as was wished and expected.

**Observe.** The sentence turns on a semicolon and *but*. The first half praises in the strongest terms available; the second half calls further praise an “unwarrantable partiality.”

**Interpret.** Madison flatters his readers' constitutions and then refuses to overstate their success. The limit is precise: they have not removed the danger of faction as well as people hoped.

**Function.** Readers who love their state constitutions hear them praised first. Only then are they asked to admit a shortcoming, and the shortcoming is framed as a matter of degree. The structure is a model for any writer who must criticise something the audience values.

### 2. A measured concession

> However anxiously we may wish that these complaints had no foundation, the evidence, of known facts will not permit us to deny that they are in some degree true.

**Observe.** The sentence begins with what *we* wish, then submits to “the evidence.” The final qualifier, *in some degree*, controls how much is granted.

**Interpret.** Madison concedes that the complaints of “our most considerate and virtuous citizens” are partly justified. He writes as one of the citizens (*we*), not as an outsider pointing out their failures.

**Function.** The concession matches the readers' own experience, so it builds trust. The qualifier keeps it from becoming a confession that the state governments have failed. This is 4.C at the level of a single phrase.

### 3. Sorting the evidence

> It will be found, indeed, on a candid review of our situation, that some of the distresses under which we labor have been erroneously charged on the operation of our governments; but it will be found, at the same time, that other causes will not alone account for many of our heaviest misfortunes

**Observe.** Two parallel clauses, each beginning *it will be found*. The first grants; the second insists. The phrase “a candid review” names the attitude.

**Interpret.** Madison divides the complaints into those he will give away (some distresses are wrongly blamed on government) and those he will keep (the worst cannot be explained any other way). The next sentence assigns those worst misfortunes “chiefly, if not wholly” to faction.

**Function.** Sorting lets a writer concede the weak part of a body of evidence so that the strong part carries more weight. And “chiefly, if not wholly” is a certainty qualifier that claims as much as Madison can support without claiming everything.

### 4. Conceding that a remedy would work

> It could never be more truly said than of the first remedy, that it was worse than the disease. Liberty is to faction what air is to fire, an aliment without which it instantly expires.

**Observe.** A proverb, then an analogy. Madison does not deny that removing liberty would end faction; he says it would do so *instantly*.

**Interpret.** He concedes the remedy's effectiveness and rejects it on cost. The analogy carries both halves: air feeds fire, but no one would abolish air.

**Function.** Rebutting on cost rather than on effectiveness is often the stronger move, because it grants the opponent's facts and contests only the conclusion. Later in the essay Madison does the same with his own preference for a large republic: “It must be confessed that in this, as in most other cases, there is a mean,” he writes, before showing how the Constitution strikes it.

## Writing lesson: the counterargument paragraph

Here is a weak counterargument paragraph from an essay arguing that the school should start at 8:45 instead of 7:30.

> *Weak:* Some people think a later start is a bad idea. But they are wrong. Students need sleep. A later start is the right choice.

The objection has no reason attached, nothing is conceded, and the rebuttal repeats the thesis. A reader who holds the objection learns nothing.

> *Strong:* The most serious objection to a later start comes from families who depend on the current schedule: parents who leave for work at seven and students who hold jobs or care for siblings in the afternoon. Their concern is real, and a plan that ignores it will fail. But the schedule can absorb it. The district's own survey found that a supervised early room, open from 7:15, would cover nearly every family that needs one, and an 8:45 start still ends the day before most after-school shifts begin. A later start, in other words, need not mean a less workable day, so long as the district keeps its doors open early for the students who need them.

Read the strong version for its five parts:

1. **Name the objection and give it a reason** (*families who depend on the current schedule*).
2. **Make it specific** (*parents who leave for work at seven*).
3. **Concede** (*Their concern is real*).
4. **Turn and rebut** (*But the schedule can absorb it*, followed by evidence).
5. **Qualify the thesis in light of the objection** (*so long as the district keeps its doors open early*).

The last sentence is the 4.C move. The writer's position has not changed, but the claim now carries a condition that answers the objection permanently.

Notice the survey in the strong paragraph is invented for this lesson; in your own essays, the evidence for a rebuttal should come from the sources or from your own knowledge, honestly described.

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving absolutes in place.** *Always*, *never*, *every* and *everyone* invite a single counterexample.
- **Over-hedging.** *It might possibly be somewhat helpful* is not a qualified claim; it is no claim.
- **Reversing the position.** On revision questions, a choice that switches sides is never the qualified version.
- **Conceding without answering.** A paragraph that ends on the opponent's point gives them the last word.
- **Answering with insults.** Calling opponents selfish or ignorant concedes nothing and persuades no one who disagrees.

## Exam connection

Many of Section I's writing questions involve qualification in some form: *Which choice most effectively qualifies the claim in sentence 2?*, *The writer wants to acknowledge a perspective that differs from the one presented in the paragraph*, *Should the writer keep or delete sentence 7?* The right answer usually narrows a claim to match the evidence in the draft. On Q3, the argument essay, a qualified thesis (often beginning *Although* or *While*) and one fair counterargument paragraph are among the most dependable routes to the sophistication point. On Q1, synthesis, you can qualify your claim by using a source that disagrees with you.
