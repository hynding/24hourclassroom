## What you need to be able to do

- **4.B** Write a thesis that **requires proof or defense**: a claim a reasonable reader could dispute and that you can support with evidence.
- **4.B** Where it helps, make the thesis **preview the structure** of your argument, by naming its reasons in order or by signalling a pattern such as concession and response.
- Recognise and repair the common faults of weak theses: the topic, the fact, the restated prompt, the device list, the announcement, the vague evaluation.

In plain language: by Friday you should be able to turn *Voting is important* into a sentence that someone could argue with and that tells your reader what your three body paragraphs will do.

## The skill: a thesis that requires defense

On every AP essay, the first rubric row awards one point for a thesis. The scoring guides use the word **defensible**, and it carries two requirements at once.

1. **The thesis must be arguable.** If no reasonable reader could disagree, there is nothing to defend. *Anthony was arrested for voting* is a fact. *Anthony's arrest exposed a contradiction in the Fourteenth Amendment's promise of citizenship* is a claim.
2. **The thesis must be supportable.** A wild claim is arguable, but no evidence will carry it. *Anthony single-handedly won women the vote* is arguable and false.

A good test is to imagine a skeptical reader who says *prove it*. If your sentence leaves that reader nothing to ask for, it is too safe. If no evidence could satisfy that reader, it is too reckless.

The second half of the skill is optional but powerful: the thesis **may preview the structure** of the argument. There are two common forms:

- **The list preview.** *Our city should let sixteen-year-olds vote in city elections, because they already live under city rules, because early voters become lifelong voters, and because the charge of immaturity applies to many adults too.* Three reasons, three body paragraphs, in that order.
- **The pattern preview.** *Although compulsory voting would raise turnout, it would not produce real consent.* The *although* clause promises a concession; the main clause promises the response. *Not only ... but* promises two claims in sequence.

Under time pressure, a list preview is safe and clear. In a more sophisticated essay, a pattern preview can do the same work more gracefully.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Defensible thesis | A claim that is arguable and that the writer can support with evidence. |
| Arguable | A reasonable reader could disagree. |
| List preview | A thesis that names the reasons in the order the essay will develop them. |
| Pattern preview | A thesis whose structure signals the essay's (for example, concession then response). |
| Restated prompt | A thesis that repeats the question's words without making a claim; earns no point. |
| Device list | A thesis that names labels (ethos, pathos, logos) instead of choices and effects. |
| Qualified thesis | A thesis that takes a position with limits (*only when*, *in most cases*). |
| Correlative construction | Paired words such as *not only ... but also* or *either ... or*, which can preview a two-part structure. |

## The text in context

On November 5, 1872, Susan B. Anthony and more than a dozen other women voted in Rochester, New York. Anthony had spent more than twenty years campaigning for women's rights. She and her allies argued that the Fourteenth Amendment, ratified in 1868, had already made women voters: it declared that all persons born or naturalised in the United States are citizens, and that no state may abridge their privileges or immunities. If voting is one of those privileges, women already had it.

Two weeks later she was arrested and then indicted under a federal statute of 1870 for “knowingly voting without having a lawful right to vote.” While waiting for trial she did something remarkable. She gave the same long speech, according to the heading in the 1874 printing, in twenty-nine post-office districts of Monroe County. When the prosecutor moved the trial to neighbouring Ontario County, she spoke in twenty-one districts there too.

Think about that audience. Every man in those halls was a possible juror. Anthony was not speaking to fellow activists; she was speaking to the people who might decide her case. That explains the form of the speech: it is a legal argument laid out for ordinary citizens, built on documents they revered.

The trial took place at Canandaigua on June 17 and 18, 1873. Judge Ward Hunt directed the jury to find her guilty without deliberating and fined her one hundred dollars and costs. Anthony answered, “May it please your honor, I shall never pay a dollar of your unjust penalty,” and she never did.

## Close reading

### 1. The thesis as a promise

> *Friends and Fellow-citizens*: I stand before you to-night, under indictment for the alleged crime of having voted at the last Presidential election, without having a lawful right to vote. It shall be my work this evening to prove to you that in thus voting, I not only committed no crime, but, instead, simply exercised my *citizen's right*, guaranteed to me and all United States citizens by the National Constitution, beyond the power of any State to deny.

**Observe.** The first sentence states the charge in the prosecution's own terms (“the alleged crime,” “without having a lawful right”). The second announces her task: “to prove.” The claim has two parts joined by “not only ... but, instead.” It ends by naming the authority: “the National Constitution,” and a limit on the states.

**Interpret.** Anthony's thesis is arguable in the strongest sense: the United States government disputed it in court. It is also supportable, because she has a body of constitutional text and precedent ready.

**Function (4.B).** This is a model thesis. It requires defense; it names the ground of the defense; and its grammar previews the order of the argument, from denial (no crime) to assertion (a right). Notice too what she does with the word *alleged*: before she has offered any evidence, the charge is already in doubt.

### 2. The premise that makes the thesis work

> We assert the province of government to be to secure the people in the enjoyment of their unalienable rights. We throw to the winds the old dogma that governments can give rights.

**Observe.** The pronoun is *we*. The verbs are bold: “assert,” “throw to the winds.”

**Interpret.** If government only secures rights that already exist, then it cannot create or withhold the vote; it can only protect it.

**Function.** This paragraph supplies the premise that the thesis depends on. And the *we* places Anthony among her listeners, citizens of a “democratic-republican government” who all profess the same principle. A potential juror who agrees with this paragraph has already taken a step toward acquittal. That is skill 1.B at work inside a 4.B thesis: the claim is built on what the audience already believes.

### 3. Evidence chosen for the audience

> Nor can you find a word in any of the grand documents left us by the fathers that assumes for government the power to create or to confer rights.

**Observe.** “The grand documents left us by the fathers” is reverent language. The paragraph goes on to list the Declaration, the Constitution, the state constitutions and the territorial laws, and ends, “Not one of them pretends to bestow rights.”

**Function.** The evidence is the audience's own heritage. A juror could not reject it without rejecting the founders.

### 4. Commentary that turns a quotation into proof

> It was we, the people, not we, the white male citizens, nor yet we, the male citizens; but we, the whole people, who formed this Union.

**Observe.** Anthony repeats the Preamble's phrase three times, ruling out two narrow readings before giving her own.

**Function.** A quotation alone proves nothing; it is her commentary that makes “we, the people” include women. The sentence is a lesson for your own paragraphs: evidence needs interpretation to support a thesis. Later in the speech she reduces the whole case to one question, “Are women persons?”, and a syllogism follows: persons born here are citizens; women are persons; therefore women are citizens whose privileges no state may abridge.

## From reading to writing: repairing weak theses

Here are six faults you will see in your own drafts, each with a repair. All the examples were written for this course.

| Weak thesis | Fault | Stronger thesis |
|---|---|---|
| *Voting is important in a democracy.* | Too broad; no one disagrees | *Our city should let sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds vote in city elections.* |
| *In this essay I will discuss compulsory voting.* | Announcement of topic | *Voting should not be compulsory, because a coerced vote expresses no real consent.* |
| *Anthony uses ethos, pathos and logos.* | Device list | *Anthony turns the founding documents her listeners revere into proof that she committed no crime.* |
| *Anthony makes rhetorical choices to argue that she is innocent.* | Restated prompt | *By reading “we, the people” to include women, Anthony makes her listeners' own Constitution testify for her.* |
| *Anthony's speech is very effective.* | Vague evaluation | *Anthony's speech is most persuasive when it stops asking for rights and insists that women already hold them.* |
| *Anthony was arrested in 1872.* | Fact | *Anthony's prosecution gave her a platform that a petition never could.* |

Now watch a weak introduction become a strong one.

> *Voting is one of the most important rights in a democracy. Today, many people have different opinions about whether teenagers should vote. In this essay I will discuss the question of lowering the voting age in our city.*

Nothing here can be disputed, and the last sentence promises only a discussion. The revision keeps the context but ends with a position and a map:

> *In 1971 the Twenty-Sixth Amendment lowered the national voting age to eighteen, and some cities have since gone further for local elections. Ours should follow. Because sixteen-year-olds already work and drive under city rules, because people who vote early tend to keep voting, and because the charge of immaturity applies to many adult voters as well, our city should let sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds vote in city elections.*

## Common mistakes

- **Writing a fact and calling it a thesis.** If it would appear in an encyclopedia without dispute, it is context.
- **Hiding behind the prompt.** Changing the order of the prompt's words is not a claim.
- **Listing labels.** Ethos, pathos and logos describe every argument ever written. Name the choices.
- **Previewing what the body does not do.** A preview is a promise. If your thesis names three reasons, write three paragraphs in that order.
- **Splitting the thesis.** Put your claim in one or two sentences in one place. Readers (and scorers) should not have to assemble it.
- **Overclaiming.** *Anthony changed history forever* is arguable but impossible to defend in a timed essay. Choose a claim your evidence can carry.

## The exam connection

In Section I, writing questions ask which version of a sentence “best states the main claim,” “most clearly establishes the writer's position” or “best previews the argument that follows.” Choose the version that is arguable and that matches the draft's body paragraphs, not the most dramatic version.

In Section II, the thesis point is the first point on each essay. For the **rhetorical analysis** essay, your thesis names the writer's choices and what they accomplish for the audience and purpose. For the **argument** essay, it takes a position on the question, ideally with the reasoning. For the **synthesis** essay, it takes a position on the issue, not on the sources. In all three, a thesis that previews your line of reasoning makes the four evidence-and-commentary points easier to earn, because it tells you, under pressure, what each paragraph is for.
