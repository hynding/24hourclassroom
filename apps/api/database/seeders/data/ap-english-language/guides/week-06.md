## The skill: the overarching thesis

An argument contains many claims. Each paragraph usually has one, and each is supported by evidence. The **overarching thesis** is the claim that all the other claims serve. It is the answer to the question *what is this whole text trying to prove?*

Three features define a thesis:

1. **It is a claim, not a topic.** *The future of the South* is a topic. *The South will prosper only if its two races cooperate economically* is a claim.
2. **It is arguable.** A reasonable person could disagree with it. Facts are not theses.
3. **It covers the whole text.** A sentence that fits one section but not another is a supporting claim, not the thesis.

Theses come in three forms, and you need to recognise all of them:

- **Explicit.** Stated outright, in a sentence or two, often near the start or the end.
- **Figurative.** Delivered as an image, a parable or an analogy that the argument then explains and applies.
- **Implicit.** Never stated in one sentence, but clear from the whole. To describe an implicit thesis, write the sentence the writer did not, then test it against every section.

The second half of skill 3.B asks about **structure**. A thesis often tells you how the argument will proceed. It may list reasons in order, name a problem and then a solution, or, as in Washington's case, imply a pattern by being addressed to different groups in turn. When a thesis gives that kind of signal, the body of the argument is keeping a promise.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Overarching thesis | The main claim the whole argument defends. |
| Supporting claim | A claim that supports one step of the argument, often a paragraph's topic sentence. |
| Topic | The subject of a text, not a claim about it. |
| Explicit thesis | A thesis stated outright. |
| Implicit thesis | A thesis the reader must infer from the whole text. |
| Figurative thesis | A thesis carried by an image, parable or analogy. |
| Preview | A thesis's indication of the parts and order of the argument. |
| Delayed thesis | A thesis held back until the audience has been prepared for it. |
| Parable | A short illustrative story told to teach a lesson. |
| Injunction | A command or instruction, here the repeated “Cast down your bucket where you are.” |

## The text in context

Booker T. Washington was born into slavery in Virginia, educated at Hampton Institute, and from 1881 led Tuskegee Institute in Alabama, a school that trained Black students in agriculture, trades and teaching. In 1895 the organisers of the Cotton States and International Exposition in Atlanta, a fair meant to show the economic progress of the New South to investors at home and abroad, invited him to speak at the opening ceremonies. A Black speaker sharing the platform at such an event in the Deep South was unprecedented.

The moment was dangerous. Thirty years after emancipation, Southern states were stripping Black men of the vote, lynching was common, and segregation was hardening into law; the Supreme Court would approve it in *Plessy v. Ferguson* the next year. Washington's audience was divided: white Southern officials and businessmen, Black Southerners, Northern visitors and philanthropists, and reporters who would telegraph his words across the country. In *Up from Slavery* he recalls being told that some listeners came “for the purpose of hearing me make a fool of myself,” and he writes of his method that “No two audiences are exactly alike.”

The speech was a sensation. It made Washington the most powerful Black leader in the country for two decades. It also drew sharp criticism, most famously from W. E. B. Du Bois in 1903, who called it the “Atlanta Compromise”: social separation and silence on political rights traded for economic opportunity. That label is itself a reading of the speech's thesis, which is why it is worth deciding for yourself what the thesis is.

## Close reading

### 1. The opening: a fact that frames the claim

> One-third of the population of the South is of the Negro race. No enterprise seeking the material, civil, or moral welfare of this section can disregard this element of our population and reach the highest success.

**Observe.** The address opens with a statistic and a consequence. The second sentence is negative: no enterprise can succeed if it *disregards* Black Southerners.

**Interpret.** The fact is not the thesis; no one would dispute it. But it frames the problem in terms the white businessmen in the room cared about: “enterprise” and “success.”

**Function.** The opening tells the audience what kind of argument is coming: an economic one, addressed to self-interest. It sets the ground on which the thesis will stand.

### 2. The parable: the thesis as a command

> A ship lost at sea for many days suddenly sighted a friendly vessel. From the mast of the unfortunate vessel was seen a signal, "Water, water; we die of thirst!" The answer from the friendly vessel at once came back, "Cast down your bucket where you are."

**Observe.** The command is repeated four times in the story. When the captain obeys, the bucket comes up full of fresh water from the mouth of the Amazon. The sailors were dying of thirst on top of what they needed.

**Interpret.** The lesson is that help sought far away may be close at hand. Washington then applies it: to those who hope to better their condition “in a foreign land,” he says, “Cast down your bucket where you are.”

**Function (3.B).** This is a **figurative thesis**. The single command is the main claim of the speech: seek progress where you already are, through the relationships and work at hand. Because it is a story with a refrain, the audience can carry it out of the hall. Everything that follows is an application of it.

### 3. The applications: the thesis tells you the structure

> To those of the white race who look to the incoming of those of foreign birth and strange tongue and habits of the prosperity of the South, were I permitted I would repeat what I say to my own race: "Cast down your bucket where you are."

**Observe.** Earlier, Washington addressed “those of my race.” Now he addresses “those of the white race,” and he says he will “repeat” what he told his own people.

**Interpret.** To Black listeners the command means: stay in the South, and build on agriculture, the trades and commerce. To white listeners it means: employ and educate the Black workers you know, rather than recruiting immigrants.

**Function (3.B).** The repeated command reveals the plan of the speech. One injunction, two audiences, two parallel sections. A listener who heard “To those of my race” could predict that a word to the other race would follow, and it does. This is what the skill means by a thesis that gives an *indication of the argument's structure*.

### 4. The image that sums it up

> In all things that are purely social we can be as separate as the fingers, yet one as the hand in all things essential to mutual progress.

**Observe.** The sentence turns on a contrast: separate as the fingers, one as the hand. The first half concerns the “purely social”; the second concerns “mutual progress.”

**Interpret.** Washington accepts social separation and asks for economic cooperation. The image makes the bargain physical and memorable.

**Function (3.B).** At the turning point of the speech, Washington restates his thesis in its most compressed form. When critics later quoted one sentence to stand for the whole address, this was the one, because it states the central position more exactly than any other. The conclusion widens the claim again, reaching past material benefits to justice and obedience to law, and closes on “a new heaven and a new earth.”

## From reading to writing: finding the thesis in a draft

On the exam, revision questions often give you a student's draft and ask which sentence is the thesis, or which revision would make it preview the body. Practise on this invented introduction:

> *(1) Last spring, a survey of our senior class found that fewer than a third could explain how interest on a credit card works. (2) Many students will sign a lease or open a credit card within two years of graduating. (3) Personal finance is a subject that some schools teach and others do not. (4) Our school should require a semester of personal finance because students need it.*

Sentence 1 is evidence. Sentence 2 is context. Sentence 3 is a topic stated as a fact. Only sentence 4 is arguable, so it is the thesis. But it is weak: *because students need it* is vague, and it previews nothing. Suppose the body will (a) describe debts recent graduates have taken on without understanding them, (b) show how the course could replace one elective, and (c) answer parents worried about academic time. A stronger thesis names those parts in order:

> *Because graduates are already taking on debts they do not understand, our school should require a semester of personal finance, which could replace one elective without displacing academic courses.*

Now a reader can predict the essay. That is the reading skill (3.B) turned into a writing habit, and next week's skill (4.B) is devoted to it.

## Common mistakes

- **Calling the topic the thesis.** *The speech is about race relations in the South* names a subject. Say what the speech claims.
- **Choosing a supporting claim.** *There is as much dignity in tilling a field as in writing a poem* supports one section; it does not cover the appeal to white employers.
- **Thinking an unstated thesis means no thesis.** Washington never says *I will argue*. His thesis is still clear.
- **Quoting the parable as if it explained itself.** If you identify *Cast down your bucket where you are* as the thesis, translate it: what does it command, and to whom?
- **Ignoring structure.** Skill 3.B has two halves. After naming the thesis, say what it signals about the organisation.
- **Turning evaluation into description.** You may think Washington conceded too much. That is an argument about the speech; first describe its thesis accurately.

## The exam connection

In Section I, reading questions ask for “the main claim of the passage,” “the primary purpose” or “which sentence best states the writer's position,” and some ask how a paragraph “relates to the passage's overall argument.” The right answer is the one that covers the whole passage and is phrased as something the writer is trying to prove. Writing questions ask which sentence best states the thesis of a draft, or which revision would preview its body.

In Section II, the rhetorical analysis essay depends on describing the writer's thesis and purpose accurately before analysing choices. A misread thesis sends the whole essay in the wrong direction. And your own essays earn the thesis point only with a claim that is defensible and goes beyond restating the prompt.
