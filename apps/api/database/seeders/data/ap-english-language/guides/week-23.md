# Week 23 · Qualifying Claims in Montaigne's “Of Cannibals”

**CED skills:** 3.C Explain ways claims are qualified through modifiers, counterarguments, and alternative perspectives · 4.C Qualify a claim using modifiers, counterarguments, or alternative perspectives
**Read:** Michel de Montaigne, “Of Cannibals” (1580), in Charles Cotton's translation (1685), [Project Gutenberg #3600](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3600)
**Spiral:** Claims and evidence (3.A, 4.A) and thesis statements (3.B, 4.B) from Units 1, 2 and 6; line of reasoning (5.A) from Unit 3; transitions (6.B) from Unit 5. This week asks a new question of every claim you have learned to find: *how far does it reach, and how does the writer know?*

## What this week is for

Unit 7 is about precision: precise claims this week, precise sentences in weeks 24 and 25. You already know how to find a claim and the evidence behind it. Now you will look at the *limits* writers set on their claims, and you will learn to set limits on your own.

By the end of the week you should be able to:

- **3.C** Point to a modifier, a counterargument or an alternative perspective in a text and explain what it does for the argument: narrows the claim, protects it from an objection, or wins the reader's trust.
- **4.C** Revise an absolute claim into one that your evidence can support, and write a thesis that includes its own limits.

In plain terms: a claim that says *always* must be true every time. A claim that says *usually, in our town, under these conditions* only has to be true in the cases it names. The second claim is smaller, and that is exactly why it is stronger.

## The skill explained: three ways to qualify

The CED names three tools.

**1. Modifiers.** Words and phrases that limit a claim. It helps to sort them by what they limit:

| Kind | Examples | What it limits |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | all, most, many, some; in rural districts; for first-year students | who or what the claim covers |
| Frequency | always, usually, often, sometimes, rarely | how often the claim is true |
| Certainty | certainly, probably, appears to, may, suggests | how sure the writer is |
| Condition | if, unless, provided that, only when | the terms under which the claim holds |

**2. Counterarguments.** An opposing claim that a fair-minded reader might raise. The writer states it and answers it, by **conceding** part of it (granting what is true), by **rebutting** it (showing that it matters less than it seems or does not lead where the opponent thinks), or by **refuting** it (showing that it is false). Unit 9 will study rebuttal and refutation in depth; this week the focus is on how a concession limits the writer's own claim.

**3. Alternative perspectives.** Not every other view is an opposing one. An alternative perspective is another way of seeing the issue (a parent's view of a school schedule, an economist's, a bus driver's) that the writer acknowledges, uses or partly accepts. A writer who brings in alternative perspectives usually ends with a more complex claim than one who only argues against a single opponent.

Qualification has a cost if it is overdone. A sentence that says something *may possibly, in some ways, perhaps* be true claims nothing a reader could disagree with. The goal is not caution for its own sake; it is a claim whose limits match the evidence.

## The text in context

**Writer.** Michel de Montaigne (1533-1592) was a French magistrate who retired to his family estate near Bordeaux in 1571 and began writing short prose pieces he called *essais*: attempts, trials, tests. The first two books appeared in 1580. He invented the form we still call the essay: a mind testing an idea in the first person, admitting what it does not know.

**Exigence and context.** Europeans had been reading travellers' reports of the peoples of Brazil for decades, and the verdict was usually the same: savages. Montaigne kept in his house a man who had lived in Brazil for ten or twelve years, and he had met three Brazilians who were brought to Rouen and presented to the young King Charles IX. At home, France was in the middle of the Wars of Religion (from 1562), in which French Catholics and Protestants tortured and killed one another.

**Audience and purpose.** His readers were educated Europeans, Catholic and Protestant, who took their own superiority for granted. Montaigne's purpose is not to praise cannibalism. It is to make those readers examine the word *barbarous* and notice who is using it.

**The translation.** This course quotes Charles Cotton's English translation (1685-86), in the edition by William Carew Hazlitt, which is in the public domain in the United States. Its spelling and punctuation are seventeenth-century: “'tis,” “betwixt,” “colour.” Quote it exactly as printed.

## Close reading

### 1. The opening anecdote: a claim about how to judge

The essay begins not with Brazil but with King Pyrrhus, who invaded Italy expecting Roman “barbarians” and found an army in perfect order. Montaigne draws the lesson at once: “By which it appears how cautious men ought to be of taking things upon trust from vulgar opinion, and that we are to judge by the eye of reason, and not from common report.”

This is a claim about method, and it governs everything that follows. Notice its contrast: *the eye of reason* against *common report*. The essay will set the common report about Brazil (savages) against what reason can make of the evidence. The anecdote also works as an alternative perspective in miniature: the Greeks called everyone else barbarians, and Montaigne's readers would smile at that before realising that they do the same.

### 2. Qualifying the evidence

Montaigne is careful about where his knowledge comes from. He admits what he cannot know, even whether more new worlds remain to be found: “I cannot be sure, that hereafter there may not be another, so many wiser men than we having been deceived in this.” Then he defends his witness: “This man that I had was a plain ignorant fellow, and therefore the more likely to tell truth.” Educated travellers, he explains, cannot resist improving a story to seem wise; a simple man has neither the skill nor the motive to invent.

Two things are worth seeing here. First, the defence of the witness is an argument about evidence (skill 3.A), and it anticipates the obvious objection: *why should we believe your servant?* Second, Montaigne does not overclaim even for his own source. He adds that the man brought him seamen and merchants from the same voyage, and he ends the passage with a principle that applies to himself as much as anyone: “I would have every one write what he knows, and as much as he knows, but no more.” That sentence is close to a definition of qualification.

### 3. The thesis, with its limit built in

Here is the claim the essay has been preparing: “I find that there is nothing barbarous and savage in this nation, by anything that I can gather, excepting, that every one gives the title of barbarism to everything that is not in use in his own country.”

Read it slowly. “By anything that I can gather” is a modifier of certainty and scope: as far as my evidence reaches. Then comes “excepting,” which looks as if it will grant a fault. But the exception is not about the Brazilians at all. It is about the people who judge them. The qualification turns into the thesis: *barbarous* is the name people give to whatever is not their own custom.

The next sentence mocks that habit with a mock-triumphant series: “there is always the perfect religion, there the perfect government, there the most exact and accomplished usage of all things.” The repeated “there” points at home, wherever home is, and the superlatives (“perfect,” “most exact”) show how absurd it is for every people to believe the same thing about itself.

### 4. The concession, and the turn

Montaigne does not hide the hardest fact: these people kill and eat their prisoners of war. He even corrects one explanation of it, an alternative perspective he rejects: “They do not do this, as some think, for nourishment, as the Scythians anciently did, but as a representation of an extreme revenge.” Then comes the concession: “I am not sorry that we should here take notice of the barbarous horror of so cruel an action, but that, seeing so clearly into their faults, we should be so blind to our own.”

The structure is *I grant X, but Y*. He grants that the practice is a “barbarous horror”; his complaint is about the people pointing at it. He then lists European cruelties (torture, roasting by degrees, setting dogs on the living) and adds, in a parenthesis, that these were “lately seen” among “neighbours and fellow-citizens” and done “under colour of piety and religion.” His readers knew he meant the Wars of Religion.

The essay's final qualified claim gathers all of this: “We may then call these people barbarous, in respect to the rules of reason: but not in respect to ourselves, who in all sorts of barbarity exceed them.” Montaigne keeps a standard (reason) and grants that by that standard the Brazilians fall short. What he refuses is Europe's right to use the word as a comparison. A writer who said simply *they are not barbarous* would be easy to dismiss. A writer who says *they are, by one measure, and we are worse by every measure* is very hard to answer.

## From reading to writing: revising an absolute claim

Here is a draft thesis of the kind AP readers see every year:

> *Social media is destroying the ability of young people to think.*

Ask the three questions. **Who** does it cover? All young people. **How often?** Always, implicitly. **How sure** can the writer be? The verb *destroying* claims a proven, total effect. No evidence a student can bring in forty minutes will support that.

Now revise one limit at a time.

> *Heavy social media use appears to be weakening many teenagers' ability to sustain attention on long texts.*

Scope (many teenagers), certainty (appears), and a narrower, testable effect (sustained attention on long texts) instead of *the ability to think*.

Then build a concession and a condition into the thesis itself:

> *Although social media helps teenagers find communities and information they could not reach before, heavy daily use appears to weaken their ability to sustain attention on long texts, and schools should teach that kind of reading on purpose.*

The *although* clause concedes an alternative perspective; the main clause carries the claim; the last clause turns it into a proposal. This thesis also previews a line of reasoning: what social media gives, what it seems to cost, and what schools can do.

Finally, watch for the opposite failure:

> *It might be possible that social media could perhaps have some effects on some teenagers in certain situations.*

Every word is safe, and the sentence says nothing. Cut the hedges until one precise limit is left.

**A concession pattern worth memorising.** *Although [the strongest point on the other side], [your claim], because [your reason].* The dependent clause concedes and the main clause, where the weight of an English sentence falls, delivers your claim. Week 24 will look closely at why that placement works.

## Common mistakes

- **The dismissive counterargument.** *Some people say homework builds discipline, but they are wrong.* Naming a view only to wave it away is worse than leaving it out, because it shows the writer has no answer.
- **The concession with no rebuttal.** Granting a point and then moving on leaves the reader thinking you lost that round.
- **Hedging everything.** Qualify the one part of the claim your evidence cannot fully support; state the rest plainly.
- **Qualifying in the conclusion only.** A limit that appears for the first time in the last paragraph reads like a retreat. Put it in the thesis.
- **Confusing evidence with a qualifier.** A vivid example supports a claim; it does not limit it. On reading questions, look for the words that set the boundaries.
- **Reading Montaigne as praising cannibalism.** He calls it a “barbarous horror.” His target is the judge, not the defendant.

## Exam connection

**Section I.** Reading questions ask what a phrase “primarily serves to” do; when the phrase is a modifier or a concession, the right answer usually says it limits, anticipates or concedes. Revision questions ask which version of a sentence best fits the writer's evidence, or how to handle an opposing view; the best version matches scope and certainty to the evidence and answers the objection with a reason.

**Section II.** On the **argument essay** (Q3), a qualified thesis is the quickest route to a complex argument. The sophistication point rewards explaining the complexities or tensions within the issue; a thesis that concedes a real point and explains why the claim still holds, and under what conditions, is already doing that. On the **synthesis essay** (Q1), sources that disagree with you are not obstacles. Using one as a counterargument and answering it with another shows the reader you have weighed the sources. On the **rhetorical analysis essay** (Q2), when a writer concedes, explain the choice: *what does granting this point buy the writer with this audience?*
