# Week 29 · Clauses in Balance in Kennedy's Inaugural Address

The last week of Unit 8 moves from words and comparisons to the architecture of the sentence. Skill 7.B asks how writers create, combine and place clauses to show how ideas relate: which is main and which subordinate, which causes or conditions another, which deserves the last word. Skill 8.B asks you to build sentences that make those relationships clear. The model is John F. Kennedy's Inaugural Address of January 20, 1961, a speech of about 1,350 words in which almost every important sentence is a balanced pair of clauses. It is one of the most imitated speeches in American history, largely because its syntax is so memorable.

## The skill explained: clauses and their relationships

A **clause** has a subject and a verb. An **independent clause** can stand alone (*we pledge our support*). A **dependent clause** cannot, because it begins with a word that ties it to another clause: a subordinating conjunction (*because*, *although*, *if*, *when*, *unless*) or a relative pronoun (*who*, *which*, *that*).

Writers relate clauses in two main ways.

**Coordination** joins clauses of equal weight, with a coordinating conjunction (*and*, *but*, *or*, *nor*, *for*, *so*, *yet*) or a semicolon. It says: these ideas matter equally, and here is how they connect (addition, contrast, choice, result).

**Subordination** makes one idea dependent on another. It ranks the ideas and names their logic: *because* for cause, *although* for concession, *if* and *unless* for condition, *when* and *until* for time. Choosing the conjunction is choosing the argument.

**Placement** matters as much as connection. English sentences put their greatest emphasis at the end (**end focus**). A **periodic sentence** delays its main clause or key word until the end, building suspense. A **loose** or **cumulative sentence** states its main clause first and adds detail after. Putting a dependent clause first usually sets up the main idea; putting it last can make it an afterthought, or a sting.

Three patterns of balance appear constantly in speeches:

- **Parallelism**: equal ideas in the same grammatical form.
- **Antithesis**: contrasting ideas in parallel form.
- **Chiasmus**: repeated terms reversed in the second of two parallel clauses (A-B, B-A).

And three faults hide relationships: the **comma splice** (two independent clauses joined only by a comma), **stringy coordination** (many clauses strung on *and*), and **choppiness** (a run of short simple sentences with no stated connections).

## The text in context

Kennedy, at 43, was the youngest person ever elected president, and he had won one of the closest elections in American history. The country was in the Cold War: the United States and the Soviet Union each held nuclear weapons, newly independent nations in Africa and Asia were choosing sides, and Latin America was unsettled. The exigence of the speech was a transfer of power at a dangerous moment. Its purposes were many: to unite a narrowly divided country, to reassure allies, to warn adversaries while offering negotiation, and to summon Americans to sacrifice. Its audiences were just as many, and the speech addresses most of them by name. Because it is a speech of the federal government, it is a U.S. government work and in the public domain.

## Close reading

### 1. The first sentence and the new generation

Kennedy opens by turning a close partisan election into a national ceremony: “we observe today not a victory of party, but a celebration of freedom--symbolizing an end, as well as a beginning--signifying renewal, as well as change.” The *not X but Y* frame is antithesis, and it speaks to the half of the country that voted against him. Then come two paired phrases (*an end, as well as a beginning*; *renewal, as well as change*) that balance continuity against novelty. The first sentence already teaches the method of the whole speech: pairs of ideas held in balance.

A few sentences later comes a long sentence about his generation:

> Let the word go forth from this time and place, to friend and foe alike, that the torch has been passed to a new generation of Americans--born in this century, tempered by war, disciplined by a hard and bitter peace, proud of our ancient heritage--and unwilling to witness or permit the slow undoing of those human rights to which this Nation has always been committed, and to which we are committed today at home and around the world.

The main clause is a command (*Let the word go forth*), and its content is a long *that* clause. Inside it, four parallel modifiers set off by dashes define the generation before the sentence states what it will not permit. The sentence ends with two parallel relative clauses (*to which this Nation has always been committed, and to which we are committed today*) that bind past and present. Clause by clause, the sentence makes Kennedy's generation the heirs of the old commitment.

### 2. Pledges in parallel

> Let every nation know, whether it wishes us well or ill, that we shall pay any price, bear any burden, meet any hardship, support any friend, oppose any foe, in order to assure the survival and the success of liberty.

The interrupting *whether* clause addresses two audiences at once (well-wishers and enemies); the five parallel verb phrases build an unconditional commitment; the purpose phrase arrives last, in the position of emphasis. Then a short sentence lands like a vow: “This much we pledge--and more.” Length followed by brevity is a pattern Kennedy uses again and again.

The pledges to allies use antithesis to make a logical point:

> United, there is little we cannot do in a host of cooperative ventures. Divided, there is little we can do--for we dare not meet a powerful challenge at odds and split asunder.

The sentences match almost word for word. Only *united* and *divided*, *cannot* and *can*, change, so the structure itself proves that unity is the condition of strength. The dash and *for* then add the reason.

The pledge to the world's poor uses placement:

> we pledge our best efforts to help them help themselves, for whatever period is required--not because the Communists may be doing it, not because we seek their votes, but because it is right. If a free society cannot help the many who are poor, it cannot save the few who are rich.

Three parallel *because* clauses: two rejected, one affirmed, with the true reason held for the end. Then a conditional sentence: the dependent *if* clause states the condition, the main clause the consequence, and the paired opposites (*many* and *few*, *poor* and *rich*) turn the logic into an aphorism that ties the fate of the comfortable to the fate of the poor.

### 3. Speaking to the adversary

When Kennedy turns to the Soviet bloc, his clauses carry a double message, firmness and openness:

> We dare not tempt them with weakness. For only when our arms are sufficient beyond doubt can we be certain beyond doubt that they will never be employed.

The *only when* clause comes first and inverts the main clause (*can we be certain*), which throws the emphasis onto the condition. Repeating *beyond doubt* in both clauses makes the paradox (arms ensure they will never be used) sound like a strict cause and effect.

Then the most precise balance in the speech:

> So let us begin anew--remembering on both sides that civility is not a sign of weakness, and sincerity is always subject to proof. Let us never negotiate out of fear. But let us never fear to negotiate.

The last two sentences are a chiasmus: *negotiate* and *fear* trade places. One policy rejects two errors, negotiating from weakness and refusing to talk at all. A run of paragraphs follows, each beginning “Let both sides,” proposing arms control, scientific cooperation, and “a new world of law, where the strong are just and the weak secure and the peace preserved.” That last series compresses its clauses (the verb *are* is understood in the second and third), so it moves quickly to its goal.

### 4. Beginning, and the inverted command

> All this will not be finished in the first 100 days. Nor will it be finished in the first 1,000 days, nor in the life of this Administration, nor even perhaps in our lifetime on this planet. But let us begin.

The second sentence stretches the timeline in a series of *nor* phrases, each longer in time than the last. Then three words. The short sentence turns from the size of the task to the present moment, and *But* marks the turn from what cannot be done to what can.

And finally the line everyone remembers:

> And so, my fellow Americans: ask not what your country can do for you--ask what you can do for your country.

The chiasmus reverses *country* and *you*, and with them the direction of duty. The next sentence repeats the frame for a second audience: “My fellow citizens of the world: ask not what America will do for you, but what together we can do for the freedom of man.” Kennedy ends where the speech began: in balanced clauses, addressed to more than one audience at once.

## From reading to writing: a sentence-combining workshop

Before combining sentences, ask four questions. Which idea is main? What is the relationship (cause, contrast, condition, time, addition)? Which word names it? Which idea should come last?

**Choppy to combined.**

> Choppy: *I joined the debate team. My sister made me. I was terrified at the first meeting. I stayed.*
>
> Combined: *I joined the debate team only because my sister made me, and although I was terrified at the first meeting, I stayed.*

*Because* names the cause; *although* concedes the fear; *I stayed* comes last and gets the emphasis.

**Stringy to ranked.**

> Stringy: *There are more than sixty clubs here, and you should join one, and it does not matter which one, and you will be glad.*
>
> Ranked: *Our school has more than sixty clubs; join one, any one, and you will be glad you did.*

**Comma splice to clear relationship.**

> Splice: *Joining one activity is not a burden, it is the way you will find your people.*
>
> Fixed: *Joining one activity is not a burden but the way you will find your people.*

**Emphasis by placement.** Compare *I stayed, although I was terrified* with *Although I was terrified, I stayed.* The clauses are the same; the second ends on the decision. Ask what you want the reader to remember, and put it last.

**Balance for a memorable close.** Kennedy's pairs are not tricks to sprinkle everywhere. Save a balanced or chiastic sentence for a moment that deserves it, usually a thesis or a conclusion.

> Unbalanced: *You will not remember your locker combination in four years, but you might remember some people, probably.*
>
> Balanced: *In four years you will forget your locker combination; you will never forget the people you met.*

## Common mistakes

- **Naming the sentence type and stopping.** *This is a complex sentence* earns nothing. Say what the structure shows: that one idea is the condition of another, or that the true reason is held for last.
- **Using and for every relationship.** *And* adds; it does not explain. Choose *because*, *although*, *so*, *unless*.
- **Burying the main idea.** If the key point sits in a dependent clause in the middle, readers miss it.
- **Over-balancing.** Every sentence in antithesis sounds like a slogan. Use balance at the moments that carry the argument.
- **Splices and fragments.** On revision questions, eliminate options that create them first.

## Exam connection

Section I asks reading questions such as *The structure of the sentence in lines 12-14 serves to...* or *The contrast between the two clauses emphasises...* Identify the main clause, the dependent clauses and what is placed last. Writing questions ask you to combine or revise sentences: *The writer wants to combine sentences 4 and 5 to emphasise the speaker's decision. Which version best accomplishes this goal?* The right answer names the relationship correctly and puts the stressed idea in the main clause at the end.

In Section II, syntax analysis is strong commentary in the rhetorical analysis essay, provided you link structure to meaning and audience. In your own essays, clear sentences are the foundation of the evidence-and-commentary row: a reader who cannot see how your ideas relate cannot credit your reasoning. A well-placed balanced sentence in a thesis or conclusion can contribute to the sophistication point.
