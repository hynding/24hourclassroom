## The skill: what a line of reasoning is

Every argument has a thesis, a position someone could dispute. But a thesis alone persuades no one. What persuades is the **line of reasoning**: the sequence of claims, each supported by evidence and explained by commentary, that leads a reader from where they are to where the writer wants them to be.

The easiest way to understand a line of reasoning is to compare it with what it is not. Here are two outlines for the same thesis, that a school should start later in the morning.

> *A list:* Teenagers need more sleep. Many students drive to school. Other districts have changed their schedules. Sports could still happen after school.

> *A line:* Teenagers' body clocks shift later in adolescence, so a 7:30 start forces them to work at their least alert hour. That fatigue does not stay in the classroom: tired teenage drivers have more accidents. A later start therefore protects students' safety, not just their grades, and the safety gain outweighs the scheduling costs that districts which have already switched have managed to absorb.

The list has four reasons that could be shuffled in any order. The line has four claims that **depend on each other**: the biology explains the fatigue, the fatigue explains the danger, the danger raises the stakes, and the raised stakes make the costs worth paying. The words that hold the line together are *so*, *that fatigue*, *therefore* and *outweighs*. They are not decoration; they *are* the reasoning.

Skill 5.A asks two things. First, **describe** the line: where does it begin, what are its turns, where does it end? Second, **evaluate** it: does each step follow, is any step missing, and does the last claim match the thesis? Three questions help:

1. Could the paragraphs be shuffled without loss? If so, you have a list.
2. Is there a gap, a link the reader must supply? If so, name the missing claim.
3. Has the argument drifted, ending on a claim different from the thesis it announced?

Skill 6.A is the same knowledge turned into writing. **Commentary** is the tool: the sentences after your evidence that explain what it implies, for whom, and why that moves the argument forward. Commentary that only repeats the evidence (*this shows that many people agree*) adds length without adding reasoning.

There are two broad shapes. A **deductive** line starts from a general principle and applies it to a case. An **inductive** line starts from cases and builds to a general claim. Thoreau's essay is mostly deductive, and that is what makes it such a clean case study.

## The text in context

In July 1846 Henry David Thoreau, then living in his cabin at Walden Pond, walked into Concord, Massachusetts, and was arrested for refusing to pay his poll tax, which he had not paid for six years. He spent one night in the town jail; someone, probably a relative, paid the tax, and he was released the next morning, reluctantly. Two years later he explained himself in a lecture at the Concord Lyceum, and in 1849 he published it as “Resistance to Civil Government” in a collection called *Aesthetic Papers*. The title “Civil Disobedience” was given to it after his death.

**Exigence.** The United States was at war with Mexico (1846-1848), a war Thoreau and many New Englanders saw as a land grab to extend slavery. Slavery itself was legal in half the country and protected by federal law. Thoreau's question was not whether these were wrong (his audience mostly agreed they were) but what an honest person who thought so should *do*.

**Audience.** His first listeners were his Concord neighbours: merchants, farmers and professionals who disapproved of slavery and the war but kept paying taxes, voting and going about their business. That is why the essay's hardest blows land on people who are “in opinion” opposed to slavery but “in effect do nothing.” He is not converting slaveholders; he is shaming the comfortable.

**Purpose.** To move readers from disapproval to refusal: to persuade them that withdrawing their support, even at the cost of prison, is a duty. That conclusion was radical, so the line of reasoning had to be patient.

## Close reading: the line, step by step

### 1. The starting premise: a motto everyone accepts

> I heartily accept the motto,—“That government is best which governs least;” and I should like to see it acted up to more rapidly and systematically. Carried out, it finally amounts to this, which also I believe—“That government is best which governs not at all;” and when men are prepared for it, that will be the kind of government which they will have.

**Observe.** The essay begins with agreement, not attack: “I heartily accept.” The motto was a familiar saying of the day. Thoreau then pushes it one step, from *least* to *not at all*.

**Explain the step.** A deductive line needs a premise the audience already holds. By starting with a motto his listeners might have quoted themselves, Thoreau makes his later conclusions look like consequences of their own beliefs. Then, a few paragraphs later, he pulls back: “I ask for, not at once no government, but *at once* a better government.” That pivot is the essay's first turn. It replaces an ideal nobody could act on with a practical question: *how does a citizen get a better government?*

### 2. The premise that does the work: conscience outranks law

> I think that we should be men first, and subjects afterward. It is not desirable to cultivate a respect for the law, so much as for the right. The only obligation which I have a right to assume, is to do at any time what I think right.

**Observe.** Just before this, Thoreau has argued that majorities rule not because they are right but “because they are physically the strongest.” Now he ranks the person (“men”) above the role (“subjects”).

**Explain the step.** This is the premise from which everything later follows. If majority rule rests on strength rather than justice, it cannot settle moral questions; if it cannot, then each person's conscience must. The vivid evidence comes next: soldiers marching “against their wills, aye, against their common sense and consciences,” turned into “small movable forts and magazines.” The image is not data, but it shows in one picture what happens when respect for law outruns respect for right. Notice how the step depends on the one before it. Remove the claim about majorities and the claim about conscience floats free.

### 3. The test: friction and the machine

> If the injustice is part of the necessary friction of the machine of government, let it go, let it go: perchance it will wear smooth,—certainly the machine will wear out. If the injustice has a spring, or a pulley, or a rope, or a crank, exclusively for itself, then perhaps you may consider whether the remedy will not be worse than the evil; but if it is of such a nature that it requires you to be the agent of injustice to another, then, I say, break the law. Let your life be a counter friction to stop the machine.

**Observe.** The paragraph is a graded series of three cases, each beginning “If.” The first two counsel patience or careful weighing; only the third commands action.

**Explain the step.** A reader who accepts that conscience outranks law will still ask: *must I resist every imperfect law?* The analogy answers. All machines have friction, and a citizen can bear it. But some injustice is built in (“a spring, or a pulley ... exclusively for itself”), and some uses *you* as its instrument. Only the last case requires breaking the law. The analogy makes the line of reasoning concrete: it turns an open question (how much injustice is too much?) into a test. It also keeps Thoreau credible. A writer who admits that small wrongs can be borne is easier to trust when he says that this wrong cannot.

Earlier in the essay he makes the same move with a sharper inversion: “when the friction comes to have its machine, and oppression and robbery are organized, I say, let us not have such a machine any longer.” The reversal (friction *having* a machine) is the hinge of the whole argument.

### 4. The conclusion: where a just man belongs

> Under a government which imprisons any unjustly, the true place for a just man is also a prison.

**Observe.** One sentence, built as a paradox: the place for the just is the place for criminals.

**Explain the step.** This is the claim the essay has been building toward, and it could not have come first. A reader who heard it at the start would have dismissed it. A reader who has accepted that conscience outranks law, that organized injustice must be resisted, and that a law making you its agent should be broken, now faces a simple consequence: if you refuse, the state will jail you, so jail is where honest people end up. Thoreau follows through with his definition of a “peaceable revolution”: if a thousand men refused their taxes, that would be less violent than paying them and enabling the state to “shed innocent blood.”

Before reaching this point, he removes one obstacle. The English philosopher William Paley had argued that obedience rests on expediency, a weighing of costs and benefits. Thoreau answers with a single hypothetical: “If I have unjustly wrested a plank from a drowning man, I must restore it to him though I drown myself.” Some duties hold whatever they cost. Refuting the strongest opposing principle is itself a step in the line of reasoning: it clears the road for the conclusion.

**Does the line support the thesis?** Mostly, yes, which is what 5.A asks you to judge. Each step depends on the last, and the final claim matches the thesis. A careful reader might still press on the first premise: if everyone acts on private conscience, what stops a person whose conscience is wrong? Thoreau answers only partly (“They only can force me who obey a higher law than I”). Spotting where a line of reasoning is strongest and where it is thin is exactly the kind of commentary the exam rewards.

## From reading to writing: building your own line

Here is a student's first draft of an argument paragraph about whether a town should open its library on Sundays.

> *The library should be open on Sundays. Many people work during the week. The library has a lot of good books. A survey said people would come. Students need to study. It would cost money but it would be worth it.*

Every sentence is relevant, and none is connected. The reader gets reasons, not reasoning. Here is the revision:

> *The town council should open the library on Sunday afternoons, because that is when the people it now fails to reach are free. Sunday is the one day many working families have off, yet it is the one day the library is closed. That is not a guess: in a survey of 300 residents, 61 percent said they would come on Sunday afternoons. If even a fraction did, the library would reach exactly the readers its weekday hours miss. Granted, an extra day of staff costs money. But the board estimates that four Sunday hours would cost less than the library already spends replacing lost books, a modest price for serving the families who need it most.*

What changed:

- The **thesis** now previews the line: the people the library fails to reach.
- Each claim **depends** on the one before it. The families' schedule creates the need, the survey shows the need is real, and the commentary (*If even a fraction did...*) explains what the number implies.
- **Transitions name relations.** *That is not a guess* signals evidence for the previous claim; *Granted* concedes; *But* answers.
- The **objection is answered**, not just mentioned, which keeps the line moving instead of stalling.
- The irrelevant reason (good books) is gone. A line of reasoning is as much about what you cut as what you add.

A quick test for your own drafts: underline the first few words of every sentence. If they are mostly *also*, *another reason* and *in addition*, you have written a list. Rewrite until you can see *so*, *because*, *granted*, *therefore* and *which means*.

## Common mistakes

- **Mistaking the order of evidence for the line of reasoning.** The line is the sequence of *claims*; evidence supports them.
- **Calling an analogy the thesis.** Thoreau's machine is how he argues, not what he argues.
- **Commentary that restates.** *This shows that people agree* after a survey is a summary, not an explanation.
- **Gaps.** Two claims side by side with the link left for the reader to supply. Name the missing step.
- **Ignoring the turns.** In a reading passage, sentences beginning *But*, *Yet* or *In other words* are often where the line changes direction. Questions love them.
- **Mentioning an objection and leaving it.** An unanswered objection ends a paragraph on the other side's point.

## Exam connection

On Section I, reading questions on the line of reasoning take forms like *Which best describes the line of reasoning in the second paragraph?*, *The sentence in line 12 serves primarily to...*, or *The writer's reference to X functions in the argument as...*. Writing questions ask you to delete a sentence that interrupts the line, add commentary after a piece of evidence, or choose the best place for a sentence. In Section II, the evidence-and-commentary row (Row B, 0-4 points) of every essay rewards a sustained line of reasoning. A 4 explains how each piece of evidence supports a claim and how the claims build; a 2 has evidence with thin or repetitive commentary. In the rhetorical analysis essay, describing a writer's line of reasoning (*Thoreau first wins agreement, then...*) is often the clearest way to organise your own.
