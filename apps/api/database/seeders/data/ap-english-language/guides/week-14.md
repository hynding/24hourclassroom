## What this week is for

In Unit 3 you met the six methods of development: narration, description, comparison, definition, cause and effect, and classification. This week focuses on the two that matter most for the edges of an argument, because they so often decide how an argument begins: **definition** and **comparison**.

Many real disputes are disputes about a word. Is a part-time job “leadership”? Is a novel with a perfect plot “alive”? Is a student who reads for pleasure a “reader” worth listening to? A writer who controls the definition often controls the argument, and a writer who sets two cases side by side can make a difference look trivial or a likeness look false.

Virginia Woolf's *The Common Reader* is a book of essays about books, but it is also a book of arguments, and almost every one of them is built on a definition or a comparison. By Friday you should be able to:

- recognise definition (by function, by negation, stipulative, extended) and comparison (including analogy) in a passage, and explain what each does for the writer's purpose (5.C);
- use a definition or a matched comparison to open or develop your own argument (6.C);
- revise a student paragraph so that its definition and comparison carry the argument instead of decorating it.

## The skill explained: methods that make claims

A method of development is not a label to stick on a paragraph. Skill 5.C asks you to **explain the use** of a method **to accomplish a purpose**. So the question is always: *what does defining or comparing let this writer do?*

**Kinds of definition you will meet:**

- **Definition by function**: what the thing is for. Woolf: the essay exists to give pleasure.
- **Definition by negation**: what the thing is not. Woolf's common reader is not a critic or a scholar.
- **Stipulative definition**: how the writer will use a word in this argument. Woolf: by “materialists” I mean writers who make the trivial look enduring.
- **Extended definition**: a definition developed with examples, contrasts and limits over a paragraph or an essay.

**Kinds of comparison you will meet:**

- **Comparison of like things**: two novelists, two students, two policies.
- **Analogy**: a comparison of unlike things to clarify one through the other (a novel and a house).
- **Matched comparison**: two cases alike in every relevant way but one, used to argue that the one difference should or should not matter.

Each of these can be a claim. A definition by function sets a standard; once you accept that essays exist to give pleasure, you have accepted a way to judge every essay. A matched comparison forces a reader to justify a difference; if the only difference between two students is a paycheck, the reader must explain why pay matters.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Method of development | A way of building an argument or paragraph: narration, description, comparison, definition, cause and effect, classification. |
| Definition by function | Defining a thing by its purpose or effect. |
| Definition by negation | Defining a thing by what it is not. |
| Stipulative definition | Declaring how a term will be used in this argument. |
| Extended definition | A definition developed with examples, contrasts and limits. |
| Analogy | A comparison between unlike things that clarifies one by the other. |
| Matched comparison | Two cases equal in relevant ways but one, used to test that one difference. |
| Classification | Sorting a subject into kinds. |

## The text in context

Virginia Woolf (1882-1941) was a novelist and one of the most influential critics of her time. Much of her criticism began as reviews, many of them unsigned, for the *Times Literary Supplement* and other papers. In 1925 she collected and revised some of these pieces as *The Common Reader*, published the same year as her novel *Mrs. Dalloway*.

The title comes from a sentence in Samuel Johnson's life of the poet Thomas Gray, in which Johnson says he rejoices to agree with the common reader. Woolf takes up the phrase as an identity. She writes not as a professor or a professional critic but as a reader who reads for pleasure, and she argues that such a reader's judgments count.

The **audience** of these essays was the educated general public of Britain and America, people who read reviews and bought books but were not scholars. The **purpose** varies by essay: to define a reader, to judge an anthology, to challenge the most popular novelists of the day. In each case, definition and comparison are how Woolf persuades readers who have no particular reason to trust a reviewer.

## Close reading

### 1. Defining a reader by contrast: “The Common Reader”

> The common reader, as Dr. Johnson implies, differs from the critic and the scholar. He is worse educated, and nature has not gifted him so generously. He reads for his own pleasure rather than to impart knowledge or correct the opinions of others.

**Observe.** The definition begins with a contrast (“differs from the critic and the scholar”) and then lists deficiencies (“worse educated,” less gifted) before naming a motive (“for his own pleasure”).

**Interpret.** Woolf defines the common reader first by negation and then by function. He is not trained, but he has a purpose of his own.

**Function.** Admitting the common reader's limits makes the definition honest, and that honesty prepares the essay's modest claim: if such a reader has some say in literary reputation, it may be worth while to write down his opinions. A few lines later she calls him “Hasty, inaccurate, and superficial,” then turns that concession into a reason to listen. The whole collection rests on this definition.

### 2. Defining a form by its function: “The Modern Essay”

> Of all forms of literature, however, the essay is the one which least calls for the use of long words. The principle which controls it is simply that it should give pleasure; the desire which impels us when we take it from the shelf is simply to receive pleasure. Everything in an essay must be subdued to that end.

**Observe.** Two balanced clauses, one about the essay (“should give pleasure”) and one about the reader (“to receive pleasure”), both with “simply.” Then a rule: “Everything in an essay must be subdued to that end.”

**Interpret.** The definition is a standard. The writer must give pleasure; the reader comes for it; nothing else matters.

**Function.** Woolf's review is about to judge many essayists. By defining the form first, she makes every later verdict an application of a principle the reader has already accepted. Notice also how the essay opens: she refuses to trace the history of the essay “since, like all living things, its present is more important than its past.” The introduction rejects the historical opening and goes straight to definition.

### 3. Comparison inside a definition

> In the interval we may pass through the most various experiences of amusement, surprise, interest, indignation; we may soar to the heights of fantasy with Lamb or plunge to the depths of wisdom with Bacon, but we must never be roused. The essay must lap us about and draw its curtain across the world.

**Observe.** A list of experiences, a pair of contrasting writers (fantasy and wisdom, heights and depths), then “but” and a limit.

**Interpret.** The essay may go anywhere, as long as it does not wake us from its spell.

**Function.** The comparison of Lamb and Bacon shows the range the definition allows; the “but” shows its boundary. A definition with both range and limit is far more useful than a one-line definition, because it tells you what counts and what does not. In the next paragraph Woolf compares again: “A novel has a story, a poem rhyme.” The essay has neither, so it depends wholly on the art of writing.

### 4. Stipulating a word: “Modern Fiction”

> If we fasten, then, one label on all these books, on which is one word materialists, we mean by it that they write of unimportant things; that they spend immense skill and immense industry making the trivial and the transitory appear the true and the enduring.

**Observe.** “We mean by it” is a stipulation. The definition rests on two balanced pairs: “the trivial and the transitory” against “the true and the enduring.”

**Interpret.** “Materialist” in ordinary speech means a lover of money and things. Woolf means something else: a novelist who spends great skill on what does not matter.

**Function.** The stipulated definition *is* the essay's thesis about Wells, Bennett and Galsworthy. Woolf also tests her own definition with a borderline case: of Wells she admits that the word “falls notably wide of the mark,” then explains how it still applies. A strong extended definition faces the case that seems not to fit.

### 5. Analogy that praises in order to criticise

> There is not so much as a draught between the frames of the windows, or a crack in the boards. And yet--if life should refuse to live there?

**Observe.** Bennett's novels are compared to a perfectly built house. Then a dash, “And yet,” and a question.

**Interpret.** The craftsmanship is complete; the problem is that no one lives in the house.

**Function.** By granting Bennett's skill in full, Woolf makes her criticism hard to dismiss as envy or ignorance. The analogy separates craft from life, which is the distinction her definition of “materialists” depends on. This is comparison working as evidence (skill 3.A).

## From reading to writing: definition and comparison in your own argument

Here is a weak paragraph arguing that a school's leadership awards should recognise students with part-time jobs:

> *The dictionary says an extracurricular activity is anything outside the regular course of study. Our school's leadership awards count clubs, sports, and volunteer work, but not paid jobs. Jobs are good because they teach students how to manage money. Leadership should be defined by what a student does.*

What goes wrong: the dictionary definition states the obvious and is never used; the money point is a different argument; the final definition is vague and unsupported.

A stronger version:

> *The awards handbook describes eligible activities as commitments that build responsibility and leadership outside the classroom, and a lifeguard's shift meets that description as well as a club meeting does. A student who spends ten hours a week coaching the robotics team can win an award; a student who spends ten hours a week supervising children at the town pool cannot. Both show up on time, solve problems, and answer to adults who depend on them. The only difference is a paycheck, and for many students a job is not a choice but a necessity for their families. Leadership, in other words, should be defined by what a student does and whom the student serves, not by whether the student is paid.*

What works: the opening definition is the one in dispute (the school's own standard), applied at once to the case; the matched comparison leaves pay as the only difference; the necessity point strengthens the comparison; the paragraph ends on a sharpened definition that is now the claim.

**Definition and comparison in introductions.** Both methods make strong openings when they state the problem. Two cases side by side (*one student wins an award, an equally committed student does not qualify*) can establish the exigence in a sentence. A definition in dispute (*the school says leadership means service; its rules say service must be unpaid*) can lead straight to a thesis. What does not work is the dictionary opening, which delays the argument and claims nothing.

## Common mistakes

- **Naming the method without explaining it.** “Woolf uses comparison” earns nothing. Say what the comparison shows and why that matters to her purpose.
- **The dictionary opening.** It tells readers what they know. Use the definition in dispute.
- **Unmatched comparisons.** If two cases differ in many ways, the reader can explain the outcome by any of them. Match the cases.
- **Definitions without limits.** Say what the term includes and what it excludes, and test it against a borderline case.
- **Confusing analogy with proof.** An analogy clarifies; it persuades only if the two things really share the feature that matters.
- **Taking a stipulated word in its ordinary sense.** Woolf's “materialists” are not lovers of money; read the definition she gives.

## Exam connection

Section I reading questions often ask what a comparison “primarily serves to” do or how a definition “contributes to” the writer's argument. The right answer names a function linked to the purpose (sets a standard, makes a difference look arbitrary, grants a strength before a criticism), not just the method's name. Revision questions ask which sentence “best uses a comparison to” support a claim, or which definition would best replace a vague one.

In the argument essay (Q3), a stipulated definition can sharpen a thesis that would otherwise be vague, and a matched comparison can be your strongest piece of evidence. In synthesis (Q1), defining the term at the centre of the sources is often what lets you bring three or more sources into one line of reasoning. In rhetorical analysis (Q2), explaining how a writer's definition or comparison serves the purpose is exactly the kind of commentary that moves an essay up the evidence-and-commentary scale.
