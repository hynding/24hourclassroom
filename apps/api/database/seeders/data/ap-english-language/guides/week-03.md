# Week 03 · Building a Claim-and-Evidence Paragraph with Franklin's Convention Speech

**CED skill:** 4.A Develop a paragraph that includes a claim and evidence supporting the claim
**Big Idea:** Claims and Evidence (CLE)
**Read:** Benjamin Franklin, speech at the close of the Federal Convention, 17 September 1787, in *The Complete Works in Philosophy, Politics and Morals of the late Dr. Benjamin Franklin*, vol. 3 (1806), [Project Gutenberg #48138](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/48138)
**Spiral:** Week 1's rhetorical situation (1.A) explains why Franklin speaks as he does; week 2's reading skill (3.A) lets you take his argument apart. This week you put the parts back together in your own paragraphs. Week 4 practises reading and writing claims together.

## What you need to be able to do

- **4.A** Write a paragraph that opens with an arguable claim, offers specific evidence that supports the arguable part of that claim, and explains the connection in commentary.
- Integrate quotations and data into your own sentences instead of dropping them in.
- Revise a draft paragraph: sharpen a vague claim, cut irrelevant evidence, and replace thin commentary with real explanation.

In plain language: by Friday you should be able to write a 150-200 word paragraph in which every sentence does a job, and to say what job each one does.

## The skill: the argument paragraph

Week 2 taught you to *find* claims and evidence in someone else's argument. Skill 4.A turns that around. Almost every paragraph you write this year, in rhetorical analysis, argument or synthesis, will have the same three working parts.

| Part | Its job | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Claim** | States the paragraph's arguable point | Could a reasonable reader disagree? Can one paragraph prove it? |
| **Evidence** | Supports the arguable part of the claim | Is it specific, relevant and, where needed, sourced? |
| **Commentary** | Explains how the evidence proves the claim | Does it answer *so what?* without just repeating the evidence? |

A closing sentence often links the point back to the thesis or forward to the next paragraph.

**The claim.** In a body paragraph, the topic sentence should be a claim, not an announcement. *This paragraph will discuss retakes* tells the reader nothing to believe. *Every department should allow one retake per semester, because the math department's trial shows that retakes push students to relearn what they missed* tells the reader what to believe and why. The second version also previews what evidence will follow.

**The evidence.** Underline the arguable word in your claim (*should*, *every department*, *push students to relearn*) and choose evidence that addresses that word, not just the topic. Specific evidence names who, what, when and how many. Vague evidence (*lots of people agree*) persuades only readers who already agree.

**The commentary.** This is where AP essays are won or lost: four of the six rubric points are for evidence *and commentary*, and the commentary is what separates a 2 from a 4. Commentary explains what the evidence shows that a reader might not see at once, and why that matters for the claim. A useful rule: write at least as many words explaining your evidence as you spend quoting or citing it.

## The text in context

On 17 September 1787, after four months of closed-door debate in Philadelphia, the Constitutional Convention met to sign the finished Constitution. Several delegates still objected to parts of it, and some were threatening not to sign. A divided signing would send the document to the states looking contested, and the states still had to ratify it. Benjamin Franklin, aged 81 and the oldest delegate, wrote a short speech for the occasion; because he was too frail to deliver it at length, his fellow Pennsylvanian James Wilson read it aloud. "Mr. President" is George Washington, who presided.

In week 1's terms: the **writer** is the most famous and most senior man in the room, with nothing left to prove. The **exigence** is the signing. The **audience** is the delegates, and above all those "who may still have objections." The **purpose** is to persuade every member to sign. The **message**: doubting one's own certainty is wisdom, and a near-perfect constitution, accepted together, serves the public good better than a perfect one that no assembly could ever produce.

## Close readings

### 1. The confession that opens the argument

> I confess that I do not entirely approve of this constitution at present: but, Sir, I am not sure I shall never approve it; for having lived long, I have experienced many instances of being obliged, by better information, or fuller consideration, to change opinions, even on important subjects, which I once thought right, but found to be otherwise.

Franklin's first move is to stand with the doubters. Then he turns his long life into **evidence**: he has often been obliged to change opinions he once thought right, only to find them otherwise. The claim the evidence supports follows at once: "the older I grow, the more apt I am to doubt my own judgment, and to pay more respect to the judgment of others." This is a complete claim-evidence-commentary unit in two sentences. The evidence is personal experience, and it works because of who is speaking. A young delegate saying the same thing would sound unsure; the oldest man in the room saying it sounds wise.

### 2. The anecdote that disarms

> But, though many private persons think almost as highly of their own infallibility as of that of their sect, few express it so naturally as a certain French lady, who, in a little dispute with her sister, said, I don't know how it happens, sister, but I meet with nobody but myself that is always in the right.

An anecdote cannot prove a general claim; one French lady is not a survey. But this one does something proof cannot. It lets the delegates laugh at a habit they all share without anyone being accused. By the time Franklin applies the lesson to them, they have already agreed with it. Notice where he puts the anecdote: after the claim and the personal evidence, as illustration, not as the main support.

### 3. Reasoning from a general truth

> For when you assemble a number of men, to have the advantage of their joint wisdom, you inevitably assemble with those men, all their prejudices, their passions, their errors of opinion, their local interests, and their selfish views. From such an assembly can a perfect production be expected?

This supports a different claim: that no other convention "may be able to make a better constitution." The evidence is reasoning from a principle every listener can confirm from four months of debate. The list grows longer and less flattering as it goes, from prejudices to selfish views, and the rhetorical question lets the audience supply the obvious answer themselves. Then the commentary turns the point around: given such an assembly, "It therefore astonishes me, sir, to find this system approaching so near to perfection as it does."

### 4. The claim, the sacrifice, the request

> Thus I consent, sir, to this constitution, because I expect no better, and because I am not sure, that this is not the best. The opinions I have had of its errors, I sacrifice to the public good. I have never whispered a syllable of them abroad. Within these walls they were born, and here they shall die.

The word *thus* marks a conclusion drawn from everything before it, and the two *because* clauses restate the reasons. Then Franklin models the sacrifice he will ask of others. The last paragraph makes the request: that every objector would "doubt a little of his own infallibility, and, to make manifest our unanimity, put his name to this instrument." The phrase about infallibility is gently ironic: nobody claims to be infallible, yet the French lady has just shown how everyone behaves as if they were.

Step back and the whole speech has the shape of a well-built paragraph: claim, evidence (experience, anecdote, reasoning), commentary, and a closing link to the action required.

## Writing lesson: from a weak paragraph to a strong one

Here is an invented weak paragraph on a common prompt, whether students should be required to take a year of a world language.

> *Languages are a very important subject. Many colleges want to see them. According to one survey, a lot of people think languages are good. My friend took Spanish. Therefore students should have to take a language.*

Diagnose it, sentence by sentence. The first sentence is a truism, not a claim. *Many colleges* and *a lot of people* are vague evidence. *My friend took Spanish* is an anecdote with no point. *Therefore* draws a conclusion that nothing above has earned, and there is no commentary at all.

Now the revision.

> *Requiring a year of a world language would prepare students for jobs in our own county, where the need for bilingual workers is growing faster than the supply. The county's workforce board reported last year that one in four job postings at the regional hospital asked for a second language, up from one in ten in 2018. Those are not distant careers in diplomacy; they are nursing, reception and pharmacy jobs that our graduates apply for every spring. A single required year will not make anyone fluent, but it gives students the foundation to continue, and it signals to them, early, that the skill has local value.*

What changed:

1. **The claim is arguable and specific**: a required year, for local jobs.
2. **The evidence is specific and sourced**: a named kind of source, two numbers, a comparison over time.
3. **Commentary follows the evidence**: it explains why the numbers matter for these students (local jobs, not distant careers).
4. **The paragraph anticipates a doubt** (one year will not make anyone fluent) and answers it, a move Unit 9 develops fully.
5. **Every sentence does a job.** Read each one and name it: claim, evidence, commentary, concession, link.

Three habits to practise this week:

- **Integrate quotations.** Introduce them with a signal phrase and a precise verb (*Franklin admits*, *the report warns*), embed short phrases in your sentences, and never let a quotation stand alone as a sentence.
- **Choose one or two pieces of evidence and explain each**, rather than stacking four with no explanation between them.
- **End with a link, not a repeat.** The last sentence should show where the point leads, not restate the claim word for word.

## Common mistakes

- **Announcing instead of claiming.** *In this paragraph I will talk about...* is not a claim.
- **Dropped quotations.** A quotation in a sentence by itself, followed by *This shows that...*, is the most common sign of thin commentary.
- **Overloading.** Three statistics in a row with no explanation reads as a list.
- **Commentary that summarises.** Retelling what the evidence says is not explaining what it shows.
- **Unearned conclusions.** *Therefore* must follow from evidence actually given.
- **Irrelevant personal anecdotes.** Personal evidence works only when it bears on the claim, as Franklin's lifetime of changed opinions does.

## Exam connection

On Section I, writing questions test this skill directly: *Which sentence would best serve as the claim of this paragraph?*, *Which piece of evidence best supports the claim in sentence 2?*, *Should the writer keep or delete sentence 5?*, *Which revision of sentence 6 best explains how the evidence supports the claim?* The right answer is usually the option that is specific, relevant and connected to the claim. In Section II, every essay is a series of argument paragraphs. The rhetorical analysis essay asks you to make claims about a writer's choices and support them with quotations; the argument essay asks you to support your own claims with evidence from your knowledge and experience; the synthesis essay asks you to use sources as evidence. In all three, commentary is where the evidence-and-commentary points are earned.

## Self-check

1. What are the three working parts of an argument paragraph, and what is the test for each?
2. Identify the claim, the evidence and the commentary in Franklin's first two sentences.
3. Why does Franklin's personal experience work as evidence for this audience when it might not for another speaker?
4. What does the anecdote of the French lady do that a statistic could not?
5. Rewrite this dropped quotation as an integrated one with commentary: *Franklin hoped for unanimity. "Doubt a little of his own infallibility." This was smart.*
6. Take the weak world-language paragraph above and write a revision that argues the opposite position.
