## What you need to be able to do

- **3.C** Explain how a speaker answers an opponent: what the rebuttal concedes, what kind of rebuttal it is, and why it suits the objection.
- **4.C** Choose and write the right kind of rebuttal for an objection, and qualify your own position so that an opponent cannot caricature it.

In plain language: by Friday you should be able to listen to an objection and say *that rests on a fact, so I need counter-evidence* or *that rests on a hidden assumption, so I need to name it*, and then do it in two sentences.

## What this week is for

Mill wrote his rebuttal at a desk. Madison wrote for a newspaper and could revise before he printed. In the summer and autumn of 1858, Abraham Lincoln had to answer Stephen A. Douglas within minutes, standing on a platform in front of thousands of people, with reporters taking down every word. The debates show rebuttal at its most practical: no time to build the opponent's case at length, a crowd that might laugh or jeer, and an opponent who would reply again.

They also show that the moves of this unit are not academic conventions. Conceding, rebutting, refuting and qualifying are what people do when they have to persuade an audience that has just heard the other side.

## Kinds of rebuttal

Last week's lesson was to state the objection fairly and concede what is true. This week's is to choose how to answer. Five kinds cover most cases.

| Kind | When it fits | In the debates |
|---|---|---|
| **Denial with evidence** | The objection makes a factual claim you can show is false. | At Ottawa Lincoln says he refused to join the committee and left the convention to attend court. |
| **Burden of proof** | The objection is a charge that cannot be disproved but has not been supported. | A man cannot prove a negative; the accuser must offer proof. |
| **Exposing an assumption** | The objection depends on a premise it never states. | Douglas's policies are logical only if slavery is not wrong. |
| **Reductio ad absurdum** | The objection's principle leads to a conclusion its holders would reject. | Mill's opponents ask whether we should lay no taxes and make no wars. |
| **Reframing the issue** | The objection is aimed at the wrong question. | At Alton Lincoln calls Douglas's charges false issues and names the real one. |

The test is always the same: **what does the objection rest on?** Answer it there. Counter-evidence against a hidden assumption misses; exposing an assumption when the dispute is factual looks evasive.

## The text in context

In 1854 the Kansas–Nebraska Act, which Douglas wrote, allowed settlers in those territories to decide by vote whether to permit slavery. Douglas called the principle **popular sovereignty**. It reopened land that earlier compromises had closed to slavery, and opposition to it helped create the Republican Party. In 1857 the Supreme Court's Dred Scott decision declared that Congress could not bar slavery from the territories at all.

In 1858 the Illinois Republicans nominated Lincoln to challenge Douglas for his Senate seat. The two agreed to seven joint debates. In each, one man spoke for an hour, the other replied for an hour and a half, and the first closed for half an hour. **Exigence:** the Senate race and the national crisis over the spread of slavery. **Audience:** Illinois voters, many of them undecided, and through the newspapers the whole country. **Purpose:** for Lincoln, to show that the real difference between the parties was moral, not a matter of local preference.

Senators were then chosen by state legislatures, and the Illinois legislature returned Douglas. But the debates, published as a book in 1860, made Lincoln a national figure. Readers today should also know that both men, Lincoln included, made statements in these debates denying the political and social equality of Black Americans, positions that shape how historians weigh his arguments. This week's passages concern the logic of his case against the spread of slavery.

## Close reading

### 1. Ottawa: the burden of proof

> Now, I have no means of totally disproving such charges as this which the Judge makes. A man cannot prove a negative; but he has a right to claim that when a man makes an affirmative charge, he must offer some proof to show the truth of what he says.

**Observe.** The first sentence is a concession: Lincoln admits a limit on what he can do. The second turns on a semicolon and *but*, from what he cannot do to what he has a right to demand.

**Interpret.** Douglas had charged that Lincoln and Lyman Trumbull struck a secret bargain. Lincoln cannot prove that a private conversation never happened. Instead of trying, he moves the obligation to Douglas, who made the charge.

**Function.** This is the right rebuttal for this objection. A denial alone would be his word against Douglas's; the burden-of-proof move makes Douglas's silence on evidence the issue. Around it, Lincoln repeats that he has no doubt Douglas is “conscientious” in saying it, each time in quotation marks. The courteous word becomes ironic: belief is not proof. And the speech opens with a joke (being grossly misrepresented, he says, is “more apt to amuse him”), which lets the crowd laugh with him before he answers.

### 2. Galesburg: exposing the premise

> If you will take the Judge's speeches, and select the short and pointed sentences expressed by him,--as his declaration that he “don't care whether slavery is voted up or down,”--you will see at once that this is perfectly logical, if you do not admit that slavery is wrong. If you do admit that it is wrong, Judge Douglas cannot logically say he don't care whether a wrong is voted up or voted down.

**Observe.** Lincoln quotes Douglas's own phrase, concedes that it is “perfectly logical,” and then attaches a condition. The next sentence reverses the condition and the conclusion.

**Interpret.** Lincoln does not attack Douglas's reasoning. He grants it and shows what it needs: the unstated premise that slavery is not wrong. Then he applies the same test to Douglas's claim that any community has a right to slavery, and to his claim that slaves and horses should be treated alike as property. Each time the verdict is the same.

**Function.** The paired *if* clauses (skill 7.B) isolate a single premise and hand the choice to the listener. A voter who believes slavery is wrong cannot accept Douglas's indifference without contradicting himself. That is refutation by exposing an assumption, and its concession (Douglas is logical) is what makes it work.

### 3. Galesburg: qualifying his own position

> Now, I confess myself as belonging to that class in the country who contemplate slavery as a moral, social, and political evil, having due regard for its actual existence amongst us and the difficulties of getting rid of it in any satisfactory way, and to all the constitutional obligations which have been thrown about it; but, nevertheless, desire a policy that looks to the prevention of it as a wrong, and looks hopefully to the time when as a wrong it may come to an end.

**Observe.** One long sentence: a firm moral claim, three qualifications (existence, difficulty, constitutional obligations), and then *but, nevertheless* and a limited policy.

**Interpret.** Lincoln qualifies how he would act, not what he believes. He does not propose to abolish slavery where it exists; he proposes to stop its spread and hope for its end.

**Function.** Douglas's strategy was to paint Lincoln as a radical who would provoke war. The qualifications answer that charge before it is repeated. This is 4.C as defence: a qualified position is harder to caricature.

### 4. Alton: reframing, and an analogy

> You may have a wen or cancer upon your person, and not be able to cut it out, lest you bleed to death; but surely it is no way to cure it, to engraft it and spread it over your whole body.

**Observe.** A comparison (skill 5.C) in two halves joined by *but surely*.

**Interpret.** The first half is a concession: some evils cannot be removed at once without danger. The second is the rebuttal: that is no reason to spread them.

**Function.** The analogy carries Lincoln's whole qualified position in one image. In the same speech he dismissed Douglas's charges as “false issues” and named the real issue as whether slavery is regarded as a wrong. Reframing tells the audience that even a full answer to Douglas's charges would miss the point.

## Writing lesson: choosing the rebuttal

Take one objection and see how the choice of rebuttal changes the paragraph. The thesis: *Our school should replace its paper textbooks with digital editions.* The objection: *Screens are bad for students.*

> *Weak (denial without evidence):* That is not true. Screens are fine.

> *Better (counter-evidence):* Reading on screens can tire the eyes, but digital editions allow adjustable text size and night modes that make long reading easier for many students.

> *Strongest (exposing an assumption, then qualifying):* The objection assumes that students would read more on screens than they already do, but they would simply read the same assigned pages on a different surface. The fair concern is total screen time, so the school should pair digital textbooks with printable chapters for students who prefer paper.

The strongest version names what the objection rests on, answers it there, and ends with a qualified claim that keeps the thesis while absorbing the legitimate part of the concern.

## Common mistakes

- **Rebutting the wrong part of an objection.** Find what it rests on first.
- **Denying without evidence.** *That is not true* is a claim that needs its own support.
- **Mistaking irony for praise.** Lincoln's “conscientious” is courteous only on the surface.
- **Forgetting to qualify your own side.** A position stated without limits invites caricature.
- **Treating a debate passage as a list of points.** Each reply answers something specific; identify what.

## Exam connection

Section I passages often come from speeches and debates, and a common stem asks how a sentence “responds to” or “anticipates” an opposing view. The revision sets will ask you to add a concession, choose a turn signal, or replace a dismissal with a fair acknowledgement, all practised in this week's draft.
