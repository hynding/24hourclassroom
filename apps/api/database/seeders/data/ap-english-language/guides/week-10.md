## What you need to be able to do

- **5.C** Recognise the method a writer uses to develop a claim (narration, description, comparison, definition, cause and effect, classification) and explain what that method accomplishes for the writer's purpose and audience.

In plain language: by Friday you should be able to say *Wollstonecraft compares women to soldiers in order to prove that education, not sex, causes the faults her readers blame on women*, and not just *she uses comparison*.

## The skill: six methods, one question

A **method of development** is the way a writer fills out a claim. You may have met these as types of essay (the narrative essay, the compare-and-contrast essay). In AP Language they are tools inside an argument, and a single paragraph often uses two or three. The CED names six.

| Method | What it does | What it can do for an argument |
|---|---|---|
| Narration | Tells events in sequence | Makes an abstract problem concrete and felt through one case |
| Description | Renders a person, place or condition in concrete detail | Lets the reader witness what the writer wants judged |
| Comparison | Sets two things side by side | Uses a familiar case to explain or prove something about an unfamiliar one |
| Definition | Fixes the meaning of a term | Decides what a contested argument is actually about |
| Cause and effect | Traces how one thing produces another | Shows where a problem comes from, and so what would fix it |
| Classification | Sorts a subject into kinds | Organises a crowded topic; exposes what the other side has lumped together |

Recognising the method is the easy half. The skill that earns points is the other half: **purpose**. For every method, ask: *what does this reader need, at this point, that this method supplies?* A reader who doubts that a problem is real needs description or narration. A reader who misunderstands a key word needs definition. A reader who thinks a problem is natural or inevitable needs cause and effect. A reader who thinks two cases are different needs a comparison showing they are alike.

Two cautions. First, **comparison is judged by the respect in which things are alike**. Government is like a machine in some ways and not in others; an analogy persuades only if the likeness holds where the argument needs it. Second, **cause and effect needs a mechanism**. Two things that happen together are not cause and effect until the writer shows how one produces the other, or rules out other causes.

## The text in context

Mary Wollstonecraft (1759-1797) was a London writer, teacher and translator who had run a school, worked as a governess and earned her living by her pen, which was rare for a woman of her time. In 1790 she answered Edmund Burke's attack on the French Revolution with *A Vindication of the Rights of Men*. Two years later she turned the revolution's language on its own blind spot.

**Exigence.** Revolutionary France was redesigning its schools. The statesman Talleyrand had presented a report on public education that gave girls only a limited, domestic schooling. Meanwhile the most popular books on female education in Britain, conduct books written by men and Rousseau's novel *Émile* above all, taught that girls should be trained to please. Wollstonecraft dedicated her *Vindication* (1792) to Talleyrand and argued that if the rights of man were real, they had to include women.

**Audience.** Educated British readers of both sexes, especially the middle class; legislators and teachers; and the male authorities on education whose books she answers. She speaks to women directly, promising to treat them as “rational creatures,” while arguing with men.

**Purpose.** To persuade readers that the faults charged against women (vanity, weakness, frivolity) are the *effects of an education*, not of nature, and that a rational education would make women better people, better partners and better citizens.

That purpose explains her method. Her readers' strongest belief was that women's weakness was natural. The only way to defeat a claim about nature is a claim about cause. So the whole book is built on cause and effect, and her other methods (comparison, definition, description) serve it.

## Close reading

### 1. The opening: cause and effect, carried by a comparison

> I have turned over various books written on the subject of education, and patiently observed the conduct of parents and the management of schools; but what has been the result? a profound conviction, that the neglected education of my fellow creatures is the grand source of the misery I deplore; and that women in particular, are rendered weak and wretched by a variety of concurring causes, originating from one hasty conclusion.

**Recognise the method.** Read the verbs and nouns: “source,” “rendered,” “causes,” “originating.” This is the vocabulary of causation. The effect is women “weak and wretched”; the cause is “neglected education.”

**Explain the purpose.** She begins with her method of inquiry (reading, observing) so that the causal claim arrives as “a profound conviction” earned by evidence, not a complaint. Note the passive “are rendered”: weakness is something *done to* women, which is the whole argument in a verb.

Then the comparison:

> for, like the flowers that are planted in too rich a soil, strength and usefulness are sacrificed to beauty; and the flaunting leaves, after having pleased a fastidious eye, fade, disregarded on the stalk, long before the season when they ought to have arrived at maturity. One cause of this barren blooming I attribute to a false system of education.

**Recognise the method.** A simile, a comparison between unlike things. But notice what it compares: a plant's condition to the soil it was planted in. The comparison has a causal structure.

**Explain the purpose.** The image lets readers *see* the effect before they have to accept the cause. Showy leaves that wither before maturity are a picture of a girl admired at eighteen and disregarded at thirty. And the image assigns the blame to the gardener, not the flower: rich soil, meaning flattery and indulgence, does the damage. The oxymoron “barren blooming” compresses the whole picture into two words, and the next clause names the cause outright. The comparison serves the causal argument; it is not decoration.

### 2. The soldiers: comparison as proof

> As a proof that education gives this appearance of weakness to females, we may instance the example of military men, who are, like them, sent into the world before their minds have been stored with knowledge or fortified by principles. The consequences are similar [...] Where is then the sexual difference, when the education has been the same?

**Recognise the method.** Comparison and contrast, between two groups of the same kind (people with the same schooling).

**Explain the purpose.** The opening words say it: “As a proof.” This comparison does not illustrate, it argues. Wollstonecraft's opponents say women's faults are natural to their sex. She finds a group of *men* who received the same kind of education, a fashionable polish without knowledge, and shows that they have the same faults: love of dress and dancing, a desire to please, politeness in “the minor virtues.” Same education, same faults, different sex. So sex cannot be the cause. It is the logic of a controlled experiment, done with words. A little later she compresses it into a phrase: “they both acquire manners before morals.”

The choice of soldiers is shrewd for her audience. Nobody in 1792 thought officers were naturally weak or womanly, so readers cannot explain away their faults as “nature.” That is a comparison chosen for the reader it has to convince.

### 3. Definition: turning an insult into a goal

> I am aware of an obvious inference: from every quarter have I heard exclamations against masculine women; but where are they to be found? If, by this appellation, men mean to inveigh against their ardour in hunting, shooting, and gaming, I shall most cordially join in the cry; but if it be, against the imitation of manly virtues [...] all those who view them with a philosophical eye must, I should think, wish with me, that they may every day grow more and more masculine.

**Recognise the method.** Definition, specifically redefinition. She splits a term in two (“If ... men mean ... but if it be”) and treats each meaning separately.

**Explain the purpose.** “Masculine woman” was an insult that could end an argument about women's education before it started: educate women and you will make them masculine. Wollstonecraft does not deny the charge; she defines it. One meaning (rough sports and gambling) she rejects along with her critics. The other (the talents and virtues that “ennoble the human character”) she embraces. By the end of the paragraph the insult has become a goal. Whoever controls the definition of a key term controls what the argument is about.

### 4. Definition by exposure, and a metaphor that describes an effect

> Women are every where in this deplorable state; for, in order to preserve their innocence, as ignorance is courteously termed, truth is hidden from them, and they are made to assume an artificial character before their faculties have acquired any strength. Taught from their infancy, that beauty is woman's sceptre, the mind shapes itself to the body, and, roaming round its gilt cage, only seeks to adorn its prison.

**Recognise the methods.** A short redefinition (“innocence, as ignorance is courteously termed”) and a metaphor of confinement (the “gilt cage”), both inside another causal sentence (“Taught from their infancy ... the mind shapes itself”).

**Explain the purpose.** The aside strips a virtue of its polite name: what parents call innocence is ignorance. The metaphor then describes the effect of the teaching from the inside: a mind so used to its decorated prison that it spends its energy decorating it further. The cage is gilded, so it does not look like a cage. That is exactly why, in her argument, the harm goes unnoticed.

## From reading to writing: choosing a method

Next week's skill (6.C) is using these methods yourself, but you can start now by noticing what each method fixes in a weak draft. Here is a student's paragraph for a city council:

> *Our city should plant trees in the Eastside neighborhood. Pine Street's sidewalk measured 128 degrees last July, and shaded Elm Street's measured 96. Heat causes problems. The Eastside has the most heat emergencies in the city. A tree is like an air conditioner.*

The evidence is good. The development is missing. *Heat causes problems* is where the cause-and-effect link should be, and it says nothing. *A tree is like an air conditioner* is a comparison with no point of likeness. Here is a revision that uses three methods on purpose:

> *Our city should plant trees in the Eastside neighborhood, because shade is the cheapest cooling the city can buy for the residents who need it most. Last July, Pine Street's treeless sidewalk measured 128 degrees while shaded Elm Street's, two blocks away, measured 96. Pavement that hot keeps radiating heat into nearby homes after sunset, so residents of treeless streets get little relief even at night, which helps explain why the Eastside has the most heat emergencies in the city. Last August a retired bus driver on Pine Street spent two nights in the hospital after his apartment reached 94 degrees indoors. Like an air conditioner, a mature tree cools the air around it; unlike one, it needs no electricity, which matters in a neighborhood where many families cannot afford a cooling bill.*

What each method does:

- **Comparison** (Pine against Elm) supplies the evidence: same city, same day, the difference is the trees.
- **Cause and effect** (*Pavement that hot keeps radiating heat...*) supplies the mechanism that joins the hot sidewalks to the emergencies.
- **Narration** (the bus driver) makes the statistic human, briefly, without replacing it.
- **Comparison again** (the air conditioner) now names the likeness that matters, cooling, and the difference that matters more, cost.

## Common mistakes

- **Naming the method without its purpose.** *Wollstonecraft uses comparison* earns nothing alone. Say what the comparison proves, and to whom.
- **Confusing a simile with the method it serves.** The flower simile is a comparison, but its job is causal. When a question asks for the *primary* method, choose the one that carries the claim.
- **Treating cause and effect as correlation.** A causal claim needs a mechanism or a reason to rule out other causes.
- **Accepting a weak comparison.** Ask whether the two things are alike in the respect the argument needs.
- **Thinking narration is only for personal essays.** A one-sentence case inside an argument is narration too.
- **Forgetting that definition can be an argument.** A redefinition (*what is called innocence is ignorance*) is often a writer's sharpest claim.

## Exam connection

Section I asks method-of-development questions in several forms: *The writer develops the second paragraph primarily by...*, *The comparison in the third paragraph serves to...*, *The writer's definition of X functions to...*, *The anecdote at the start of the passage primarily...*. The right answer always pairs a method with a purpose; distractors name the right method with the wrong purpose, or a plausible purpose with the wrong method. Writing questions ask which sentence would best develop a comparison, add a brief example, or explain a cause. In the rhetorical analysis essay, methods of development are often the clearest choices to analyse: *Wollstonecraft first shows the effect through a simile, then proves its cause through a comparison* is a line of reasoning for an essay.
