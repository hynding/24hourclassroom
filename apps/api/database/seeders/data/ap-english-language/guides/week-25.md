## What this week is for

This is the last week of Unit 7 and the most hands-on. The exam's Section I includes twenty or so writing questions on drafts, and a good share of them come down to grammar and mechanics: a comma splice, a dangling phrase, a verb that does not agree, a series that is not parallel, a colon where a semicolon belongs. These are not rules for their own sake. Each error makes a reader stumble or misread, and a reader who stumbles stops trusting the argument.

By the end of the week you should be able to:

- **7.C** Explain what a writer's punctuation and grammatical choices do: join, separate, balance, set aside, emphasise, or prevent a misreading.
- **8.C** Find and fix the errors that blur meaning, and choose punctuation that gives your own argument the emphasis you intend.

The week's reading is an essay that argues for exactly this: William Hazlitt's “On Familiar Style,” a case for plain, precise English, written in the style it recommends.

## The skill explained: four toolkits

### 1. Punctuation that joins and separates

| Mark | What comes before | What comes after | What it signals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Period | independent clause | new sentence | full stop |
| Semicolon | independent clause | independent clause (or complex list items) | a related idea of equal weight |
| Colon | independent clause | explanation, example, list or answer | here is what I promised |
| Comma + and, but, so, yet, or, for, nor | independent clause | independent clause | a relationship named by the conjunction |

The **comma splice** (two independent clauses joined by a comma alone) is the most common error on student drafts and on the exam. *However*, *therefore* and *moreover* are adverbs, not conjunctions, so *the plan failed, however it was cheap* is still a splice. Write *the plan failed; however, it was cheap*.

### 2. Punctuation that sets aside: the volume scale

An interrupter can be set off three ways, at three volumes:

- **commas**, normal volume: *The cost, about a million dollars, was never explained.*
- **dashes**, loud: *The cost--about a million dollars--was never explained.*
- **parentheses**, quiet: *The cost (about a million dollars) was never explained.*

A single dash near the end of a sentence creates a pause before a turn or a punch: *The report ran to forty pages--and made no recommendation.*

### 3. Modifiers that attach to the right word

A **dangling modifier** is an opening phrase with nothing to describe: *Walking to school, the rain started.* The fix is to put the doer after the comma: *Walking to school, I got caught in the rain.* A **misplaced modifier** sits next to the wrong word: *She only reads the summaries* (and does nothing else with them?) against *She reads only the summaries*. Put the modifier beside what it limits; last week's qualifiers depend on this.

### 4. Agreement and parallelism

**Subject-verb agreement.** Find the true subject and ignore what comes between it and the verb: *The list of complaints is long.* *Each of the blocks has a strip of grass.* **Pronoun reference.** A pronoun needs one clear antecedent; *they* and *this* are the usual offenders. **Parallelism.** Items that do the same job take the same form: *to plan, to fund and to build*, not *to plan, funding and we will build*.

## The text in context

**Writer.** William Hazlitt (1778-1830) was an English essayist and critic who began as a painter. He wrote for magazines and newspapers, and he quarrelled, in print and out of it, with many of the writers of his age, including his friends Wordsworth, Coleridge and Charles Lamb.

**Publication.** “On Familiar Style” appeared in the second volume of *Table-Talk; or, Original Essays* (1822; later editions are subtitled *Essays on Men and Manners*). The collection's title promises conversation at the dinner table, and the essays are written in that voice: first person, opinionated, full of examples from daily life.

**Exigence and audience.** Hazlitt's readers were the educated, magazine-reading public of London. Hazlitt himself had been attacked by reviewers for writing too loosely, in “vulgarisms and broken English.” The essay is partly his answer: a definition of the style he writes in, and an argument that it is harder and better than the pompous Latinate style many readers still admired, the style he associates with Samuel Johnson.

**Purpose.** To persuade readers that plain English is not careless English, and that the measure of a word is how exactly it fits the idea.

## Close reading

### 1. Defining by contrast, balanced on a semicolon

Hazlitt opens with a claim that sounds simple: “It is not easy to write a familiar style.” He corrects a misreading at once: “Many people mistake a familiar for a vulgar style, and suppose that to write without affectation is to write at random.” Then he defines the style by what it refuses and what it chooses:

> It is not to take the first word that offers, but the best word in common use; it is not to throw words together in any combinations we please, but to follow and avail ourselves of the true idiom of the language.

Two independent clauses, each built on *not... but*, held in balance by a semicolon. The semicolon says: these two ideas are equal and belong together. The parallel structure makes the definition easy to remember, and its precision is the precision Hazlitt is describing.

### 2. A colon as hinge

> Thus it is easy to affect a pompous style, to use a word twice as big as the thing you want to express: it is not so easy to pitch upon the very word that exactly fits it.

The first clause is about what is easy; the second about what is hard. The colon makes the second clause the completion of the first: the promised other half of the thought. It also leaves Hazlitt's main point in the final position, where emphasis falls. Notice the plain, physical verbs (“pitch upon,” “fits”) in the second half, and the comic exaggeration of “a word twice as big as the thing” in the first. The sentence mocks pomposity in plain words.

### 3. An aside at low volume

> I have been (I know) loudly accused of revelling in vulgarisms and broken English. I cannot speak to that point; but so far I plead guilty to the determined use of acknowledged idioms and common elliptical expressions.

The parentheses turn “(I know)” into a quick, conversational aside, a shrug. Put the same words between dashes and the admission would sound wounded or defiant; between commas it would sound neutral. The parentheses fit an essay about the familiar style, because they sound like talk.

The second sentence is a qualified concession, the move from week 23. “So far” limits what he pleads guilty to, and his restatement turns “vulgarisms and broken English” into “acknowledged idioms and common elliptical expressions”: the same habits, renamed as virtues.

### 4. Colon, dash and analogy; then plain clauses

> It is not pomp or pretension, but the adaptation of the expression to the idea, that clenches a writer's meaning:--as it is not the size or glossiness of the materials, but their being fitted each to its place, that gives strength to the arch; or as the pegs and nails are as necessary to the support of the building as the larger timbers, and more so than the mere showy, unsubstantial ornaments.

The colon and dash together (a common nineteenth-century combination) announce an illustration and let it burst in. The two analogies (the arch, the pegs and nails) are joined by a semicolon because each is a long unit with its own commas. Then Hazlitt drops into short, plain sentences:

> I hate anything that occupies more space than it is worth. I hate to see a load of bandboxes go along the street, and I hate to see a parcel of big words without anything in them.

Three parallel *I hate* clauses, homely nouns (bandboxes were light hat boxes, bulky and nearly empty) and no ornament. After the elaborate arch sentence, the plain ones show what he means.

Elsewhere in the essay Hazlitt compares words to coins: “I conceive that words are like money, not the worse for being common, but that it is the stamp of custom alone that gives them circulation or value.” Common words are not cheap; they are the currency everyone accepts.

## From reading to writing: a revision workshop

Here is an invented draft paragraph, then a revision.

> *Every one of the town's playgrounds need repair. Built in the 1970s, rust covers the swings at Miller Park, the slide at Oak Street has a cracked step. Parents have complained, the council has not acted. The repairs would be expensive. The council should fix the worst playgrounds this year, they should also plan the rest.*

Work through it with four questions.

1. **Does every verb agree with its true subject?** *Every one... need* becomes *Every one... needs*.
2. **Does every opening phrase describe the word right after it?** *Built in the 1970s, rust covers* says the rust was built in the 1970s. Attach the phrase to the swings.
3. **Where are two sentences pretending to be one?** Three comma splices. Fix each with the punctuation or conjunction that states the relationship you mean.
4. **Is the emphasis where the argument needs it?** *The repairs would be expensive* stands alone as a full sentence and hands the objection the floor. Subordinate it.

> *Every one of the town's playgrounds needs repair. The swings at Miller Park, built in the 1970s, are covered in rust, and the slide at Oak Street has a cracked step. Parents have complained; the council has not acted. Although the repairs would be expensive, the council should fix the worst playgrounds this year and plan the rest.*

The semicolon in *Parents have complained; the council has not acted* is a deliberate choice: two equal facts, set side by side, and the reader draws the conclusion. The *although* clause concedes the cost and leaves the proposal at the end of the main clause.

**Punctuation for emphasis in your own essays.** Use a dash when you want a word to land: *The district spent a million dollars on laptops--and gave teachers no training.* Use a colon when you have promised something: *The plan has one weakness: no one has said who will pay.* Use each sparingly. A paragraph full of dashes sounds breathless, and emphasis everywhere is emphasis nowhere.

## Common mistakes

- **Comma before however.** *It was cheap, however it failed* is a splice. Use a semicolon before *however*.
- **Colon after a verb or after such as.** *The causes are: cost and time* and *causes such as: cost* are both wrong.
- **Semicolon before a dependent clause.** *The plan failed; because no one funded it* is wrong. No punctuation is needed before *because*.
- **The vague this.** *This shows that...* after a long paragraph points at nothing. Add a noun: *This reversal shows.*
- **Mismatched series.** *Students need time to eat, to rest, and for studying.* Make all three infinitives.
- **Treating grammar as decoration in analysis.** In an essay, do not list Hazlitt's punctuation marks. Explain what one does: the colon makes the contrast between easy pomp and difficult exactness a single, balanced thought.

## Exam connection

**Section I.** Writing questions often ask *Which choice best maintains the sentence pattern*, *Which version is punctuated correctly* or *Which revision corrects the error*. Read the whole sentence, find the subject and main verb, then test each option against one question at a time: Is each clause attached correctly? Does the verb agree? Does the modifier attach to the right word? Is the series parallel? Reading questions may ask what a dash, a colon or a parenthesis “primarily serves to” do; think of the volume scale and of what each mark promises.

**Section II.** No essay row scores grammar separately, but the rubric expects you to communicate your argument clearly, and errors that blur meaning cost you in the evidence-and-commentary row because the reader cannot follow your reasoning. Save two minutes at the end of each essay for the routine below. In the rhetorical analysis essay, punctuation is good evidence when you explain its effect.

**A two-minute proofreading routine.** (1) Read your thesis alone: one clear, complete sentence? (2) Read the first sentence of each body paragraph: does the verb agree, and does any opening phrase dangle? (3) Scan for commas between two claims (splices) and for a bare *this* or *they*.

2. What is the difference between a semicolon and a colon?
3. Explain the volume scale: how do commas, dashes and parentheses differ when they set off the same words?
4. Why is *The plan was cheap, however it failed* incorrect, and how would you fix it?
5. In Hazlitt's sentence about using “a word twice as big as the thing you want to express,” what does the colon do?
