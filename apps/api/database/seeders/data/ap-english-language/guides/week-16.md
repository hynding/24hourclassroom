# Week 16 · Organization and Transitions in Bacon's Essays

**CED skills:** 5.B Explain how the organization of a text creates unity and coherence and reflects a line of reasoning · 6.B Use transitional elements to guide the reader through the line of reasoning of an argument
**Read:** Francis Bacon, “Of Studies” and “Of Revenge,” from *The Essays or Counsels, Civil and Moral* (1625), [Project Gutenberg #575](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/575)
**Spiral:** Week 9 taught the line of reasoning (5.A, 6.A): which claims support a thesis and how. This week asks where those claims go on the page and how a reader gets from one to the next. ## What you need to be able to do

- **5.B** Look at how a text is arranged (the order of its claims, the shape of its sentences, the words that link them) and explain how that arrangement makes the argument unified, coherent and true to its line of reasoning.
- **6.B** In your own writing and in revision questions, choose transitional elements that name the real relationship between ideas: contrast, cause, example, restatement, concession, summary.

In plain language: by the end of the week you should be able to say *Bacon's “Of Studies” stays clear because every list of three answers the list before it*, and you should be able to fix a draft paragraph whose sentences are all correct but do not connect.

## Unity, coherence and the order of claims

Two words carry this week. A text has **unity** when everything in it serves one purpose: every sentence supports its paragraph's claim, and every paragraph supports the thesis. A text has **coherence** when a reader can follow it: each sentence visibly connects to the one before, and each paragraph to the one before that. The two are different. A paragraph can stay entirely on topic, with every sentence about the school library, and still feel like a pile of facts because nothing tells the reader how the facts relate.

Coherence comes from three sources, and strong writers use all of them.

1. **Order.** Claims arranged so that each one builds on what the reader already knows. Common orders for an argument's body are order of importance (often the strongest reason last), problem then solution, cause then effect, claim then objection then reply, and general then particular.
2. **Repetition.** Key terms repeated, or carried forward by pronouns and clear synonyms, so the reader knows the subject has not changed. A useful habit is the old-to-new pattern: begin a sentence with something the last sentence established and end it with the new information.
3. **Transitional elements.** Words, phrases, clauses and whole sentences that state how one idea relates to the next.

The third is what most students think of first, and it is where the most mistakes happen, so it deserves its own table.

| Relationship | Typical transitions | What the transition commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| Addition | moreover, also, in addition | The next point is of the same kind and supports the same claim. |
| Contrast | however, yet, but, on the other hand | The next sentence pushes against the last: an exception, an objection, a turn. |
| Cause and effect | therefore, so, as a result, consequently | The second idea follows from the first. That is a claim, and it needs evidence. |
| Example | for instance, for example | The next sentence is one case of the general claim just made. |
| Restatement | that is, in other words | The next sentence says the same thing more plainly. |
| Concession | granted, admittedly, to be sure | You are about to admit a point to the other side, and then reply. |
| Sequence | first, then, finally | The order matters in time or in steps. |
| Summary | in short, in sum, in the end | You are gathering several reasons into one. |

Read the right-hand column carefully. A transition is not decoration; it is a small claim about how your ideas fit together. “Therefore” says *this follows*. If it does not follow, the reader notices, and a reader who notices a false “therefore” stops trusting the argument.

## The text in context

Francis Bacon (1561-1626) was an English lawyer and statesman who rose to be Lord Chancellor under James I, fell from office in 1621 after admitting he had accepted gifts from people with cases before him, and spent his last years writing. He is remembered above all as a philosopher of science who argued that knowledge should be built from observation and experiment rather than inherited from ancient authorities.

His *Essays* grew across his life. The first edition of 1597 held ten short pieces; the 1612 edition held thirty-eight; the final edition of 1625 held fifty-eight. Bacon borrowed the word *essay* from the French writer Montaigne, but his essays are very different: not wandering self-portraits, but compressed counsel for ambitious men making their way in public life. His audience was educated readers who wanted practical wisdom, and his purpose was to give them maxims they could remember and use. That purpose shapes his style. The sentences are short or tightly balanced, the transitions few, and the ideas many. Reading Bacon is like reading an outline that someone has polished until every line is quotable.

That density is exactly why he is useful this week. An essay with so few connecting words should be hard to follow. It is not, and the reasons are the reasons this unit is about.

## Close reading

### 1. A first sentence that maps the paragraph

> STUDIES serve for delight, for ornament, and for ability. Their chief use for delight, is in privateness and retiring; for ornament, is in discourse; and for ability, is in the judgment, and disposition of business.

**Observe.** The first sentence names three uses. The second sentence takes them up again, in the same order, and assigns each a place: private reading, conversation, the management of affairs.

**Interpret.** The first sentence is a preview. It works like the thesis that names its reasons, which you practised in Unit 2: the reader knows what is coming before it arrives.

**Function.** Because the second sentence follows the map exactly, the reader never has to wonder which use is under discussion. Order alone creates coherence here; there is not a single transition word in either sentence.

### 2. A second triad that answers the first

> To spend too much time in studies is sloth; to use them too much for ornament, is affectation; to make judgment wholly by their rules, is the humor of a scholar.

**Observe.** Another set of three, in a parallel pattern (*to do X too much is Y*), and in the same order as before: delight overdone becomes laziness, ornament overdone becomes showing off, ability overdone becomes the rigid bookishness of a man who judges everything by rules.

**Function.** The repeated shape tells the reader that this sentence is the shadow of the opening. Bacon has turned from praise to warning without writing *however*; the parallel structure is the transition. This is a key point for 5.B: organization can carry relationships that most writers would spell out in connectives.

### 3. Connectives that carry real weight

> Crafty men contemn studies, simple men admire them, and wise men use them; for they teach not their own use; but that is a wisdom without them, and above them, won by observation.

**Observe.** Three attitudes, ending with the one Bacon endorses. Then two connectives: *for* (meaning “because”) and *but*.

**Interpret.** The last word of the triad, *use*, is the first idea of the next clause: wise men use studies, because studies do not teach how they should be used. The repeated word is a hinge. Then *but* turns to where that knowledge does come from: observation, which is to say experience.

**Function.** When Bacon does use a transition, it is the exact one. *For* gives a reason; *but* marks a limit. A reader can trace the logic of the whole sentence through its two small connecting words.

### 4. A restatement that translates a metaphor

> Some books are to be tasted, others to be swallowed, and some few to be chewed and digested; that is, some books are to be read only in parts; others to be read, but not curiously; and some few to be read wholly, and with diligence and attention.

**Observe.** A classification of books, made vivid by eating (a method of development from week 10), then *that is* and a literal version of the same three classes.

**Function.** The restatement transition holds the figurative and the literal sentence together as a single idea. It also prevents misreading: *tasted* might mean “enjoyed,” but the gloss makes it mean “read only in parts.” Restatement is an underused transition in student writing, and it is often the right one after a figure of speech or a statistic.

### 5. “Therefore” as a hinge in “Of Revenge”

> There is no man doth a wrong, for the wrong's sake; but thereby to purchase himself profit, or pleasure, or honor, or the like. Therefore why should I be angry with a man, for loving himself better than me?

**Observe.** A general claim about human motives, then *Therefore* and a rhetorical question in the first person.

**Function.** The transition makes the question a conclusion: if every wrongdoer simply loves himself, anger at him is as odd as anger at anyone's self-interest. The test of a causal transition is to rewrite the pair as *Because ..., therefore ...*, and here it holds. “Of Revenge” is built from a chain of such compact reasons, several of them introduced by *For*, *Certainly* or *therefore*. Its line of reasoning (5.A) is cumulative, and its organization (5.B) makes each reason a separate, clearly linked step.

## Writing lesson: revising for coherence

Here is a weak body paragraph from a student essay arguing that the town should keep its public pools open.

> *The pools are important. Also, many families cannot afford a private club. Also, the pools give teenagers summer jobs. However, the pools cost the town money. The pools are a good thing for the town. Lifeguards are trained in first aid.*

Every sentence is on topic, so the paragraph has unity of a sort, but it is not coherent. The two uses of *also* hide what kind of reasons these are. *However* introduces an objection that is never answered. The fifth sentence just repeats the first. And the last sentence is a stray detail that belongs nowhere in particular.

Here is a revision.

> *The town's pools are one of the few summer services that reach families who cannot afford a private club, and that is the strongest reason to keep them open. They also do something a club cannot: each summer they employ about forty teenagers, many in their first paid jobs. Admittedly, the pools cost the town money. Yet most of that cost is lifeguard wages, which means the budget line critics want to cut is the same line that pays those first jobs. In short, closing the pools would save less than it seems and take away more than it saves.*

What changed? The claim now names its reason. The second sentence begins with *They*, carrying the subject forward, and states how the new reason differs from the first. *Admittedly* and *Yet* turn the objection into a concession and a reply. *Which means* links the cost back to the jobs, so the two halves of the paragraph meet. *In short* closes the paragraph by gathering its reasons. The stray lifeguard detail was either cut or put to work.

Notice what did not happen: the revision does not have a transition on every sentence. The second sentence relies on the pronoun *They*; the fourth relies on order. Choose transitions where the relationship would otherwise be hidden.

## Common mistakes

- **List transitions.** *Also*, *another reason* and *next* say only “one more thing.” An argument built on them reads like a list. Ask how the new reason relates to the last one and say so.
- **The false *therefore*.** A cause-and-effect transition between two facts that merely happened together claims more than the evidence shows. When you cannot prove cause, use *at the same time* or *meanwhile*.
- **The dangling concession.** *Granted* or *admittedly* without a following *but* or *yet* leaves the reader thinking you have given up the point.
- **Elegant variation.** Changing the key term every sentence (pools, facilities, amenities, venues) makes readers wonder whether the subject has changed. Repeat the important word.
- **Transitions for variety.** Picking *however* because you used *moreover* last time. Choose the transition for its meaning.
- **Mistaking order for chronology.** On the exam, “organization” rarely means “the order things happened.” It usually means the order of claims and why that order serves the reasoning.

## Exam connection

On **Section I**, about a fifth of the writing questions test these skills directly. Expect stems such as *The writer wants to begin sentence 5 with a word or phrase that shows its relationship to sentence 4* and *Where should the writer place sentence 9?* For the first, read the sentence before and after, name the relationship in your own words, and only then look at the choices; the wrong answers are usually real transitions for the wrong relationship. For the second, find what the sentence refers back to and put it beside the sentence that introduces that idea. Reading questions also ask what a transition or a paragraph does in a passage: answer with its function in the line of reasoning, not with a label.

On **Section II**, the essay rubric's evidence-and-commentary row rewards a line of reasoning a reader can follow. Readers looking at a 4 in that row often see body paragraphs linked by sentences that name both the idea just proved and the idea coming next. A rhetorical analysis can also earn credit by explaining how a writer's organization serves the purpose, as you did with Bacon.
