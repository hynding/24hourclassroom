# Week 27 · Tone Through Irony in Swift's A Modest Proposal

Last week you watched Roosevelt fit his words to a frightened audience. This week you study how words, comparisons and sentence shapes create a tone, and how a tone can argue. The text is the most famous ironic essay in English, Jonathan Swift's *A Modest Proposal* (1729), in which a calm, reasonable gentleman recommends that the children of the Irish poor be sold and eaten. Nobody who reads it closely thinks Swift meant it. The question for skill 7.A is how the essay makes that clear, and how its tone turns a monstrous joke into one of the angriest arguments ever written.

## What you need to be able to do

- **7.A** Explain how word choice, comparisons and syntax contribute to the specific tone or style of a text. Name the tone precisely, point to the choice that makes it, and explain why that tone serves the argument.
- Spiral review: the rhetorical situation (1.A), the audience's knowledge (1.B and 2.B), implied thesis (3.B), and choosing words for a tone in your own writing (8.A), which next week makes its focus.

## The skill explained: how tone is made

**Tone** is the writer's attitude toward the subject and audience, as the words reveal it. In an argument, tone is not decoration. It tells readers how to feel about a claim and about the person making it. Skill 7.A names three sources of tone.

**Word choice.** Every word has a **denotation** (its dictionary meaning) and **connotations** (the associations it carries). *Mother* and *dam* can denote the same person, but *dam* is a word for a horse or a cow. Words also belong to a **register**, the kind of language suited to a setting: legal, scientific, culinary, casual, biblical. A register used outside its home creates a tone at once, solemn, comic or chilling.

**Comparisons.** Similes, metaphors, analogies and allusions carry the feelings attached to the thing compared. When a writer compares people to livestock, or a crisis to a war, the comparison imports an attitude.

**Syntax.** Sentence length, order and pattern shape tone too. Long cumulative sentences can sound exhaustive or overwhelming; short ones can sound blunt or final; repetition can sound insistent or ritual; balanced clauses can sound measured and judicious.

Precise **tone words** are half the battle. *Negative* and *serious* are almost useless. Try *mock-modest*, *clinical*, *detached*, *indignant*, *contemptuous*, *wry*, *deadpan*, *reverent*, *urgent*. Then prove the word with a choice.

One special case matters this week. **Verbal irony** says one thing and means another. **Satire** uses irony, exaggeration and understatement to expose and criticise a real target. A satirist often speaks through a **persona**, a character whose views are not the author's. The tone of the persona and the tone of the author can be opposite: Swift's proposer is calm and pleased with himself; Swift is furious.

## The text in context

Jonathan Swift (1667-1745), the author of *Gulliver's Travels*, was Dean of St Patrick's Cathedral in Dublin. In the late 1720s Ireland suffered failed harvests and famine. Much of the land was owned by landlords who lived in England and drew rents from it; English law restricted Irish trade; beggars crowded the roads. Swift had written practical pamphlets urging the Irish to buy Irish goods and the landlords to show mercy. They had changed nothing.

In 1729 he published *A Modest Proposal for Preventing the Children of Poor People from Being a Burthen to Their Parents or Country, and for Making Them Beneficial to the Publick*. Its form imitates a familiar genre: the pamphlet of a **projector**, a man with a scheme for public improvement, full of computed populations and confident promises of profit. Swift's readers, the Anglo-Irish gentry and the English who governed Ireland, knew the genre well. That knowledge is what lets the irony work. They recognise the voice of reasonable policy and then watch it calmly recommend cannibalism.

## Close reading

### 1. A sympathetic opening

The essay begins in genuine pity:

> It is a melancholy object to those, who walk through this great town, or travel in the country, when they see the streets, the roads and cabbin-doors crowded with beggars of the female sex, followed by three, four, or six children, all in rags, and importuning every passenger for an alms.

The tone is grave and compassionate, and the reader agrees with every word. The speaker then promises that whoever finds “a fair, cheap and easy method of making these children sound and useful members of the common-wealth” would deserve a statue. Nothing yet signals irony, which is the point: Swift wins our agreement in the voice of a decent reformer before the proposal arrives, so that the shock implicates us in the reasoning that led there.

### 2. The proposal in the voice of a cookbook

> I have been assured by a very knowing American of my acquaintance in London, that a young healthy child well nursed, is, at a year old, a most delicious nourishing and wholesome food, whether stewed, roasted, baked, or boiled; and I make no doubt that it will equally serve in a fricasie, or a ragoust.

Here the tone becomes the argument. The words are those of a household manual: *delicious*, *nourishing*, *wholesome*, a cheerful list of cooking methods, two fashionable French dishes. Their register is domestic and pleasant; their subject is a baby. The reader recoils precisely because the speaker does not. His calm is grotesque. Notice also the authority he cites, “a very knowing American of my acquaintance,” a vague acquaintance reported with complete confidence, mocking the way such pamphlets treated hearsay as evidence.

The mock-modest frame surrounds it all. The speaker will “humbly propose my own thoughts, which I hope will not be liable to the least objection,” and later “humbly offer it to publick consideration.” Courtesy and humility wrap the most objectionable proposal imaginable, and the gap between manner and matter is the engine of the irony.

Then the numbers. A child weighs “12 pounds” at birth and “28 pounds” at a year. The kingdom has a million and a half souls, of whom a hundred and twenty thousand children are born to the poor each year; twenty thousand are to be kept for breeding. Precise figures make the speaker sound rational and detached. They also show what happens when people are treated only as quantities.

### 3. Livestock and landlords

> I do therefore humbly offer it to publick consideration, that of the hundred and twenty thousand children, already computed, twenty thousand may be reserved for breed, whereof only one fourth part to be males; which is more than we allow to sheep, black cattle, or swine.

The comparison with livestock is delivered as if it were obvious. That casualness is the target. The speaker says aloud an attitude Swift believed the ruling class held in practice: that the Irish poor were stock to be managed for profit. The diction of breeding (*breeders*, *dam*, *reserved for breed*) runs through the essay and keeps the comparison alive.

Then a single word lets the mask slip:

> I grant this food will be somewhat dear, and therefore very proper for landlords, who, as they have already devoured most of the parents, seem to have the best title to the children.

*Devoured* works on two levels. In the scheme it is literal; about rents and evictions it is a metaphor, and an accusation. For a moment Swift's own anger shows through the persona's salesmanship, and the reader learns who the real cannibals are. Similar moments follow: the skin, “artificially dressed, will make admirable gloves for ladies, and summer boots for fine gentlemen,” which makes fashionable society the consumer of the poor.

### 4. The turn: the remedies no one may mention

Near the end the speaker dismisses alternatives in one of the most remarkable sentences in the essay. Here is a portion:

> Therefore let no man talk to me of other expedients: Of taxing our absentees at five shillings a pound: Of using neither cloaths, nor houshold furniture, except what is of our own growth and manufacture: Of utterly rejecting the materials and instruments that promote foreign luxury: Of curing the expensiveness of pride, vanity, idleness, and gaming in our women: Of introducing a vein of parsimony, prudence and temperance [...] Of teaching landlords to have at least one degree of mercy towards their tenants.

Look at the syntax. A colon, then phrase after phrase beginning with the same word, each a sensible reform: tax the landlords who live abroad, buy Irish goods, give up luxury, show mercy. The pretence is dismissal; the effect is an indictment. These are Swift's real proposals, the ones he had made before, and the repetition piles them up like charges in a courtroom. The tone moves from deadpan to barely restrained indignation. Then the speaker repeats himself: let no man talk of these remedies “'till he hath at least some glympse of hope, that there will ever be some hearty and sincere attempt to put them into practice.” The cannibal scheme turns out to be a way of shaming readers who had ignored the obvious.

The ending restores the mask. The speaker swears he has “not the least personal interest” in the plan, since he has no children young enough to sell. The final note of self-satisfied innocence leaves the outrage, again, for the reader to supply.

## From reading to writing: analysing tone, and keeping one

**Writing about tone.** A strong 7.A sentence has three parts: a choice, a precise tone, and a function.

> Weak: *Swift uses a lot of imagery about food, which is gross and makes his tone negative.*
>
> Stronger: *By describing infants in the cheerful language of recipes, Swift gives his proposer a calm, practical tone, and the reader's revulsion at that calm exposes how coldly Ireland's rulers already calculate the value of the poor.*

The stronger version names the choice (recipe diction), the tone (calm, practical), and what the tone does for the argument.

**Keeping a tone steady.** If you write satire yourself, the hardest part is not breaking character. Compare:

> Breaks the tone: *The cafeteria lines are so long that it's honestly ridiculous and somebody should do something.*
>
> Keeps it: *The administration has wisely ensured that the lunch line takes twenty-eight of our thirty minutes, sparing students the temptation to eat too quickly.*

The second version keeps the speaker approving and composed; the reader supplies the criticism. Notice, too, that it gives readers a signal (the absurd arithmetic) so that no one takes it literally. Irony is a bet on the audience; leave clues.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading the persona as the author.** Writing *Swift believes the poor should be eaten* fails the essay at once. Write *Swift's speaker* or *the proposer*.
- **Vague tone words.** *Serious*, *negative* and *sarcastic* rarely earn credit alone. Swift's speaker is not sarcastic; he is sincere, and that is what makes him horrifying.
- **Listing devices without tone.** *Swift uses numbers, French words and comparisons* is a list. Say what tone each creates and why.
- **Missing the shift.** The essay's tone changes in the expedients passage. A response that treats the whole as uniformly deadpan misses where Swift states his case.

## Exam connection

Multiple-choice questions on tone ask you to name an attitude (*The speaker's attitude toward the landlords is best described as...*) or to explain how a word or comparison contributes to the tone. Eliminate answers that confuse the persona with the author, or that choose a tone the passage's diction does not support. Irony passages appear often because they test exactly this distinction.

In the rhetorical analysis essay (Q2), tone is one of the richest sources of commentary, especially tone shifts. Sophistication credit often goes to essays that explain a tension: here, between the speaker's calm and the writer's fury, or between the sympathy of the opening and the horror that follows.
