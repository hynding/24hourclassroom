# Week 05 · Audience in Douglass's “What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?”

**CED skills:** 1.B Explain how an argument demonstrates understanding of an audience's beliefs, values, or needs · 2.B Demonstrate an understanding of an audience's beliefs, values, or needs
**Big Idea:** Rhetorical Situation (RHS)
**Read:** Frederick Douglass, “What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?” (oration at Rochester, July 5, 1852), in the appendix to *My Bondage and My Freedom* (1855), [Project Gutenberg #202](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/202)
**Spiral:** Unit 1 taught you to name the parts of the rhetorical situation (1.A), to find claims and evidence (3.A) and to build a paragraph on them (4.A). This week asks a harder question about one of those parts: not just *who* the audience is, but what the writer knew about them and how that knowledge shapes every choice.

## What you need to be able to do

- **1.B** Point to a specific choice in an argument (a premise, a piece of evidence, an allusion, a pronoun, a concession, a tone) and explain how it shows the writer's understanding of what the audience believes, values or needs.
- **2.B** In your own writing, make choices that show the same understanding: start from a value you share with your reader, choose evidence your reader trusts, answer the objection your reader will raise, and keep a tone that keeps your reader reading.

In plain language: by Friday you should be able to say *Douglass knew his listeners revered the Declaration of Independence, so he made the Declaration his evidence against them*, and then prove it with a short quotation.

## The skill: audience as beliefs, values and needs

In Unit 1 you learned to identify the audience as one part of the rhetorical situation. That was skill 1.A, and it ends with a name: *the audience is the crowd in Rochester*. Skill 1.B begins where 1.A ends. It asks what the writer knew about that crowd, and how the text shows it.

Three words in the skill's wording do most of the work:

- **Beliefs** are what the audience holds to be true. Douglass's listeners believed that the Declaration of Independence announced universal principles of liberty.
- **Values** are what the audience holds to be good or important. They valued patriotism, Christian faith, gratitude, and their own sense of themselves as friends of freedom.
- **Needs** are what the audience wants or must have. A holiday crowd needs to feel that its celebration is deserved; a school board needs a proposal it can afford and defend.

A writer who understands an audience does not simply tell it what it wants to hear. That is **pandering**, and it usually means dropping the claim. Understanding an audience means keeping the claim and finding the route by which *this* audience can be brought to accept it. The route is built from the audience's own materials: its documents, its scripture, its fears, its pride, its stated goals.

How do you recognise 1.B in a text? Look for these signs:

1. **Shared premises.** The argument starts from something the audience already accepts.
2. **Chosen evidence.** The examples, authorities and facts are ones this audience trusts.
3. **Anticipated objections.** The writer voices what the audience is likely to think, then answers it.
4. **Calibrated tone.** The writer sounds the way this audience needs to hear the claim: sorrowful, respectful, urgent, plain.
5. **Pronouns and address.** Who is *we*? Who is *you*? When does the writer move from one to the other?

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Primary (immediate) audience | The people directly addressed: the listeners in the hall, the readers of the letter. |
| Secondary (extended) audience | Readers or listeners beyond the immediate one: readers of the printed speech, the nation, later generations. |
| Beliefs | What the audience holds to be true. |
| Values | What the audience holds to be good or important. |
| Needs | What the audience wants or must have in order to act. |
| Common ground | A belief or value writer and audience share, used as a premise. |
| Shared premise | A starting point the audience already accepts, so that the conclusion follows from its own beliefs. |
| Anticipated objection | An objection the writer voices on the audience's behalf in order to answer it. |
| Pandering | Telling an audience what it wants to hear at the expense of the claim. |
| Allusion | A reference to a text or event the audience knows, borrowing its authority. |

## The text in context

Frederick Douglass escaped slavery in Maryland in 1838. By 1852 he was the most famous Black orator in the United States, the author of the *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass* (1845), and the editor of his own newspaper in Rochester, New York. The Rochester Ladies' Anti-Slavery Society invited him to give an Independence Day address. The Fourth fell on a Sunday that year, so he spoke on Monday, July 5, in Corinthian Hall.

His audience was several hundred people, most of them white Northerners, many of them already opposed to slavery. That is the first thing to understand about them: they were not enemies. They had come to hear an abolitionist. But they were also proud Americans at a patriotic celebration, and many of them thought of slavery as a Southern problem. Two years earlier Congress had passed the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, which required citizens of free states like New York to help return people who had escaped from slavery. The North was implicated whether its citizens liked it or not.

The exigence, then, is a collision: a nation celebrating liberty while holding millions in bondage, and an invitation asking a man who had been enslaved to join the celebration. After a modest opening, the full speech praises the founders and the Declaration at length and with sincerity. Then it turns. The extract Douglass reprinted in *My Bondage and My Freedom* begins at that turn, and it is the part we read most closely.

The speech was printed as a pamphlet the same year, so it also had a **secondary audience**: readers across the country who never sat in Corinthian Hall.

## Close reading

### 1. Questions that make the audience answer

> Fellow-Citizens--Pardon me, and allow me to ask, why am I called upon to speak here to-day? What have I, or those I represent, to do with your national independence?

**Observe.** Douglass addresses his listeners as “Fellow-Citizens” and then asks a polite question (“Pardon me”) that is anything but polite in its implications. The pronoun is already shifting: *your* national independence.

**Interpret.** The questions are rhetorical. No one in the hall is meant to answer aloud, but every listener answers silently, and the honest answer is uncomfortable: the Declaration's principles were not extended to the enslaved.

**Function (1.B).** Douglass knows his audience believes the holiday belongs to everyone. Instead of contradicting that belief, he asks questions that make them test it. A conclusion an audience reaches for itself is harder to reject than one it is told.

### 2. Sharing the value before refusing it

> Would to God, both for your sakes and ours, that an affirmative answer could be truthfully returned to these questions! Then would my task be light, and my burden easy and delightful.

**Observe.** “Would to God” is a wish. “For your sakes and ours” includes the audience in the loss. Then come three more questions, each describing someone so “cold,” “obdurate” or “selfish” that he would refuse to be grateful for freedom, and a short answer: “I am not that man.”

**Interpret.** Douglass is about to refuse to celebrate. A patriotic audience might hear that refusal as ingratitude. So first he shows that he values gratitude as much as they do; he would celebrate if he could.

**Function (1.B).** The paragraph protects the speaker's relationship with his listeners. When the refusal comes, it reads as forced on him by the facts, not as contempt for the occasion or for them.

### 3. The pronouns draw a line

> The sunlight that brought life and healing to you, has brought stripes and death to me. This Fourth of July is *yours*, not mine. You may rejoice, I must mourn.

**Observe.** The sentences are built as pairs: life and healing against stripes and death, rejoice against mourn, *you* against *me*. The printed text italicises “yours.”

**Interpret.** Until this moment, a holiday crowd would have heard *we* and *our* as including everyone in the room. Douglass splits the pronouns.

**Function (1.B).** The audience feels, in the grammar of the sentences, the exclusion he is describing. This works because Douglass knows what the audience assumes about *we*; he uses that assumption to make the line visible.

### 4. Scripture as shared ground

> How can we sing the Lord’s song in a strange land?

**Observe.** Douglass quotes Psalm 137, in which the Israelites, captive in Babylon, are asked by their captors to sing the songs of Zion.

**Interpret.** The parallel is exact. Douglass, whose people are held captive, has been asked to sing on the nation's holiday.

**Function (1.B).** A churchgoing audience in 1852 knew this psalm well. Douglass borrows its authority and makes his listeners recognise themselves in a role they would never choose: the Babylonians. Then he names his subject outright: “My subject, then, fellow-citizens, is AMERICAN SLAVERY.” The capitals mark the stress; the word “then” presents the subject as the only honest conclusion from everything before it.

Notice, finally, what Douglass does *not* do. He does not soften his claim. America, he says, is “false to the past, false to the present,” and his tone grows fiercer as the speech goes on. Later he even voices the objection his listeners are likely to hold: “But I fancy I hear some one of my audience say” that abolitionists should “argue more, and denounce less.” Understanding the audience shapes his route, never his destination.

## From reading to writing: writing for a skeptical reader (2.B)

On the exam, skill 2.B appears in two places. In Section I, revision questions name an audience and ask which version of a sentence best serves it. In Section II, the argument essay rewards a writer who seems to know who might disagree and why.

Here is a weak opening to a letter asking a school board to move the start of the school day later:

> *I think school starts way too early and it needs to change. Everyone is tired all the time. Maybe the board just doesn't care about how students feel.*

The claim is there, but nothing shows any understanding of the board. *Everyone is tired* is a complaint, not evidence the board can weigh. The last sentence accuses the very people whose votes the writer needs.

Here is a revision:

> *Like every member of this board, I want our students to arrive at school ready to learn. That shared goal is why I am asking you to move our start time from 7:15 to 8:30 next fall. I know the board's first question will be cost, so let me begin there: two neighbouring districts made this change without raising bus costs, by reordering routes so that elementary schools start first.*

What changed:

1. **Common ground first.** The opening names a goal the board certainly holds.
2. **The concern answered early.** The board has said that money is its main worry, so the revision answers that worry right after the request.
3. **Evidence the audience trusts.** A comparable district's experience is the kind of fact a board can check.
4. **Tone that keeps the reader.** The accusation is gone; firmness remains.

Notice that the claim did not get weaker. The revision still asks for a later start. What changed is the route.

## Common mistakes

- **Stopping at 1.A.** *The audience is the people in Rochester* is identification, not explanation. Go on to say what they believed and how the text uses it.
- **Confusing understanding with flattery.** Douglass praises the founders, but the praise is a premise he uses, not a compliment he pays.
- **Treating the audience as hostile.** Douglass's listeners were largely antislavery. The challenge was their complacency and pride, not their opposition.
- **Naming a device without the audience.** *Douglass uses rhetorical questions* earns little. *Douglass's questions make listeners who assume the holiday includes everyone test that assumption* earns credit.
- **Pandering in your own writing.** Softening your claim until no one could disagree leaves you with nothing to argue.
- **Insulting the reader you need.** In a revision question, the sentence that mocks or blames the audience is almost always the one to delete.

## The exam connection

In Section I, reading questions often ask why a writer includes a detail *given the audience*, or which choice *most clearly reflects the writer's awareness of the audience*. Writing questions say things like *The writer wants to appeal to an audience of parents who worry about safety. Which choice best accomplishes this goal?* The answer is the version that serves the named audience's stated values or needs, even when another version sounds more impressive.

In Section II, the rhetorical analysis essay almost always rewards a student who connects the writer's choices to the audience. A thesis such as *Douglass turns his audience's patriotism and faith into evidence against them* gives you a line of reasoning for the whole essay. In the argument essay, showing that you understand who might disagree with you is part of what earns the sophistication point.
