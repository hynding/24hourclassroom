## What this week is for

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Counterargument | A claim that opposes the writer's position, stated within the argument so it can be answered. |
| Alternative perspective | A different way of seeing the issue, not necessarily opposed, that complicates the writer's claim. |
| Concession | Granting that part of an opposing view is true or reasonable. |
| Rebuttal | The writer's answer to a counterargument: why the claim still stands. |
| Refutation | A rebuttal that shows the opposing claim is false. |
| Qualifier | A word or phrase that limits a claim's scope or certainty. |
| Straw man | A distorted, weaker version of an opposing view, set up to be knocked down. |
| Attribution tag | A phrase that marks whose view is being stated (*critics argue*, *such reasoners may say*). |
| Burden of proof | The obligation, usually on the person making a claim, to support it. |

Two distinctions matter most. A **concession** gives ground; a **rebuttal** takes it back. And a **rebuttal** may simply argue that an objection matters less than the writer's reasons, while a **refutation** argues that the objection is wrong. Both are legitimate; they make different promises to the reader.

## The text in context

John Stuart Mill (1806–1873) was an English philosopher, economist and member of Parliament. *On Liberty* appeared in 1859, written in close partnership with Harriet Taylor Mill, who had died the year before. Its question is how far society, whether through laws or through the pressure of public opinion, may rightly control an individual. Chapter 2 takes up the freedom to think and speak.

**Exigence.** Victorian Britain had no official censor of books, but Mill saw a quieter danger: a public opinion so confident of its own beliefs that people with unpopular views kept silent. Laws against blasphemy were still occasionally enforced.

**Audience.** The educated British reading public, many of whom believed that some opinions (atheism, for example) were too dangerous to spread. Mill had to persuade readers who were inclined to agree with the people he was arguing against.

**Purpose.** To show that silencing any opinion, true or false, harms everyone, including the silencers.

That audience explains the chapter's method. Mill cannot simply announce that censorship is wrong. His readers already have reasons for thinking some suppression is wise, and they will not abandon those reasons until they hear them stated and answered.

## Close reading

### 1. The argument the objection attacks

> We can never be sure that the opinion we are endeavouring to stifle is a false opinion; and if we were sure, stifling it would be an evil still.

**Observe.** One sentence, two halves joined by a semicolon. The second half begins with *if we were sure*, which grants, for argument's sake, the opponents' best case.

**Interpret.** Mill qualifies his own claim by anticipating the reply to it. Even if certainty were possible, he says, his position would hold. That sets up the structure of the chapter: one branch for the case in which the silenced opinion is true, another for the case in which it is false.

**Function.** Before any objection is voiced, the sentence shows a writer who has thought about the other side. The structure of the whole chapter is a concession and a refusal: *suppose you are right about the facts; you are still wrong about the conclusion.*

### 2. The objection, at full strength

> The objection likely to be made to this argument, would probably take some such form as the following. There is no greater assumption of infallibility in forbidding the propagation of error, than in any other thing which is done by public authority on its own judgment and responsibility. Judgment is given to men that they may use it. Because it may be used erroneously, are men to be told that they ought not to use it at all?

**Observe.** Mill labels the paragraph as an objection, then speaks in its voice. The objection has a rhetorical question, a confident principle and, later, an analogy to taxes and wars. It even has its own maxim: “An objection which applies to all conduct, can be no valid objection to any conduct in particular.”

**Interpret.** This is not a straw man. Mill gives the other side its best arguments and its best phrasing. A reader who favours suppression would recognise every step as his own reasoning, perhaps better put than he could put it.

**Function.** The paragraph is the reason the next one works. A rebuttal of a weak objection proves nothing; a rebuttal of the strongest objection proves a great deal. Notice the small tag in the middle of the paragraph, “(such reasoners may say),” which keeps the reader aware of whose voice this is, even when the objection slips into *we*.

### 3. The answer

> I answer that it is assuming very much more. There is the greatest difference between presuming an opinion to be true, because, with every opportunity for contesting it, it has not been refuted, and assuming its truth for the purpose of not permitting its refutation.

**Observe.** After a paragraph of nearly four hundred words, the reply opens with nine. It picks up the objection's last verb, *assume*, and turns it around.

**Interpret.** Mill grants a premise and attacks an inference. He agrees that people must act on opinions that might be wrong. What he denies is that silencing is just another such act. A government that taxes still lets you argue against the tax; a government that silences you removes the argument itself. Confidence that has survived open challenge is earned. Confidence used to forbid challenge is not.

**Function.** This is a refutation, not merely a rebuttal: Mill shows that the opponents' analogy fails at a precise point. The brevity matters too. The long objection followed by a short answer creates a tone of calm confidence, which is itself persuasive.

### 4. Why the other side matters at all

> He who knows only his own side of the case, knows little of that. His reasons may be good, and no one may have been able to refute them. But if he is equally unable to refute the reasons on the opposite side; if he does not so much as know what they are, he has no ground for preferring either opinion.

**Observe.** A short, aphoristic sentence, then a concession (*His reasons may be good*), then the turn on *But*.

**Interpret.** Mill applies his method to himself. The passage concedes that a one-sided believer may be right and then argues that being right by accident is not the same as knowing. A few lines later he goes further: he says we must hear objections “from persons who actually believe them; who defend them in earnest, and do their very utmost for them.”

**Function.** This passage is Mill's theory of the counterargument. It explains why the objection paragraph exists. For your purposes it is also a rule for the exam: the student who knows the other side writes the more convincing essay.

## Writing lesson: stating the other side fairly

The first job in a counterargument paragraph is to state the objection so that its holders would accept the statement. Compare three versions for an essay arguing that a town should build a skate park.

> *Weak:* Some people are just against fun and don't want teenagers to have anywhere to go.

This is a straw man. It assigns a silly motive to the opposition, concedes nothing, and invites the reader who has a real objection to stop reading.

> *Better:* Some residents oppose the park because of cost.

Accurate but thin. It names the objection without giving it any force, so the rebuttal that follows will seem easy and unearned.

> *Strong:* Residents who oppose the park point out that the town is already deferring repairs on two playgrounds, and they reasonably ask why a new facility should come first. That concern deserves an answer, and the answer is in the numbers: the park's construction would be paid for by a state recreation grant that cannot be spent on repairs.

The strong version states the objection in specific terms (deferred repairs), grants that it is reasonable, and then answers it with a reason the opponents would have to address. It follows Mill's pattern: full statement, concession, precise reply.

A checklist for the statement of an objection:

1. **Attribute it.** Use a tag: *critics argue*, *residents who oppose the plan point out*.
2. **Make it specific.** A real objection has a reason attached.
3. **Give it its best form.** If you can make it stronger, do.
4. **Grant what is true.** Even one clause (*that concern is reasonable*) changes the tone.
5. **Turn clearly.** *But*, *yet*, *even so*, *still*. The reader must see where the concession ends.

## Common mistakes

- **Mistaking the opponent's voice for the writer's.** Readers of Mill sometimes quote a sentence from the objection as if it were his view. Check for attribution tags and for where the reply begins.
- **Calling every counterargument a refutation.** A writer who says an objection matters less than her own reasons is rebutting, not refuting.
- **Treating a concession as weakness.** A concession that narrows the dispute usually makes the claim harder to attack.
- **Answering a straw man.** If you would be embarrassed to read your summary of the objection aloud to someone who holds it, rewrite it.
- **Dismissing instead of answering.** *They are wrong* is a verdict, not a rebuttal.

## Exam connection

On Section I, reading passages regularly include a sentence in which the writer anticipates a reply; questions ask what the sentence “primarily serves to” do, or how a paragraph “functions” in the argument. Look for attribution tags and concession signals. On the revision questions, expect stems such as *The writer wants to acknowledge an opposing view before answering it* or *Which choice best qualifies the claim in sentence 1?* On the argument essay (Q3), answering a counterargument well is one of the clearest routes to the sophistication point, which rewards a nuanced argument that explores the complexities and tensions of the issue. The same move strengthens a synthesis essay, where one of the six sources nearly always disagrees with the others.

## Self-check

1. In your own words, state the objection Mill imagines in two sentences, as fairly as he does.
2. What exactly does Mill grant the opponents, and what exactly does he deny?
3. Is Mill's reply a rebuttal, a refutation or both? Explain.
4. Find the attribution tag in the objection paragraph and explain why Mill needs it.
5. Rewrite the weak skate-park sentence so that it states the objection fairly, then add a one-sentence answer.
