## What this week is for

Last week Ida B. Wells showed how a writer's perspective shapes the evidence she chooses. This week we look at the other place a writer's attitude shows: the **tone** of the prose itself. By the end of the week you should be able to:

- **8.A** Choose a tone for your own argument that suits its audience and purpose, and revise words, comparisons and sentences to achieve it.

In plain language: by Friday you should be able to say *Twain sounds politely puzzled while he is being merciless, and that gap is how he wins*, and then point to the exact words that prove it.

## The skill: tone is evidence of attitude

**Tone** is a writer's attitude toward the subject or the audience, made visible in the writing. You never see tone directly; you infer it from three kinds of choices, the same three the CED names:

1. **Word choice (diction).** Denotation is what a word means; connotation is what it suggests. *Offence*, *defect*, *weakness* and *slip* all denote a fault, but each carries a different judgement.
2. **Comparisons.** Similes, metaphors and analogies carry attitude. Calling a novelist's imagination a horse is an insult; calling it a clothes-horse is a worse one.
3. **Syntax.** Sentence length, order and structure. A long sentence that builds and a short one that lands can work like the setup and punchline of a joke.

Two rules for describing tone. First, **be precise**. *Positive*, *negative* and *emotional* explain nothing; *scathing*, *mock-solemn*, *wry*, *indignant*, *measured* and *rueful* do. Second, **tone can be double**. Some of the best writing is courteous on the surface and contemptuous underneath, and the gap is the point. When a sentence's manner and meaning diverge, name both.

A **tone shift** is a change in attitude within a passage. Look for turn words (*but*, *yet*, *still*), a change in sentence length, a sudden question, or a move from plain words to formal ones. When you find a shift, name the tone on each side and explain what the writer gains by turning there.

## The text in context

James Fenimore Cooper (1789–1851) was one of the first American novelists to win an international reputation. His Leatherstocking Tales, frontier adventures that include *The Last of the Mohicans* (1826), *The Pathfinder* (1840) and *The Deerslayer* (1841), were treated in the late nineteenth century as founding classics of American literature, praised by professors and novelists alike.

Mark Twain (Samuel Clemens, 1835–1910) belonged to a different literary generation. His fame rested on comic writing and on the realistic, vernacular voice of *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* (1884), and he disliked the romantic fiction Cooper stood for: noble heroes, improbable rescues and characters who speak like books. In July 1895 he published "Fenimore Cooper's Literary Offences" in the *North American Review*, a serious monthly read by educated Americans.

The **exigence** is placed on the first page. Twain opens with three epigraphs: praise of *The Pathfinder* and *The Deerslayer* from Thomas Lounsbury, a professor at Yale; from Brander Matthews, a professor at Columbia; and from the English novelist Wilkie Collins, who called Cooper *the greatest artist in the domain of romantic fiction yet produced by America.* The **audience** is readers who had accepted that verdict. The **purpose** is to puncture it, and Twain's chief tool is tone.

A note on perspective. Twain mocks Cooper's Native American characters as unrealistic, but some of his own jokes about them repeat the dismissive attitudes of his era. A writer can expose one distortion while carrying another. Noticing both is exactly the kind of reading this unit asks of you.

## Close readings

### 1. Courteous on the surface

> It seems to me that it was far from right for the Professor of English Literature in Yale, the Professor of English Literature in Columbia, and Wilkie Collins to deliver opinions on Cooper's literature without having read some of it. It would have been much more decorous to keep silent and let persons talk who have read Cooper.

**Diction.** *It seems to me*, *far from right* and *more decorous* belong to polite disagreement. Nothing here is loud.

**Meaning.** The charge underneath is brutal: the only possible explanation for the professors' praise is that they never read the books. The formal titles (*the Professor of English Literature in Yale*) make the targets sound grand, which makes the implied failure funnier.

**Tone.** Politely insulting, or mock-courteous. The gap between manner and meaning is the tone, and it sets up a persona Twain keeps throughout: a reasonable reader, slightly pained, who has done the reading.

### 2. Understatement, then a record score

> Cooper's art has some defects. In one place in 'Deerslayer,' and in the restricted space of two-thirds of a page, Cooper has scored 114 offences against literary art out of a possible 115. It breaks the record.

**Understatement.** *Some defects* says far less than Twain means; it is a setup.

**Hyperbole as mock precision.** The next sentence swings to the opposite extreme. *114 out of a possible 115* parodies objective measurement, as if literary art had a scorecard, and *offences* turns a book review into a criminal charge.

**Syntax.** After a long, carefully qualified sentence comes a four-word one: *It breaks the record.* The short sentence lands like a punchline. The shape of the passage (small claim, enormous claim, flat verdict) is the shape of the joke, and of the argument.

### 3. Laws of fiction

> 3. They require that the personages in a tale shall be alive, except in the case of corpses, and that always the reader shall be able to tell the corpses from the others. But this detail has often been overlooked in the Deerslayer tale.

**Mock-legal form.** Numbered rules that *require* things of a tale are the language of statutes. The form treats taste as law, so Cooper can be found guilty.

**Deadpan.** *Except in the case of corpses* is a solemn legal exception to an absurdly obvious rule. The joke depends on the flat delivery, and its implication is sharp: Cooper's living characters are so lifeless that a reader may confuse them with the dead.

The minor rules work the same way. *Use the right word, not its second cousin* is a comic comparison and sound advice at once, and *Eschew surplusage* is a pompous way to say *cut extra words*: a joke about formal style inside a rule about style.

### 4. The final turn

> I may be mistaken, but it does seem to me that Deerslayer is not a work of art in any sense; it does seem to me that it is destitute of every detail that goes to the making of a work of art; in truth, it seems to me that Deerslayer is just simply a literary delirium tremens.
>
> [...]
>
> Counting these out, what is left is Art. I think we must all admit that.

**Mock modesty.** *I may be mistaken* and the triple *it seems to me* sound tentative, but each hedge introduces a harsher claim, ending in the image of the book as a drunkard's hallucination. The hedges are a pose.

**Catalogue and chiasmus.** Between these paragraphs comes a breathless list of everything the novel lacks (*no invention ... no order, system, sequence, or result*), including a crossed pair, *its humor is pathetic; its pathos is funny*, which shows Cooper's effects arriving backwards.

**The shift.** Then the tone drops into deadpan concession. With every element of a novel *counted out*, nothing is left, so calling the remainder *Art* is irony, and it hands Lounsbury's phrase, a *pure work of art*, back to him as a punchline. The essay ends where it began: with the authorities, now answered.

## Tone as argument

Twain never writes *Cooper is overrated, and here is why.* He does not need to. The tone makes the argument for him in three ways:

- **It positions the reader.** Laughing with Twain means agreeing with him; the reader who still admires Cooper is left alone with the professors who did not read him.
- **It makes the writer seem reasonable.** The courteous, hesitant persona suggests that the verdict was forced on him by the evidence.
- **It depends on evidence.** Every joke points to something checkable: a stream that changes width, a nail-head no one could see at a hundred yards, a twig in every scene. Comedy without evidence would be mere insult; comedy with it is proof that is pleasant to read.

That last point matters for your own writing. Tone persuades only when it rests on substance.

## From reading to writing: choosing and controlling tone

Skill 8.A asks you to use words, comparisons and syntax to create a tone *on purpose*. The first decision is not *what tone do I feel?* but *what tone will move the reader I most need to persuade?* Indignation can rally readers who already agree; it often hardens those who do not. Sarcasm almost always backfires in a student argument: it tells undecided readers that anyone who disagrees is a fool, and they may suspect they are the fools in question. Twain gets away with it because he is a famous comic writer with pages of evidence. You are writing for a reader you need to win.

Here is a weak sentence from an invented letter to a school board about start times:

> *Apparently the board thinks teenagers just love waking up at 5:45 in the dark, which is truly brilliant.*

The tone is sarcastic, and its target is the reader. Now three revisions, each using a different lever:

- **Word choice and fact.** *The current schedule asks students to wake at 5:45, hours before their bodies are ready to work.* The time carries the force; no word mocks anyone.
- **Comparison.** *Asking a teenager to concentrate at 7:15 is like asking an adult to sit an exam at 4:30 in the morning.* The analogy invites adult readers into the students' experience instead of scolding them for not sharing it.
- **Syntax.** *Students wake at 5:45. They are in class by 7:15. Most of them could not fall asleep before eleven.* Three short, flat sentences make the arithmetic do the arguing. This is controlled indignation: the facts are stark, the voice is calm.

Two more habits. First, **prefer a precise detail to an intensifier**. *My first class is a total disaster* is loud and vague; *most mornings, several students in my first-period class fall asleep before the bell* is quiet and much more serious. Second, **place the key word at the end** of the sentence, where stress naturally falls: *The board has postponed this decision three times* puts the weight on *three times*.

Irony has its place, especially in a humorous column or an editorial answering a claim that deserves ridicule. Compare two invented sentences:

> *The council postponed the vote on the new library for the fourth time.*
>
> *The council, apparently in no hurry, has now postponed the library vote four times.*

One aside, *apparently in no hurry*, turns a report into wry criticism. Small choices shift tone more reliably than loud ones.

## Common mistakes

- **Vague tone words.** *The tone is negative* or *the author is emotional* earns nothing. Name the attitude precisely and support it.
- **Naming a device without its effect.** *Twain uses hyperbole* is an observation; *the absurdly exact score of 114 out of 115 parodies objective measurement while insisting the faults are countable* is analysis.
- **Missing the double tone.** If you call Twain's opening *polite*, you have described only its surface. Name the gap.
- **Taking irony literally.** *Counting these out, what is left is Art* is not praise.
- **Confusing tone with mood.** AP Language asks about the writer's attitude, not your feelings while reading.
- **Using sarcasm in your own argument.** It almost always costs more readers than it wins.

## Exam connection

On the multiple-choice section, style questions (skill 7.A) often read: *The tone of the passage is best described as...*; *The shift in the third paragraph is from ... to ...*; *The phrase X contributes to the passage's tone primarily by...* For a two-part tone answer (*from A to B*), check both halves against the text; distractors often get one half right.

Writing questions on skill 8.A give you a student draft and ask which revision *best maintains the tone of the passage* or *best conveys the writer's concern*. Eliminate options that are louder, vaguer or more sarcastic than the draft's purpose allows.

In Section II, the **rhetorical analysis essay** (Q2) frequently rewards a student who traces a tone shift and explains why the writer turns where they do; that kind of attention to complexity is one route to the sophistication point. In the **argument essay** (Q3), a controlled, precise tone makes your reasoning more credible to the reader the rubric imagines: an intelligent person who has not yet agreed with you.
