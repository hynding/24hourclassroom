## What you need to be able to do

- **7.A** Point to specific words, comparisons and sentence patterns in a passage, name the tone or style they create, and explain how that tone serves the writer's purpose.

In plain language: you should be able to write *Johnson's long, accumulating sentence imitates the pile of excuses it describes, so his warning sounds grave but not cold*, and then defend it with a quotation.

## The skill explained: three sources of tone

**Tone** is the writer's attitude toward the subject and the audience. It is not the subject itself: a writer can be playful about death or grave about a dinner party. Tone is built from three kinds of choices, which the CED names directly.

**1. Word choice (diction).** Every word has a *denotation*, its dictionary meaning, and *connotations*, the associations and judgments it carries. “Writers,” “journalists” and “scribblers” denote roughly the same people; only the last one sneers. Diction also has a register. Long words from Latin (*procrastination, pertinacious, remonstrance*) tend to sound formal, abstract and weighty. Short words from Old English (*act, fear, sleep, lie*) tend to sound plain and concrete. A writer who moves between the two can make a plain word land with force.

**2. Comparisons.** Similes, metaphors, analogies, personification and allusions. A comparison brings a whole field of associations with it. Call procrastination an enemy and the reader imagines a war; call it a disease and the reader imagines a cure. A 7.A answer says what the comparison implies, not just that it exists.

**3. Syntax.** The arrangement of words into sentences: their length, their order and their patterns.

| Pattern | What it is | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|
| Parallelism | The same grammatical form repeated across a series | Shows the items belong together; builds rhythm or climax |
| Antithesis | Opposites in parallel form | Sharpens a contrast; sounds like a maxim |
| Balanced sentence | Two halves of matching structure | Measured, judicious, authoritative |
| Periodic sentence | Main clause held back to the end | Suspense; weight on the final words |
| Cumulative sentence | Main clause first, then added phrases | Expansive; piles up detail or examples |
| Short sentence after long ones | A sudden change of length | Emphasis; a verdict after deliberation |
| Rhetorical question | A question asked for effect | Invites the reader to supply the obvious answer |

**Naming a tone.** Vague words lose points: “positive,” “negative,” “emotional.” Precise words earn them: *grave, admonitory, earnest, wry, sardonic, scornful, mock-solemn, sympathetic, incredulous.* Often the best answer joins two: *grave but sympathetic; amused yet contemptuous.* If you can say what each half of the pair comes from, you have the start of a strong paragraph.

## The text in context

**Samuel Johnson** (1709-1784) was the son of a provincial bookseller who came to London poor and spent years writing for hire before his *Dictionary of the English Language* (1755) made him the most famous man of letters in England. He was also an essayist, poet, critic and biographer, and he talked as brilliantly as he wrote; James Boswell's *Life of Johnson* (1791) records the conversation.

**The Rambler** was Johnson's own periodical, published twice a week from 1750 to 1752. He wrote almost all of its 208 essays himself, often at the last minute. Its subjects are moral (hope, envy, ambition, the use of time) and its audience was the growing reading public of London, who bought single sheets to read and discuss. Its style is grave, Latinate and carefully balanced. **No. 134** opens with a confession: Johnson sat down to write, could not decide on a subject, let his mind wander, and was finally “awakened from this dream of study by a summons from the press.” The essay he then wrote is about procrastination.

**The Idler** was a series of 103 essays Johnson contributed to a London weekly newspaper between 1758 and 1760. They are shorter and lighter than the Ramblers, and often ironic. **No. 30** was written during the Seven Years' War, as newspapers multiplied to feed a public hungry for news of battles. Johnson's exigence was what he saw as a flood of invented or slanted war reporting.

The two essays make an ideal pair for 7.A: the same writer, the same habits of sentence-building, and two very different attitudes. Comparing them shows that tone is not a fixed personality but a set of choices made for a purpose.

## Close reading

### 1. Diction that dignifies a small habit

> The folly of allowing ourselves to delay what we know cannot be finally escaped, is one of the general weaknesses, which, in spite of the instruction of moralists, and the remonstrances of reason, prevail to a greater or less degree in every mind

**Observe.** “Folly,” “weaknesses,” “moralists,” “remonstrances”: the vocabulary of moral philosophy, mostly Latinate and abstract. Notice also the paired phrases, “the instruction of moralists, and the remonstrances of reason,” each built as *the + noun + of + noun.*

**Interpret.** Johnson treats putting things off as a serious failing of character, the kind moralists and reason have argued against for centuries.

**Function.** The elevated diction raises the subject. A reader who might have laughed off delay as a quirk is made to see it as a weakness worth an essay. The balanced pair gives the sentence a judicious rhythm: this is a writer weighing, not ranting.

### 2. A comparison that turns delay into an enemy

> even they who most steadily withstand it, find it, if not the most violent, the most pertinacious of their passions, always renewing its attacks, and though often vanquished, never destroyed.

**Observe.** “Attacks,” “vanquished,” “destroyed”: the language of war. “Pertinacious” means stubbornly persistent.

**Function.** Personified as a foe that returns after every defeat, procrastination becomes something to be fought daily for life. The comparison supports the tone of earnest warning. It also flatters the reader a little: even the steadiest people must fight this enemy, so there is no shame in admitting you face it.

### 3. Syntax that enacts its meaning

> Thus life is languished away in the gloom of anxiety, and consumed in collecting resolutions which the next morning dissipates; in forming purposes which we scarcely hope to keep, and reconciling ourselves to our own cowardice by excuses, which, while we admit them, we know to be absurd.

**Observe.** One long cumulative sentence. The main clause comes first (“life is languished away”), and then phrases keep arriving: “in collecting,” “in forming,” “reconciling.” Note the alliteration of “life is languished” and the plain, bitter word at the end: “absurd.”

**Interpret.** The procrastinator's life is a cycle of resolving and failing to act.

**Function.** The sentence's shape imitates its content. Each new phrase is another resolution made and broken, so the reader experiences the drag of the cycle. The word “we” inside the series keeps Johnson among the guilty, which is why the tone is grave rather than contemptuous. The paragraph's first word, “Thus,” is a transition from week 16: it tells us this is the consequence of the false comfort described just before.

### 4. Antithesis, and an allusion that sharpens a judgment

> To act is far easier than to suffer; yet we every day see the progress of life retarded by the *vis inertiae*, the mere repugnance to motion

**Observe.** A short antithesis, two infinitives balanced across “is far easier than.” Then “yet” and a Latin term from physics, *vis inertiae* (the force of inertia), glossed in plain English.

**Function.** After long sentences, the short maxim sounds like a verdict. The physics term makes idleness sound like a natural force that must be deliberately overcome, which is both a comparison and a quiet joke.

A few lines later Johnson turns to myth. Tantalus, punished with fruit that retreated whenever he reached for it, “was somewhat to be pitied.” Then the rhetorical question: “what tenderness can be claimed by those who, though perhaps they suffer the pains of Tantalus, will never lift their hands for their own relief?” The allusion works by difference. Tantalus could not reach; the idle will not reach. The comparison withdraws the sympathy Johnson has extended so far and ends the passage on reproach.

### 5. The same tools, used for scorn

Now Idler No. 30. Johnson borrows a famous joke by the diplomat Sir Henry Wotton, which he gives as a definition of an ambassador: “a man of virtue sent abroad to tell lies for the advantage of his country.” Johnson's news-writer is “a man without virtue, who writes lies at home for his own profit.”

**Observe.** The same structure, with each part inverted: virtue becomes its absence, abroad becomes home, country becomes self.

**Function.** Parallelism here is a weapon. Because the two definitions share a shape, every difference counts against the news-writer, who is worse than a licensed liar at each step. The tone is scornful wit.

The next sentence completes the attack:

> To these compositions is required neither genius nor knowledge, neither industry nor sprightliness; but contempt of shame and indifference to truth are absolutely necessary.

The sentence wears the solemn clothes of a list of job requirements and fills them with vices. “Absolutely necessary,” said gravely of shamelessness, is mock solemnity. The essay ends with a balanced comparison that places writers beside looters: Johnson does not know whether more is to be feared “from streets filled with soldiers accustomed to plunder, or from garrets filled with scribblers accustomed to lie.” The parallel “accustomed to plunder ... accustomed to lie” makes the two equal.

Compare the two essays. Both use Latinate diction and balanced syntax. The difference is in the pronouns and the irony. In the Rambler, “we” share the fault and the balance sounds like moral reflection. In the Idler, “they” are held at a distance and the balance sounds like an indictment.

## Writing lesson: writing about style

Here is a weak analytical sentence about Johnson.

> *Johnson uses a lot of diction and syntax to create a tone in his essay, which makes it more effective.*

Every essay has diction and syntax, so naming them proves nothing. “A tone” is not a tone. “More effective” is not an effect. Here is a revision.

> *Johnson describes procrastination in the formal vocabulary of moral philosophy (“folly,” “remonstrances,” “pertinacious”), and that elevated diction insists that a daily habit is a serious failing of character; yet by writing “we” and “ourselves” he places himself among the guilty, so the tone stays grave without becoming superior.*

The revision quotes the words, names their shared quality (formal, moral, Latinate), states the attitude they convey, and adds a second choice that adjusts the tone. It also says what the tone does for the argument: it lets Johnson reprove readers without alienating them.

A useful formula, to be varied rather than copied: **choice + quality + attitude + purpose.** *The writer's [specific words or pattern], which [quality], conveys [precise tone], which [helps the argument how].*

## Common mistakes

- **Naming categories instead of choices.** “The author uses imagery” earns nothing. Quote the words.
- **Vague tone words.** Replace “positive,” “negative,” “emotional” and “strong” with a precise adjective, or a pair.
- **Confusing subject and tone.** A grave subject can be treated with irony; Idler No. 30 treats wartime lies with scornful wit.
- **Feature-spotting.** Listing a simile, an alliteration and a parallel structure, with no claim connecting them, is a scavenger hunt, not an analysis.
- **Missing the shift.** Johnson's Rambler moves from sympathy to reproach at the Tantalus sentence. Questions often ask about such a turn.
- **Ignoring purpose.** Tone is always in service of something. Say what.

## Exam connection

On **Section I**, style questions make up roughly an eighth of the multiple-choice section. Typical stems: *The diction in the second paragraph primarily serves to*, *The tone of the passage is best described as*, *The comparison in the final sentence emphasizes*, *The syntax of the third paragraph contributes to the passage by*. Wrong answers often name a real feature with the wrong effect, or a tone the passage is near but not at; read for degree (*wry* is not *scathing*).

On **Section II**, the rhetorical analysis essay (Q2) is where 7.A pays most. The evidence-and-commentary row rewards explaining how specific choices serve the writer's purpose; the sophistication point often goes to writers who trace how a tone shifts, or who explain the tension between two tones (Johnson's gravity and his sympathy). In your own argument essays, tone is next week's subject.
