# Week 11 · Developing an Argument with Du Bois's “The Souls of Black Folk”

## What you need to be able to do

- **6.C** Choose the method of development that a claim and an audience call for, and write it so that it advances the argument rather than decorating it.

In plain language: by Friday you should be able to look at a claim in your own draft and say *my reader has never seen this problem, so I need two sentences of description here*, or *my reader thinks this is natural, so I need to show its cause*, and then write those sentences well.

## The skill: method follows need

Last week's table listed six methods and what each can do. Using them is a matter of diagnosis. Before you write a paragraph, ask what your reader is missing.

| If your reader... | ...they need | Write it like this |
|---|---|---|
| has never seen the problem | description or narration | a brief, specific case or picture, then commentary that says what it shows |
| misunderstands or disputes a key term | definition | the meaning you will use, set against the meaning you reject |
| thinks the problem is natural or inevitable | cause and effect | the cause, the mechanism, and a modest claim |
| thinks two cases have nothing to do with each other | comparison | the point of likeness that matters, and often a telling difference |
| sees a crowded subject as one thing | classification | categories sorted by one principle, each leading somewhere |

Four habits make the difference between a method that argues and one that only fills space.

1. **Keep it short.** Narration in an argument is a few sentences, not a page. Description selects three details, not twelve.
2. **Be specific.** A place, a time, a number, a named object. *A student had trouble* persuades nobody; *a seventh grader on her way to the library was clipped by a turning truck* does.
3. **Say what it shows.** Every narrative, description or comparison needs a sentence of commentary that tells the reader what it proves.
4. **Choose for the reader, not for yourself.** The method that is easiest to write is often not the one this audience needs.

Writers who handle the methods well rarely use just one. The model this week, W. E. B. Du Bois, moves from a question to a story to a definition to a causal argument in four paragraphs, and each one prepares the next.

## The text in context

W. E. B. Du Bois (1868-1963) grew up in Great Barrington, Massachusetts, studied at Fisk University in Nashville, at Harvard and in Berlin, and in 1895 became the first African American to earn a Harvard Ph.D. By 1903 he was a professor at Atlanta University, publishing careful sociological studies of Black life in the South. *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903) collects fourteen essays, several revised from magazine articles, each opening with lines of poetry and a bar of music from a spiritual.

**Exigence.** The decade before had seen Southern states pass segregation laws and strip Black citizens of the vote, a wave of lynching, and a national habit of discussing Black Americans as “the Negro problem.” The most prominent Black leader of the day, Booker T. Washington, urged patience and industrial training; Du Bois argued in one chapter that this accepted too much. (The language of race in the book is the language of 1903, and you will see it in the passages quoted below.)

**Audience.** The Forethought addresses a “Gentle Reader,” chiefly the white American public who had read about Black Americans as a problem but never imagined what being called one felt like. At the same time the book speaks to Black readers, giving words to an experience many knew. That double audience shapes his methods: statistics would not reach the first audience's imagination, and an abstract theory would not do justice to the second audience's experience.

**Purpose.** To make readers see “the strange meaning of being black here at the dawning of the Twentieth Century,” and to argue that the obstacles Black Americans faced came from the society around them, not from any lack in themselves. His thesis is announced in the Forethought: “the problem of the Twentieth Century is the problem of the color line.”

## Close reading: four methods in sequence

### 1. A question, then a story

> Between me and the other world there is ever an unasked question: unasked by some through feelings of delicacy; by others through the difficulty of rightly framing it. All, nevertheless, flutter round it. [...] To the real question, How does it feel to be a problem? I answer seldom a word.

The book opens not with a claim but with a question no one asks. White acquaintances circle it with substitutes (“I know an excellent colored man in my town”). Du Bois's first move is to name his readers' evasion, and his second is to answer the question they will not ask. He answers with a story:

> In a wee wooden schoolhouse, something put it into the boys' and girls' heads to buy gorgeous visiting-cards--ten cents a package--and exchange. The exchange was merry, till one girl, a tall newcomer, refused my card,--refused it peremptorily, with a glance.

**Why narration here?** His readers have never been called a problem. A definition would tell them; a story lets them feel it. Look at how well the narrative follows the habits above. It is **brief**: a few sentences. It is **specific**: the wee wooden schoolhouse, ten cents a package, a tall newcomer. It is built on a **turn**, a single gesture, “with a glance,” and the dashes make the reader pause on “refused.” The scene is innocent and ordinary, which is exactly why the exclusion hurts. Then comes the commentary that tells us what the story shows: “shut out from their world by a vast veil.”

### 2. A definition that generalises the story

> It is a peculiar sensation, this double-consciousness, this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity. One ever feels his twoness,--an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings; two warring ideals in one dark body, whose dogged strength alone keeps it from being torn asunder.

**Why definition here?** The story was one boy's moment. To argue about a people, Du Bois needs a concept: a named condition readers can recognise and discuss. The definition gives the feeling a name and a structure. Notice how the definition is written. “This double-consciousness, this sense of ..., of measuring ...” restates the term three times, each more concrete. Then the syntax enacts the meaning: “two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings; two warring ideals.” The repeated “two” makes division audible. The “veil” from the story returns (“born with a veil”), so the reader knows the story and the definition are about the same thing.

### 3. Cause and effect that overturns a judgment

> Here in America, in the few days since Emancipation, the black man's turning hither and thither in hesitant and doubtful striving has often made his very strength to lose effectiveness, to seem like absence of power, like weakness. And yet it is not weakness,--it is the contradiction of double aims.

**Why cause and effect here?** Many of his white readers believed that Black Americans' struggles showed some natural weakness. That belief is the obstacle the argument has to remove, and only a causal claim can remove it. Du Bois takes the very effect they point to (what “seem[s] like absence of power”) and supplies a different cause: double aims. Then he gives the mechanism through an example. The Black artisan, trying both to escape white contempt and to serve a poor community, had “but half a heart in either cause” and so became a poor craftsman. The minister, the scholar and the artist follow, each pulled two ways. The pattern of examples makes the causal claim hard to dismiss.

### 4. Narration and description as a whole argument

The fourth chapter, “Of the Meaning of Progress,” is almost entirely story. Du Bois, then a Fisk student, walks the hills of rural Tennessee looking for a school to teach, finds one through a young woman named Josie, and teaches there for two summers. He describes her carefully:

> Josie told me of it; she was a thin, homely girl of twenty, with a dark-brown face and thick, hard hair.

Ten years later he returns. Some families have bought land; Josie, worn out by work and disappointment, has died. The chapter ends:

> My journey was done, and behind me lay hill and dale, and Life and Death. How shall man measure Progress there where the dark-faced Josie lies? How many heartfuls of sorrow shall balance a bushel of wheat?

> Thus sadly musing, I rode to Nashville in the Jim Crow car.

**Why narration and description for a whole chapter?** “Progress” was a word readers met in reports full of acres and dollars. Du Bois's argument is that those measures leave something out, and he can only show what they leave out by making readers care about one person. The early description of Josie is the investment; the closing questions cash it in. The last line is a concrete detail that answers the questions without stating an answer: the educated narrator rides home in a segregated car. Narration has become argument because the ending tells us what the story was evidence for.

## From reading to writing: developing your own claims

Here is a student's draft for a school board considering budget cuts:

> *The school board should not cut the music program. Music is good. Students in music have better attendance. There are many kinds of music classes, such as band, choir and orchestra. Cutting the program would save $40,000. Music is like math.*

Every sentence gestures at a method and develops none. *Music is good* is a definition with nothing in it. *Better attendance* is a correlation with no cause. *Band, choir and orchestra* is a classification without a point. *Music is like math* is a comparison with no likeness named. Here is a revision in which each method does work:

> *The school board should not cut the music program, because it is one of the district's most effective tools for its own top priority: getting students to school. By the music program I mean not an after-school hobby but daily classes in which students practise discipline and teamwork in front of an audience. According to district records, students in those classes attend more often than students who are not, and the reason is not hard to find: an ensemble cannot rehearse around an empty chair, so band and choir give some students a reason to come in on mornings when they otherwise might not. Each part of the program reaches a different group, and orchestra is the only place in the district where a student can learn a string instrument free. Forty thousand dollars is less than one percent of the budget; it buys the one class that, for some students, is the reason to come at all.*

What changed:

- The **thesis** speaks to the board's stated value, attendance.
- The **definition** rejects the meaning that makes cutting easy (a hobby).
- The **cause and effect** supplies a modest mechanism (*some students*, *an empty chair*).
- The **classification** keeps only the category that argues: a group that would lose something unavailable elsewhere.
- The weak **comparison** is cut. Not every method belongs in every paragraph.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing the method first.** Start from the reader's need, not from a method you want to show off.
- **Narration that runs on.** If your story is longer than your argument, cut it to its turn.
- **Description without selection.** Keep only the details that help the reader judge.
- **The dictionary opening.** *Webster's defines...* tells the reader nothing. Stipulate your meaning and show why it matters.
- **Correlation dressed as cause.** Supply a mechanism, and qualify (*some*, *often*, *helps explain*) when other causes may contribute.
- **A comparison with no point of likeness.** Name what the two things share that matters to the claim.
- **Leaving the method unexplained.** Every story, picture or comparison needs commentary saying what it shows.

## Exam connection

Section I writing questions test 6.C directly: *The writer wants to add a brief example that illustrates the claim in sentence 4*, *Which version of sentence 6 best develops the comparison?*, *The writer wants to explain the cause of the problem described in sentence 3*. The right answer is specific and connected to the claim; distractors are vague, irrelevant or merely decorative. In Section II, methods of development are how your argument essay (Q3) earns its evidence-and-commentary points: a brief, specific narrative, a well-chosen comparison or a causal explanation is your evidence, and the commentary that explains it is your reasoning. A sophistication point often goes to an essay that defines a key term carefully or explains a complex cause.

1. For each of the six methods, name the kind of reader who most needs it.
2. Why does Du Bois answer the “unasked question” with a story rather than a definition?
3. How does the definition of double-consciousness depend on the story before it? What does it add that the story cannot?
4. What belief does Du Bois's causal claim about “double aims” overturn?
5. Explain how the last sentence of “Of the Meaning of Progress” makes an argument without stating one.
6. Take a claim from your own recent writing. Decide what your reader needs, and write three sentences that develop the claim with the right method.
