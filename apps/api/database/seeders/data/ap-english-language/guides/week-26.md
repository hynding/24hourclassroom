# Week 26 · Speaking to a Frightened Nation in Roosevelt's First Inaugural

Unit 8 is about style for an audience: how the same argument changes shape when the people listening change. This first week asks the question underneath every choice of style. Who is this for, what do they already believe, what do they care about, and what do they need from me right now? You will read Franklin D. Roosevelt's First Inaugural Address of March 4, 1933, a speech given to a country that was frightened, angry and short of money, and you will practise revising a draft for an audience that does not start out on the writer's side.

## What you need to be able to do

- **1.B** Explain how an argument demonstrates understanding of an audience's beliefs, values or needs. Point to a specific choice and say what it shows the writer knows about the audience, and how the writer uses that knowledge.
- **2.B** Demonstrate an understanding of an audience's beliefs, values or needs in your own writing: pick evidence the audience trusts, words it uses, concessions it expects and appeals to what it already values.
- Spiral review: name the exigence (1.A), separate claim from evidence (3.A), describe a line of reasoning (5.A), choose transitions by their logic (6.B) and explain how syntax creates tone (7.A).

## The skill explained: beliefs, values, needs

In Unit 1 you learned to identify the audience as one part of the rhetorical situation. Skill 1.B goes further. Naming the audience is only the start; the question is what the text shows the writer understands about that audience. Three words in the skill give you a checklist.

**Beliefs** are what the audience holds to be true. In 1933 many Americans believed that the banks were unsafe and that the financiers had caused the crash. A writer can confirm a belief, correct it or build on it.

**Values** are what the audience holds to be good or important: honesty, hard work, faith, fairness, independence, family. Values are steadier than beliefs, so writers who want to change a belief usually appeal to a value. If you want a cautious audience to accept a new idea, show that the idea serves something it already cherishes.

**Needs** are what the audience lacks or wants at this moment: information, reassurance, a plan, permission to act, a reason to hope. A need is tied to the occasion. The same audience needs different things in a crisis and in a calm year.

When you analyse, the strongest sentences link all three to a choice: *By conceding that times are hard before promising recovery, the speaker meets his listeners' need for honesty and shows he shares their belief that earlier optimism was false.* That sentence names a choice, an audience belief, an audience need, and an effect.

Two further distinctions help. A **primary audience** is directly addressed; a **secondary audience** also hears or reads. Roosevelt speaks to “my fellow Americans,” but Congress, bankers and foreign governments were listening too. And audiences can be **friendly, neutral or hostile** to a claim. A friendly audience needs a plan and motivation; a neutral one needs clear evidence; a hostile one needs common ground and concessions before it will hear a claim at all.

## The text in context

Roosevelt took office in the fourth winter of the Great Depression. About a quarter of the workforce was unemployed, farm prices had collapsed, and in the weeks before the inauguration a wave of bank runs had led state after state to close its banks. The outgoing administration had spent three years predicting that recovery was near. Roosevelt had won the election of 1932 decisively, but the audience he faced on the Capitol steps, and the much larger audience listening by radio, was not cheering. It was scared and it had stopped trusting the people in charge.

That is the exigence: a crisis of confidence as much as of money. The purpose of the speech follows from it. Roosevelt has to calm panic, earn trust, name who is to blame, and win consent for strong and fast government action, including, if Congress hesitated, broad executive power. The genre helps him. An inaugural address is a ceremonial speech that is expected to unite the nation and set a direction, so the audience was ready to hear about shared values and common effort.

The address, as a speech of the federal government, is a U.S. government work and in the public domain. It is short, about 1,900 words, and well worth reading in full.

## Close reading

### 1. Naming what the audience expects

The speech opens not with Roosevelt's plans but with the audience's expectations:

> I am certain that my fellow Americans expect that on my induction into the Presidency I will address them with a candor and a decision which the present situation of our Nation impels.

The sentence shows two things about his listeners. They want **candor**, frankness, because they have heard years of comfortable forecasts. And they want **decision**, a leader who will act. By naming these expectations himself, Roosevelt shows he knows them, and he makes a promise he must keep for the rest of the speech. The next sentence keeps it at once: “This is preeminently the time to speak the truth, the whole truth, frankly and boldly.” The phrase “the whole truth” echoes the courtroom oath, which a broad audience would recognise as the language of honesty under obligation.

Then comes the most famous line: “the only thing we have to fear is fear itself.” Read it as audience analysis. Roosevelt cannot fix the economy in a sentence, but he can change what his listeners think the immediate danger is. He calls fear “nameless, unreasoning, unjustified terror which paralyzes needed efforts to convert retreat into advance.” If the danger is panic, the remedy is in the audience's own power, and the form it takes is support for leadership: “I am convinced that you will again give that support to leadership in these critical days.” The need for reassurance and the need for action are met in the same move.

### 2. Acknowledgment before hope

Roosevelt does not rush to optimism. He lists the hardships in a long sentence of short clauses separated by semicolons:

> Values have shrunken to fantastic levels; taxes have risen; our ability to pay has fallen; government of all kinds is faced by serious curtailment of income; the means of exchange are frozen in the currents of trade; the withered leaves of industrial enterprise lie on every side; farmers find no markets for their produce; the savings of many years in thousands of families are gone.

Every listener could find a clause about their own life here: the shopkeeper in “taxes have risen,” the farmer in “no markets for their produce,” the family whose bank failed in the last clause. The syntax matters (that is 7.A spiral review): the series accumulates, so the hardship feels complete. Then he concedes outright: “Only a foolish optimist can deny the dark realities of the moment.” For an audience that had been told for three years that prosperity was around the corner, this concession is the most persuasive sentence in the opening. It separates Roosevelt from the failed optimists and proves he means his promise of candor.

Only after acknowledgment does hope arrive, and it arrives as an argument: “Yet our distress comes from no failure of substance. We are stricken by no plague of locusts.” The comparison with a biblical plague tells a scripture-reading audience that this disaster is not an act of God. “Plenty is at our doorstep.” If the problem is human, humans can solve it.

### 3. A villain the audience already knew

Who failed? Roosevelt answers: “the rulers of the exchange of mankind's goods have failed, through their own stubbornness and their own incompetence.” Then he reaches for an allusion every churchgoer in the audience would know:

> The money changers have fled from their high seats in the temple of our civilization. We may now restore that temple to the ancient truths.

In the Gospels, Jesus drives the money changers out of the temple. The allusion confirms a belief the audience already held (bankers and speculators were to blame) and joins it to a value (moral and religious order). It also gives frightened people something they badly need: an enemy outside themselves, and a cause larger than recovery. Roosevelt builds the moral argument further: “Happiness lies not in the mere possession of money; it lies in the joy of achievement, in the thrill of creative effort.” For an audience that had lost its money, this is consolation as well as argument. What matters most, he says, has not been taken.

Notice what he does **not** do: he does not blame the people. Blaming a frightened audience would cost him its trust. Throughout, the people are wise and the financiers foolish.

### 4. Action, war and a mandate

The second half of the speech answers the need for decision. “This Nation asks for action, and action now.” The short sentence, with its repetition of action, sounds like the audience's own impatience, and Roosevelt speaks it on their behalf: the Nation asks. A string of sentences then begins “It can be helped by,” each naming a measure (farm prices, foreclosures, relief, transportation), and the series ends with a jab at the previous years: “it can never be helped merely by talking about it.”

The war analogy suits an audience that remembered 1917 and 1918, when the country had united and government had moved fast. Roosevelt proposes treating unemployment “as we would treat the emergency of a war,” asks the people to “move as a trained and loyal army,” and warns that if Congress fails to act he will ask for “broad Executive power to wage a war against the emergency, as great as the power that would be given to me if we were in fact invaded by a foreign foe.” The analogy makes an extraordinary request feel familiar and patriotic.

Finally, the credit goes to the audience: “The people of the United States have not failed. In their need they have registered a mandate that they want direct, vigorous action.” By describing his own power as the people's wish, Roosevelt turns his request into their decision.

## From reading to writing: revising for an audience

Skill 2.B is the writing side of this week. Before you draft an argument for a specific audience, answer three questions in a sentence each. What do these readers already believe about the issue? What do they value most? What do they need from me? Then make four kinds of choices.

**Evidence the audience trusts.** Suppose a student is arguing that the high school should start an hour later. For the school board, the evidence that matters is cost and logistics; for parents, health and safety; for teachers, attention in first period. Same claim, different proof.

> Weak (for a school board): *Everyone knows teenagers are tired in the morning, so school should start later.*
>
> Stronger: *A later start would cost the district one additional bus route, about the price of two classroom laptops per school, and the three districts nearby that made the change report fewer first-period absences.*

**Words the audience uses.** Jargon tells an audience it is not the one being addressed. Translate abstractions into people and times.

> Weak: *The proposal would optimize stakeholder engagement across demographics.*
>
> Stronger: *Later hours would let working parents, students and retirees use the library when they are actually free.*

**Concessions the audience expects.** If your readers have an objection, they are waiting for you to mention it. Grant what is true, then answer it, as Roosevelt grants the dark realities before arguing that the distress is man-made.

> Weak: *Some people think the levy is too expensive, but they are wrong.*
>
> Stronger: *Admittedly, any new tax is a burden on a fixed income, which is why the levy exempts groceries and medicine and ends after six years.*

**Appeals to values the audience holds.** A closing that returns to the audience's values outlasts one that simply asks for agreement.

> Weak: *Please vote yes because the library needs your help.*
>
> Stronger: *Many of you paid for this library forty years ago; I am asking you to keep its doors open for the people who will use it next.*

## Common mistakes

- **Naming the audience and stopping.** *Roosevelt speaks to the American people* is 1.A. For 1.B, say what he knows about them and how a choice uses it.
- **Treating the audience as one mind.** Roosevelt's listeners included farmers, bank depositors, the unemployed, Congress and foreign governments. Strong essays notice when a passage turns to a particular group.
- **Confusing pandering with understanding.** Roosevelt tells hard truths. Understanding an audience lets a writer challenge it effectively.
- **Ignoring the moment.** A need belongs to an occasion. Write *in March 1933, with banks closed,* not *people always want hope.*
- **In your own drafts, writing to yourself.** Jargon, inside jokes and evidence only you find convincing all signal that the writer has forgotten who is reading.

## Exam connection

On the multiple-choice section, reading questions ask how a choice “reflects the writer's understanding of the audience” or what a passage assumes about its readers. Writing questions give you a draft and a goal: *The writer wants to appeal to an audience of local business owners. Which version of sentence 6 best accomplishes this goal?* Choose the version whose evidence and diction fit the named audience, not the one that sounds most impressive.

On the free-response section, the rhetorical analysis prompt (Q2) always names a speaker, an occasion and an audience. Use them. A thesis that says *Roosevelt meets his frightened audience's need for honesty and action by conceding the crisis, naming a villain and casting recovery as a war the people have chosen to fight* is already earning the evidence-and-commentary points, because every body paragraph will connect a choice to an audience. In the argument essay (Q3), sophistication often comes from awareness of who would disagree and why.
