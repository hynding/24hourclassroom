## What you need to be able to do

- **3.B** Identify a thesis wherever it sits (first, last, in a figure, or implied) and describe the structure it signals, including structures built from questions, refusals and syllogisms.
- **4.B** Write a thesis that matches its body: every reason previewed gets a paragraph, every paragraph serves a reason. Write comparison and synthesis theses that make one claim, not two.
- Explain how the audience shapes where and how a writer states a thesis.

In plain language: by Friday you should be able to say *Anthony puts her thesis first because jurors need to know what she is proving, Washington puts his in a story because a divided crowd needs something to remember, and Douglass holds his back because a comfortable crowd needs to be prepared*, and then write a thesis that does the same kind of thinking.

## The skill: thesis and structure as one decision

Weeks 6 and 7 treated the thesis on its own. In real arguments, the thesis, the structure and the audience are a single decision. A writer asks: *What am I trying to prove? To whom? What must they grant before they will hear it? In what order should the proof come?* The answers determine where the thesis goes, how explicit it is, and what the body looks like.

Three patterns from this unit:

| Speaker | Where the thesis sits | Why, given the audience | Structure it signals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anthony | First paragraph, announced (“It shall be my work this evening to prove”) | Potential jurors must judge one question; clarity is the strategy | A legal case: first principles, founding documents, the Fourteenth Amendment |
| Washington | In a parable told early and repeated | A divided crowd needs a single memorable command | One command applied to each audience in turn |
| Douglass | Subject delayed (“My subject, then”); method stated at the end of a passage | A sympathetic but complacent crowd must be prepared | Questions raised and dismissed, building to a claim |

None of these is the right way to place a thesis. Each is right for its room.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Delayed thesis | A thesis held back until the audience has been prepared. |
| Thesis about method | A claim about how a subject should be addressed rather than about the subject itself. |
| Apophasis (paralipsis) | Emphasising a point by claiming not to discuss it. |
| Syllogism | Reasoning from a major premise and a minor premise to a conclusion. |
| Thesis-body match | The correspondence between the reasons a thesis previews and the paragraphs that develop them. |
| Comparison thesis | A single claim about the relationship between two texts, usually with a turn (*but*, *while*, *whereas*). |
| Synthesis thesis | A position on the issue the sources discuss, which the sources then support. |
| Off-thesis paragraph | A paragraph that does not support any part of the thesis. |

## The texts in context

You know the occasions by now. Douglass spoke in Rochester on July 5, 1852, to several hundred mostly white, mostly antislavery listeners at an Independence Day event. Washington spoke in Atlanta on September 18, 1895, to a divided crowd at a Southern exposition. Anthony spoke in some fifty towns in 1873 to the men who might sit on her jury.

The new passage this week comes just after Douglass announces his subject and promises to use “the severest language I can command.” He knows how that promise will sound to many sympathetic Northerners, who admired abolitionists' goals but disliked their tone. So he imagines one of them speaking.

## Close reading

### 1. Douglass answers his audience's objection

> But I fancy I hear some one of my audience say, it is just in this circumstance that you and your brother abolitionists fail to make a favorable impression on the public mind. Would you argue more, and denounce less, would you persuade more and rebuke less, your cause would be much more likely to succeed. But, I submit, where all is plain there is nothing to be argued.

**Observe.** The objection comes from a friend: someone who wants “your cause” to succeed. Douglass's answer is short and flat.

**Interpret.** The objector assumes there is still something to prove. Douglass denies it.

**Function (1.B, 3.B).** By voicing the objection, Douglass shows that he knows his audience's reservation. By answering it with a premise (“where all is plain there is nothing to be argued”), he sets up the structure of everything that follows: he will test each point the objector might want argued and show that it is already plain.

### 2. A structure built from refusals

> Must I undertake to prove that the slave is a man? That point is conceded already. Nobody doubts it. The slaveholders themselves acknowledge it in the enactment of laws for their government.

**Observe.** A question, then a refusal, then evidence. The pattern repeats: *Would you have me argue that man is entitled to liberty? ... You have already declared it.* *Must I argue the wrongfulness of slavery? Is that a question for republicans?* *What, then, remains to be argued? Is it that slavery is not divine?*

**Interpret.** Each proposition is one the audience either grants already or could not deny without contradicting itself. The evidence comes from the opponents (Virginia's seventy-two capital crimes for Black men against two for white men; the laws against teaching enslaved people to read) and from the audience (“You have already declared it”).

**Function (3.B).** This is **apophasis** on the scale of a whole passage: in refusing to argue each point, Douglass argues it, and argues it decisively. The structure is a series of closed doors. By the end, the only door left open is the one he has chosen.

### 3. The thesis arrives as a conclusion

> At a time like this, scorching irony, not convincing argument, is needed. Oh! had I the ability, and could I reach the nation’s ear, I would to-day pour out a fiery stream of biting ridicule, blasting reproach, withering sarcasm, and stern rebuke. For it is not light that is needed, but fire; it is not the gentle shower, but thunder.

**Observe.** The first sentence is a claim with a built-in contrast (*irony, not argument*). The next sentence lists four kinds of rebuke, each an adjective and a noun. The third restates the contrast twice in images.

**Interpret.** This is a **thesis about method**: not *slavery is wrong* (already conceded) but *what this moment needs is rebuke*.

**Function (3.B).** Because it comes after every alternative has been dismissed, the thesis reads as the conclusion of an argument rather than as the provocation it would have been at the start. The paragraph then previews the denunciation to come with four parallel clauses: the feeling of the nation must be quickened, its conscience roused, its propriety startled, its hypocrisy exposed. Next comes the famous answer to the speech's title question.

### 4. Anthony's thesis as a chain of reasoning

> The only question left to be settled, now, is: Are women persons? And I hardly believe any of our opponents will have the hardihood to say they are not. Being persons, then, women are citizens, and no state has a right to make any new law, or to enforce any old law, that shall abridge their privileges or immunities.

**Observe.** A question, a dismissal of the opposing answer (with a touch of irony in “hardihood”), then a conclusion introduced by “then.”

**Interpret.** Anthony reduces her thesis to a syllogism: persons born or naturalised here are citizens; women are persons; therefore women are citizens whose privileges no state may abridge.

**Function (3.B).** Like Douglass, Anthony makes the decisive step look so obvious that disagreement seems absurd. Unlike Douglass, she has stated her thesis at the start and returns to it here as the end of a logical chain. Her structure is a proof; his is a series of refusals. Both suit their audiences: jurors need a proof; a complacent crowd needs to be stripped of excuses.

## From reading to writing: matching thesis and body

The most common thesis problem in revision is not a weak claim but a mismatch. Here is a draft introduction and the topic sentences of a student's rhetorical analysis of Washington's address, written for this course:

> *In 1895 Booker T. Washington spoke at the Cotton States and International Exposition in Atlanta. His goal was to win support for the economic progress of Black Southerners. Washington uses many rhetorical strategies in his speech.*
>
> *Body 1: Washington's parable of the ship gives both audiences a single command to remember.*
> *Body 2: Washington appeals to white employers' self-interest by describing Black workers as loyal and free of strikes.*
> *Body 3: Washington was born into slavery in Virginia and later studied at Hampton Institute.*
> *Body 4: Washington's image of the fingers and the hand compresses his compromise into one memorable picture.*

Run the match check. The thesis (*many rhetorical strategies*) previews nothing, so no paragraph can match it. Body 3 is biography; it analyses no choice. Bodies 1, 2 and 4 are strong. So build the thesis from them, cut or repurpose Body 3, and sharpen the context so that it names the audience:

> *In 1895 Booker T. Washington addressed a divided crowd at the Atlanta Exposition: white Southern officials wary of Black progress, Black Southerners hoping for a champion, and Northern visitors whose money and opinion mattered. Through a parable both audiences could remember, an appeal to employers' self-interest and an image that compresses his compromise, Washington wins that divided audience's support for economic cooperation.*

Now every body paragraph answers to a phrase in the thesis, in order.

**Comparison theses** need the same discipline. Two descriptions joined by *and* (*Douglass's speech is about slavery and Anthony's is about voting*) make no claim. A comparison thesis makes one claim about the relationship:

> *Both speakers build on the nation's founding documents, but Anthony uses them as legal proof to win an acquittal while Douglass uses them as an indictment to shame a nation.*

**Synthesis theses** take a position on the issue, never on the sources. *Source C says parks raise property values* is a summary. *Cities should convert some downtown parking to parks, because green space draws more shoppers than parking does* is a thesis the sources can support.

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming the thesis must be first.** Douglass's passage states its thesis last, on purpose.
- **Mistaking a supporting step for the thesis.** *The slaveholders themselves acknowledge it* proves one point; the thesis is the claim about method all the points lead to.
- **Previewing reasons the body never develops,** or writing paragraphs the thesis never mentions.
- **Writing two theses and calling it a comparison.** Join them with a claim about the relationship.
- **Summarising sources in a synthesis thesis.** Your position first; the sources serve it.
- **Keeping a paragraph because it is interesting.** Biography, history and summary belong only when they explain a choice.

## The exam connection

Section I passages often hold their main claim until late, as Douglass's does. When a question asks for the passage's central claim, test each option against the whole passage, not just its opening. Writing questions on drafts frequently ask which paragraph should be cut, which sentence best states the main claim, or which revision best previews what follows; the match check answers all three.

In Section II, a thesis that previews your line of reasoning gives you a plan you can follow under time pressure. For rhetorical analysis, name choices in the order you will discuss them; for argument, name your reasons or your concession-and-response pattern; for synthesis, state your position on the issue and let the sources line up behind it. The unit exam ends with a full rhetorical analysis essay on an unseen speech, so practise writing a thesis for an unfamiliar passage in five minutes.
