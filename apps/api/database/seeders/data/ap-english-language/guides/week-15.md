## What this week is for

The three weeks before this one studied the edges of an argument: how it begins, how it ends and how definition and comparison can shape both. This week returns to the sentence that holds those edges together, the **thesis**. An introduction exists to deliver it; a conclusion exists to return to it, changed. If the thesis is weak, no opening or closing can save the essay.

You studied thesis statements in Unit 2. This week adds three things: how to find the thesis of a long, sprawling argument that states its claim many times in many forms; how a thesis can **preview structure**, whether in one sentence or in a mid-essay map; and how introduction, thesis, topic sentences and conclusion work as a single system.

By Friday you should be able to:

- identify the overarching thesis of a passage, tell it apart from restatements and supporting maxims, and explain how it signals structure (3.B);
- write a defensible thesis that previews your argument's structure, open or closed (4.B);
- align your topic sentences and conclusion with that thesis.

## The skill explained: claim, restatement, map

**The overarching thesis** is the claim every other part of the argument serves. In a short editorial it may be one sentence. In a long essay it may be stated early and then restated many times: as a definition, as a command, as a maxim, as a principle applied to a new area. Your job in 3.B is to find the claim that the others are versions of.

A useful test: take a candidate sentence and ask whether the sentences around it **explain, support or restate** it. If they do, it is probably the thesis. If it explains or supports something else, it is a supporting claim.

**Previewing structure.** A thesis can show the path of the argument in several ways:

- a **closed (blueprint) thesis** names the main reasons in order: *X, because A, B and C*;
- a **contrast** previews a two-part structure: *not X, but Y*; *although X, Y*;
- a **movement** previews a direction: from principle to application, from the individual to society;
- a **mid-essay preview** maps a later section, as Emerson does when he lists the areas of life his thesis will transform.

An **open thesis** states the claim without the reasons and lets topic sentences reveal the path. Neither kind is better in itself. A closed thesis is clear and efficient under time pressure; an open thesis suits an argument that builds, turns or qualifies.

**The system.** Introduction leads to thesis; each topic sentence carries one part of the thesis; the conclusion returns to the thesis with its significance added. If you can read only the thesis, the topic sentences and the last sentence of an essay and still follow the argument, the system works.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Overarching thesis | The main claim of the whole argument. |
| Restatement | The thesis expressed again in new words or a new context. |
| Closed (blueprint) thesis | A thesis that names the main reasons in order. |
| Open thesis | A thesis that states the claim without listing the reasons. |
| Implied thesis | A main claim the text makes clear without stating it in one sentence. |
| Topic sentence | The claim of a paragraph, serving the thesis. |
| Maxim (aphorism) | A short, memorable statement of a general truth. |
| Defensible | Arguable: a reasonable reader could dispute it, so it requires proof. |

## The text in context

“Self-Reliance” appeared in 1841 as the second essay in Emerson's first collection, *Essays*. It was built from journal entries and lectures of the 1830s, the same years as “The American Scholar” (week 13), and it carries that oration's call for intellectual independence from the scholar to every person.

Emerson's **audience** was the reading public of New England and beyond: educated, often churchgoing, many involved in the reform movements of the time (temperance, abolition, education), and generally respectful of tradition, society and institutions. His **purpose** was to unsettle that respect: to argue that conformity, even to good causes and to one's own past opinions, betrays the self.

The essay is famous for its **aphoristic style**. Emerson wrote in sentences that can stand alone, and many have become proverbs. That makes the essay quotable and also makes its thesis harder to locate: almost every paragraph contains a sentence that sounds like the main claim. Learning to find the thesis that the maxims serve is excellent practice for the exam's long, dense passages.

## Close reading

### 1. The thesis arrives early

> I READ the other day some verses written by an eminent painter which were original and not conventional. The soul always hears an admonition in such lines, let the subject be what it may. The sentiment they instil is of more value than any thought they may contain. To believe your own thought, to believe that what is true for you in your private heart is true for all men,--that is genius.

**Observe.** One sentence of anecdote, two of reflection, then a sentence built on repetition (“to believe ... to believe”) that ends with a short, emphatic clause: “that is genius.”

**Interpret.** The thesis is a definition: genius is believing your own thought and trusting that it holds for everyone.

**Function.** This is the overarching thesis, stated in the fourth sentence. The anecdote (2.A) gives it an occasion; the definition (5.C) gives it force. Everything that follows in the paragraph supports it: Moses, Plato and Milton “spoke not what men, but what they thought”; our rejected thoughts come back “with a certain alienated majesty”; a stranger will say what we thought, and “we shall be forced to take with shame our own opinion from another.”

### 2. Restatements: command, metaphor, maxim

> Trust thyself: every heart vibrates to that iron string.

> Whoso would be a man, must be a nonconformist.

**Observe.** The first is an imperative followed by a metaphor of the heart as an instrument. The second is a maxim with an old-fashioned subject (“Whoso”).

**Interpret.** Both restate the thesis. “Trust thyself” is the thesis as a command. “Whoso would be a man” is the thesis applied to society: trusting your own thought means refusing the conformity that “Society everywhere” demands.

**Function.** Restatement is how a long argument keeps its thesis in view while moving into new territory. Each restatement marks a stage: the self, then society. Notice that neither replaces the first statement; both depend on it.

### 3. Answering an objection

> A foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds, adored by little statesmen and philosophers and divines. With consistency a great soul has simply nothing to do.

**Observe.** The famous sentence turns on one adjective, “foolish.” It is followed by the advice to “Speak what you think now in hard words and to-morrow speak what to-morrow thinks in hard words again, though it contradict every thing you said to-day.”

**Interpret.** Emerson anticipates the objection that self-trust will make a person inconsistent, and answers that only a foolish consistency fears contradiction. The passage ends: “To be great is to be misunderstood.”

**Function.** This is part of the line of reasoning (5.A): after stating and restating the thesis, Emerson clears an obstacle. The thesis now covers not only society's opinions but your own past ones.

### 4. A map in the middle

> It is easy to see that a greater self-reliance must work a revolution in all the offices and relations of men; in their religion; in their education; in their pursuits; their modes of living; their association; in their property; in their speculative views.

**Observe.** One claim (“must work a revolution”) followed by a list of domains separated by semicolons. The sections that follow are numbered, beginning “1. In what prayers do men allow themselves!”

**Interpret.** The thesis is now applied, area by area: prayer and creeds, the superstition of travelling, imitation in art and thought, society and its supposed progress, the reliance on property.

**Function.** This sentence is a blueprint thesis for the final movement of the essay. Skill 3.B asks about a thesis and about any signal it gives of the argument's structure; here the indication arrives mid-essay, at the turn from principle to application. Long arguments often preview structure this way, and so can your own essays: a sentence at the start of a new section can tell the reader where it is going.

### 5. The thesis returns

> Nothing can bring you peace but yourself. Nothing can bring you peace but the triumph of principles.

**Observe.** Two sentences with the same frame; only the last words change, from “yourself” to “the triumph of principles.”

**Interpret.** The self and principle are paired. Self-reliance is not whim; it is obedience to principles found within.

**Function.** The conclusion compresses the thesis into a memorable, repeated line and adds one thing the first paragraph did not say: that the self worth trusting is a self of principle. That addition answers a reader who has worried, through the whole essay, that self-reliance sounds like selfishness. This is the conclusion's job exactly: the thesis returns, changed by the argument.

## From reading to writing: a thesis system

Here is a weak introduction and conclusion for an argument about whether a district should require a semester of public speaking:

> *In our district, a student can graduate without ever giving a speech to more than a handful of classmates. This spring the school board will vote on whether to require a semester of public speaking for every tenth grader. Public speaking is a topic that many people have opinions about. There are good and bad things about requiring it.*

> *In conclusion, public speaking has good and bad sides.*

What goes wrong: the first two sentences establish a real exigence, but the third is filler and the fourth takes no position. The conclusion repeats the non-position. Nothing in between can earn the thesis point, because there is no thesis.

A revised system:

> **Thesis:** *The board should require the course, because it builds the confidence students need now, the skills employers expect later, and the voice citizens need for life.*

> **Topic sentence 1:** *First, a required course would give anxious students what they rarely choose for themselves: enough practice to stop fearing the room.*

> **Topic sentence 2:** *The same practice answers what employers say they cannot find: graduates who can explain an idea clearly to people who disagree.*

> **Topic sentence 3:** *Most of all, a democracy depends on citizens who will stand up at a meeting and be heard.*

> **Conclusion:** *Confidence, a skill employers expect and a citizen's voice are not extras to be picked up by luck; they are what a semester of practice can give every tenth grader. A student who learns at fifteen to stand up and explain an idea will not be learning it for the first time at a job interview or a town meeting.*

What works: the thesis takes a side and previews three reasons in a logical order (now, later, for life). Each topic sentence carries one reason, in the same order, in fresh words. The conclusion gathers the reasons without listing them mechanically, sharpens the claim (not luck, but every student) and ends on an image that looks forward.

**Open or closed?** A closed thesis fits this essay because the three reasons are parallel. If your argument turns (for example, *the course should be offered but not required, because the students who most need it are the ones a requirement would most harm*), an open thesis or a contrast-shaped thesis will preview the structure better than a list.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing a maxim as the thesis.** In an aphoristic essay many sentences sound like theses. Choose the one the others serve.
- **The non-committal thesis.** “There are good and bad things” and “is important” take no position.
- **Previewing reasons the essay does not develop.** A closed thesis is a promise; keep it.
- **Mismatched order.** If the thesis lists A, B, C, the body should take them as A, B, C.
- **Topic sentences that announce.** “First, I will talk about confidence” is a topic, not a claim.
- **The copied conclusion.** Restate the thesis in new words with its significance, never word for word.

## Exam connection

Section I reading questions ask *Which sentence best states the main claim of the passage?* or “The author's argument is best summarised as...” Eliminate answers that are true of only one paragraph, or that restate a supporting example. Questions on structure ask what a sentence “indicates about the organization” of what follows; a list of domains or a “First ... second” pattern is your evidence. Revision questions ask which version of a thesis “most clearly states the writer's position” or “best previews the essay's structure”; match the preview to the body described in the question.

In Section II, Row A gives one point for a defensible thesis that responds to the prompt, and readers look for it at the end of the introduction. A thesis that previews your line of reasoning also helps Row B, because it lets the reader see each body paragraph as a step. For Q3, a **qualifying** thesis that says where a claim like Emerson's holds and where it fails is often the most persuasive position, and it can open the door to the sophistication point.
