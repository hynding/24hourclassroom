# Week 12 · Openings and Closings in Paine's *Common Sense*

## What this week is for

Unit 4 is about the edges of an argument: the first paragraph, the last paragraph and the thesis that connects them. Until now you have mostly studied the middle, where claims meet evidence and commentary explains the link. This week asks a different question. Before a reader has seen a single reason, what must the writer already have done? And after the last reason, what is left to do?

By Friday you should be able to:

- name the jobs an introduction and a conclusion do in an argument, and say which jobs a particular opening or closing is doing;
- explain how a writer's opening and closing fit the audience, purpose and occasion (skill 2.A, with 1.A and 1.B behind it);
- revise a weak student introduction and conclusion so that they fit their rhetorical situation, as the multiple-choice revision questions ask you to do.

## The skill explained: fit, not formula

Skill 2.A says introductions and conclusions must be **appropriate to the purpose and context of the rhetorical situation**. The key word is appropriate. There is no single correct way to begin or end. A funeral oration, a newspaper column and a timed AP essay need different openings, because they meet different readers in different circumstances.

Still, almost every argument's introduction does three jobs:

1. **Orient** the reader: what is the issue, and why does it matter now? This is where the **exigence** usually appears.
2. **Position** the writer: what stance does the writer take, and on what terms does the writer ask to be heard? This is where **ethos** and **audience awareness** show.
3. **Point ahead**: what is the claim, and how will the argument proceed? This is where the **thesis** sits, often with a hint of the line of reasoning.

A conclusion also does three jobs:

1. **Gather**: pull the reasons together into one idea, without listing them all again.
2. **Extend**: show significance, the answer to “so what?” The stakes may widen to a larger group, a longer time or a deeper principle.
3. **Release**: leave the reader with a final position, a question, an image or an action.

Common openings include naming the exigence, entering a conversation (what others say), a concession to the resistant reader, a striking fact, a short scene or a question. Common conclusions include widening the stakes, a call to action, an echo of the opening and a statement of what remains complicated. None is correct in itself. Each is right when it serves this reader on this occasion.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Exigence | The problem, event or need that makes an argument necessary now. |
| Occasion | The time, place and setting in which an argument is delivered or read. |
| Concession | Granting a point to the opposing side or to a doubting reader. |
| Stakes | What is to be gained or lost, and for whom, depending on the argument's outcome. |
| Call to action | A conclusion that tells the audience what to do next. |
| Frame (echo) | A conclusion that returns to an image or phrase from the introduction. |
| Line of reasoning | The sequence of claims that leads to the thesis. |
| Ethos | The writer's credibility and character as the audience perceives them. |

## The text in context

Thomas Paine arrived in Philadelphia from England in late 1774, a former excise officer with little money and a gift for plain, forceful prose. In April 1775 fighting broke out at Lexington and Concord, yet through the following months most colonists, and most colonial leaders, still spoke of reconciliation with Britain. They blamed Parliament and the king's ministers, not the king, and they hoped to win back their rights as British subjects.

In January 1776 Paine published *Common Sense* anonymously. It argued that the colonies should declare independence, that monarchy itself was absurd, and that America could stand alone. It sold in enormous numbers and was read aloud in taverns and camps. Six months later, the Continental Congress declared independence.

Think about the rhetorical situation. The **audience** was huge and mixed: farmers, artisans, merchants, ministers, many of them loyal to the crown out of habit and fear. The **exigence** was the war that had already begun while the political question was unsettled. The **purpose** was not only to persuade but to move readers from sympathy to commitment. Every choice Paine makes at the start and end of the pamphlet answers that situation.

## Close reading

### 1. An opening that concedes

> Perhaps the sentiments contained in the following pages, are not yet sufficiently fashionable to procure them general favor; a long habit of not thinking a thing wrong, gives it a superficial appearance of being right, and raises at first a formidable outcry in defense of custom. But the tumult soon subsides. Time makes more converts than reason.

**Observe.** The first word is “Perhaps.” Paine admits that his views are “not yet sufficiently fashionable.” Then he explains why: habit makes a wrong thing look right. Three short sentences follow, the last a maxim.

**Interpret.** Paine expects resistance and names it before the reader can. But the concession is a trap of a kind: once resistance is explained as “a long habit,” a reader who objects seems to be defending custom rather than reason.

**Function.** For a reader who is loyal out of habit, this opening is disarming. It does not shout; it predicts. And the word “yet” quietly assumes the future: the sentiments are not fashionable *yet*. Paine's opening fits an audience that he must persuade gradually, not one he can command.

### 2. Naming the exigence

> As a long and violent abuse of power, is generally the Means of calling the right of it in question [...] and as the good people of this country are grievously oppressed by the combination, they have an undoubted privilege to inquire into the pretensions of both, and equally to reject the usurpation of either.

**Observe.** One long periodic sentence builds through three “as” clauses before reaching its main point: the people “have an undoubted privilege to inquire.”

**Interpret.** The exigence is oppression by “the combination” of king and Parliament. Paine does not yet argue for independence; he argues for the right to ask the question.

**Function.** This is a modest-sounding step that opens a large door. A reader who grants the right to inquire has already agreed that the king's authority can be questioned, which is most of the distance to Paine's thesis.

### 3. Widening the stakes at the end of the introduction

> The cause of America is in a great measure the cause of all mankind. Many circumstances hath, and will arise, which are not local, but universal, and through which the principles of all Lovers of Mankind are affected, and in the Event of which, their Affections are interested.

**Observe.** The Introduction's last paragraph begins with a sweeping claim, then piles up a contrast (“not local, but universal”).

**Interpret.** A dispute over taxes and troops becomes a struggle for “the natural rights of all Mankind.” The paragraph ends by defining the author as one of those “to whom Nature hath given the Power of feeling,” and it signs off simply as “AUTHOR.”

**Function.** Paine ends his introduction where many writers end their conclusions: on the widest stakes. He wants readers to begin the body already feeling that the question is enormous. And by defining himself through feeling, not name or rank, he invites every feeling reader to stand where he stands.

### 4. Two conclusions: discomfort and friendship

The main pamphlet's closing passage, which begins “To Conclude,” lists four numbered reasons for an open declaration of independence, then ends with an image:

> These proceedings may at first appear strange and difficult; but, like all other steps which we have already passed over, will in a little time become familiar and agreeable; and, until an independance is declared, the Continent will feel itself like a man who continues putting off some unpleasant business from day to day, yet knows it must be done, hates to set about it, wishes it over, and is continually haunted with the thoughts of its necessity.

**Observe.** The sentence concedes again (“strange and difficult”), then echoes the Introduction's prediction that resistance will fade. The final simile is a long list of the procrastinator's feelings.

**Interpret.** The reader is the man in the simile. Paine does not repeat his four reasons; he describes what it feels like to know them and still delay.

**Function.** The conclusion turns hesitation into the thing to escape. It also echoes the opening: there, habit made a wrong look right; here, delay makes a necessity feel like a haunting.

The Appendix, added to later editions, ends differently:

> Wherefore, instead of gazing at each other with suspicious or doubtful curiosity; let each of us, hold out to his neighbour the hearty hand of friendship, and unite in drawing a line, which, like an act of oblivion shall bury in forgetfulness every former dissension.

Here the conclusion turns inward, from Britain to the colonists themselves. Its call to action is a gesture any reader can make. By asking that “the names of Whig and Tory be extinct,” Paine offers former opponents a way to join without humiliation. A divided audience at war needs exactly this ending.

## From reading to writing: a weak and a strong opening

Here is a weak introduction on a prompt about whether a school should replace its final exam week with a project showcase:

> *Since the beginning of time, people have taken tests. Tests are a part of school, and everyone has an opinion about them. In this essay I will talk about final exams and projects.*

What goes wrong: the first sentence is unprovable and says nothing about this school. The second is true of every school. The third announces a topic but takes no position. A reader finishes the paragraph knowing nothing about the writer's claim or why it matters now.

A stronger version:

> *Every June, the week meant to measure a year of learning is the week our hallways are emptiest: last year, exam week had the lowest attendance of the school year. Teachers are right that any replacement must be as demanding as an exam. A project showcase, judged by families and local employers, can be, and it would give seniors a reason to stay engaged after their college plans are settled.*

What works: the first sentence names a specific problem and supports it with a fact (exigence). The second grants the strongest objection from the likely audience (concession, 2.B). The third states a defensible thesis with two reasons that preview the body (4.B).

Now a weak conclusion:

> *In conclusion, final exams and projects are both ways to test students. As I have shown, there are many reasons. Overall, the school should think about it.*

And a stronger one:

> *A showcase would not lower the bar; it would move it into public view, where a year's work is seen by the people students will soon work beside. Before the faculty votes on next year's calendar, the school should pilot a showcase for one grade and let June show what students can do.*

The stronger conclusion gathers the argument into one idea (the bar moves into public view), extends the stakes (the people students will work beside), and releases the reader with a concrete, modest action (a pilot before a named vote).

## Common mistakes

- **The universal opener.** “Throughout history” and “since the dawn of time” signal filler. Start with the specific situation.
- **The topic announcement.** “In this essay I will discuss” names a subject, not a claim.
- **The thesis that hides.** A thesis buried mid-paragraph, or spread over three sentences, may not earn the point.
- **The echo conclusion.** Repeating the thesis and body points in the same order wastes the paragraph where significance belongs.
- **The vague call to action.** “Think about it” retreats from your own argument. Name the step.
- **Ignoring the audience.** An opening that would insult or bore the actual readers fails 2.A even if it is clever.

## Exam connection

On the multiple-choice section, revision questions regularly ask you to choose a better first sentence, decide where a sentence belongs in an introduction, or pick the version of a final sentence that best “concludes the paragraph by” doing something specific. Read the stated goal in the question stem carefully: the right answer is the one that meets that goal for that audience, not the most impressive sentence.

In the essays, a defensible thesis earns the Row A point wherever it appears, but readers look for it at the end of the introduction. Keep the timed introduction short: two to four sentences of context made specific, then the thesis. A conclusion is never required, but one or two sentences that explain your argument's broader significance or a tension within it can support the sophistication point. Summary alone cannot.
