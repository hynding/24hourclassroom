## What this week is for

Last week you looked at what a claim says and how far it reaches. This week you look at how a sentence is *built*, because the build is part of the argument. Two sentences can hold the same facts and argue different things, depending on which idea sits in the main clause, which is tucked into a dependent clause, and which comes last.

By the end of the week you should be able to:

- **7.B** Identify the independent and dependent clauses in a writer's sentence and explain what their arrangement does: which idea it makes primary, what relationship it signals (cause, contrast, condition, time), and where it places the emphasis.
- **8.B** Combine, reorder and repair sentences in a draft so that their structure states the relationship you mean, and put your claim where the reader will feel it.

## The skill explained: clauses and what they do

**An independent clause** has a subject and a verb and can stand alone: *The council voted.* **A dependent clause** has a subject and a verb but begins with a word that makes it lean on something else: *because the council voted*, *although the council voted*, *which the council voted on*.

Writers relate ideas in two main ways.

**Coordination** joins independent clauses as equals, with *and, but, or, nor, for, so, yet* (after a comma) or with a semicolon. It says: *these ideas carry the same weight.*

**Subordination** puts one idea in a dependent clause. It says: *this idea is background, cause, condition or concession; the main clause is the point.* The subordinator names the relationship:

| Relationship | Subordinators |
|---|---|
| Cause | because, since, as |
| Contrast or concession | although, though, while, whereas, even though |
| Condition | if, unless, provided that, only if |
| Time | when, after, before, until, once |

**Placement matters too.** Readers of English give the most weight to the end of a sentence, and to the main clause over any dependent clause. That gives you three basic shapes:

- **Loose (cumulative):** main clause first, then additions. *The town changed slowly, street by street, until no one remembered the mill.* Natural and easy to follow.
- **Periodic:** dependent material first, main clause last. *Because the mill had closed and the young had left, the town changed.* Suspense, then the point.
- **Interrupted:** a phrase or clause set inside the main clause, between subject and verb or verb and complement. It adds a condition or detail and delays what follows, which throws weight on the words after it.

## The text in context

**Occasion.** On July 19 and 20, 1848, about three hundred people met at the Wesleyan Chapel in Seneca Falls, New York, for the first convention called to discuss “the social, civil, and religious condition and rights of woman.” Elizabeth Cady Stanton had drafted a declaration with Lucretia Mott, Martha Wright, Mary Ann McClintock and others. It was read, debated, amended and signed by about one hundred people, sixty-eight women and thirty-two men, Frederick Douglass among them.

**Form.** The drafters chose to follow the Declaration of Independence sentence by sentence: the same opening, a version of the same self-evident truths, and a long list of grievances. Where the 1776 document named the king, this one names “man.” The choice is an argument in itself. Readers who honoured 1776 would have to accept its principles for women or explain why they applied to men only.

**Audience and purpose.** The immediate audience was the convention; the wider one was the American public, the press and the legislatures. The purpose was to state women's grievances as a formal indictment and to demand equal rights as citizens, including the vote. The writers expected ridicule, and they got it: the account in *History of Woman Suffrage* says the proceedings were “unsparingly ridiculed by the press, and denounced by the pulpit.”

**The text.** This course quotes the Declaration as printed in Volume I of *History of Woman Suffrage* (1881), edited by Stanton, Susan B. Anthony and Matilda Joslyn Gage, which is in the public domain in the United States.

## Close reading

### 1. The changed truth

The Declaration's second paragraph begins: “We hold these truths to be self-evident: that all men and women are created equal; that they are endowed by their Creator with certain inalienable rights.” Two words, *and women*, have been added to a sentence every American reader knew. The rest of the sentence's structure is untouched: a colon, then a series of *that* clauses, each a dependent clause naming one truth. The parallel series makes the truths feel like a single, self-evident package, and the new words ride inside it.

### 2. Coordinated grievances: the indictment

Then the list begins:

> He has never permitted her to exercise her inalienable right to the elective franchise.
>
> He has compelled her to submit to laws, in the formation of which she had no voice.
>
> He has withheld from her rights which are given to the most ignorant and degraded men--both natives and foreigners.

Each grievance is an independent clause, most of them printed as their own paragraph, with the same subject and a verb of action. Grammatically they are equals: no grievance is subordinate to another. That is the grammar of an indictment, count after count, and it proves the claim made just before the list, that the history of mankind is “a history of repeated injuries and usurpations on the part of man toward woman.” The repetition of “He has” (anaphora) keeps one agent responsible for every injury.

Notice the small dependent clauses inside these sentences. “In the formation of which she had no voice” is a relative clause attached to *laws*: it is what makes the laws unjust. “Which are given to the most ignorant and degraded men” turns the third grievance into a comparison, appealing (uncomfortably, to a modern reader) to the prejudices of an audience that would find it outrageous for women to rank below such men.

### 3. A cause placed first

One grievance breaks the pattern: “Having deprived her of this first right of a citizen, the elective franchise, thereby leaving her without representation in the halls of legislation, he has oppressed her on all sides.”

The sentence opens with a participial phrase, not with “He has.” The phrase names a prior act (deprivation of the vote) and, with “thereby,” its result (no representation). Only then does the main clause arrive: “he has oppressed her on all sides.” The order of the sentence is the order of cause and effect. The structure argues what the list alone cannot: that the vote is “this first right,” the one from which all the others follow. That was the convention's most disputed claim. The account in *History of Woman Suffrage* reports that the resolution on the franchise was “The only resolution that was not unanimously adopted,” and that Stanton and Douglass carried it “by a small majority.”

### 4. A verdict delayed

“He has made her, if married, in the eye of the law, civilly dead.”

The subject, verb and object come quickly. Then two interrupters, “if married” and “in the eye of the law,” hold the sentence open, and the complement “civilly dead” lands in the last position. The delay builds suspense, and the phrase that ends the sentence is the most shocking in the whole document.

The interrupters also qualify, which links this week to last. Under the common law of *coverture*, a married woman's legal identity was merged with her husband's: she could not own property in her own name, make contracts or sue. “If married” limits the claim to the women of whom it was literally true, and “in the eye of the law” makes clear the death is legal, not physical. The qualified claim is harder to deny than a sweeping one.

### 5. The periodic demand

The list ends, and the writers gather it into one sentence:

> Now, in view of this entire disfranchisement of one-half the people of this country, their social and religious degradation--in view of the unjust laws above mentioned, and because women do feel themselves aggrieved, oppressed, and fraudulently deprived of their most sacred rights, we insist that they have immediate admission to all the rights and privileges which belong to them as citizens of the United States.

Two “in view of” phrases and a “because” clause come before the main clause. This is a periodic sentence. The reasons (a summary of the grievances, then the feeling of the women themselves, in a triple “aggrieved, oppressed, and fraudulently deprived”) arrive first; “we insist” comes only when the case is complete. Note the emphatic *do* in “women do feel”: it answers in advance the claim, common at the time, that women were content.

The final paragraph then turns to the audience's response: “In entering upon the great work before us, we anticipate no small amount of misconception, misrepresentation, and ridicule; but we shall use every instrumentality within our power to effect our object.” The concession (we expect ridicule) sits in the first independent clause and the resolve in the second, after *but*, where it gets the final word.

## From reading to writing: combining and placing clauses

Revision questions on the exam test exactly this. Here is a choppy passage from an invented draft:

> *The bridge closed in March. Deliveries to the market now take an hour longer. Three stalls have shut. The council has no plan to reopen the bridge.*

Every idea is an independent clause, so every idea seems equally important, and the reader has to work out how they connect. Ask three questions: *Which idea matters most to my argument? What is the relationship? Which structure names it?*

> *Since the bridge closed in March, deliveries to the market have taken an hour longer, and three stalls have already shut; yet the council has no plan to reopen it.*

“Since” states cause and time; “and” joins two equal results; the semicolon and “yet” set up the contrast with the council's inaction, which comes last, where the writer wants the reader's indignation to land.

**Repair comma splices with the right relationship.** *Students use the library after school, they have nowhere else to study* has four possible fixes: a period, a semicolon, *, so*, or *because*. Only *because* also tells the reader why: *Students use the library after school because they have nowhere else to study.*

**Concede in the dependent clause; claim in the main clause.** Compare:

> *Although the new schedule saves money, it cuts the only class many seniors need to graduate.*
>
> *Although the new schedule cuts the only class many seniors need to graduate, it saves money.*

The facts are identical; the arguments are opposite. The main clause, at the end, wins.

**Use the periodic sentence sparingly.** One periodic sentence at the climax of a paragraph has force. A paragraph of them exhausts the reader. Loose sentences carry most of an argument; periodic ones deliver its key claims.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing a conjunction by sound.** *However* is not a conjunction; *although* and *because* are not interchangeable. Name the relationship first, then choose the word.
- **Burying your claim in a dependent clause.** If your main point follows *although* or *which*, the sentence argues against you.
- **Stringing ideas with *and*.** Coordination hides cause and effect. One *and* is fine; three is a list pretending to be reasoning.
- **Fragments from over-subordinating.** *Because the bus was late.* is not a sentence. Every dependent clause needs a main clause.
- **Labelling without explaining.** In an analysis, *Stanton uses a periodic sentence* earns nothing by itself. Say what the delay does: the reasons accumulate so the demand lands with their full weight.

## Exam connection

**Section I.** Writing questions often read: *The writer wants to combine sentences 3 and 4 to show...* or *Which version of the underlined portion best...* The right answer uses the conjunction or structure that states the intended relationship and keeps the main idea in the main clause. Reading questions ask what the placement of a phrase or the structure of a sentence “primarily serves to” do; answers that name emphasis, cause and effect, or accumulation are usually in play.

**Section II.** In the **rhetorical analysis** essay, syntax is evidence only when you explain its function: *By opening with the loss of the vote and only then naming the oppression on all sides, the drafters make the sentence's order enact their claim that the franchise is the first right.* In your own **argument** and **synthesis** essays, well-built sentences make your reasoning visible, which is part of what the evidence-and-commentary row rewards, and controlled syntax can contribute to the sophistication point when it serves the argument.

## Self-check

1. What is the difference between an independent and a dependent clause? Give an example of each.
2. What does coordination say about two ideas, and what does subordination say?
3. Why do the Declaration's grievances work as separate independent clauses rather than as one long sentence?
4. In the “Having deprived her” grievance, what relationship does the opening participial phrase establish with the main clause?
5. What two jobs do the interrupters in “He has made her, if married, in the eye of the law, civilly dead” perform?
6. Why is the Declaration's final demand a periodic sentence, and what does the delay achieve?
7. Combine into one sentence that shows the relationships: *The district bought new laptops. Test scores did not change. Teachers received no training.*
8. Rewrite this sentence so that the writer's claim gets the emphasis: *Although uniforms reduce bullying, they cost families money.* (Assume the writer supports uniforms.)
