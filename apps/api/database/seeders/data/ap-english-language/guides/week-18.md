## What you need to be able to do

- **8.A** Choose words, comparisons and sentence structures on purpose, so that your argument has a tone that suits its audience and helps it persuade, and keep that tone consistent.

In plain language: you should be able to take a sentence like *Obviously, anyone with a brain can see the school day starts way too early* and rebuild it so that a sceptical parent would keep reading, and you should be able to say why your version works.

## The skill explained: tone as a persuasive choice

Week 17 treated tone as something to read. This week treats it as something to build, and the first question is always **who must be persuaded.** A tone is right or wrong only for a particular audience and purpose. A rally of supporters can take heat and exclamation; a school board weighing a budget needs a measured, respectful voice; a reader who disagrees with you needs to feel that you have been fair. Choose the tone that keeps *that* reader listening.

You have three sets of tools, the same three you analysed in Johnson.

**Words.** Decide on a **register** (formal, neutral or informal) and keep it; most AP arguments should be neutral to formal. Then choose among near-synonyms by **connotation**: *thrifty* and *frugal* praise, *stingy* and *cheap* blame, though all four mean “spends little.” Prefer **precise** words to vague ones (*a forty-minute bus ride*, not *really far*). Cut most **intensifiers** (*very, really, totally*), which insist instead of showing. And handle **hedges** with care: qualify what your evidence cannot fully prove (*most*, *the survey suggests*), but do not hedge everything into mush (*maybe, I guess, sort of*).

**Comparisons.** An analogy persuades when the reader already accepts its familiar half. *We would never hand someone the car keys without driving lessons* works on almost anyone; the question is whether the comparison is fair to your subject. Keep an extended comparison in one field: if you start with driving, end with the road, not with a recipe.

**Syntax.** Use **parallel structure** for lists and for points you want to sound equal and emphatic. Place a **short sentence** after longer ones when you want a verdict to land. Use a **periodic sentence**, with the main clause held to the end, to build toward a claim. Vary length so that your emphasis means something.

Notice that none of this is decoration. A writer who sneers at the audience, or overclaims, or sounds unsure, loses readers before the evidence arrives. Style is part of the argument.

## The text in context

**Benjamin Franklin** (1706-1790) was a printer, writer, scientist, inventor and diplomat, and one of the most persuasive public figures of the eighteenth century. He began his *Autobiography* in 1771, at the age of sixty-five, as a letter to his son William; he took it up again in 1784-85 and in 1788, and it breaks off around 1757. It was first published, in French, in 1791, the year after his death; the full English text from his manuscript did not appear until 1868.

Two features of its rhetorical situation matter for us. First, the *audience* begins as a son and widens to posterity, so Franklin often writes as a teacher, offering his own life as a set of examples. Second, the *purpose* is partly to explain how an apprentice with two years of schooling became a man of influence, and Franklin's answer keeps returning to writing and speaking. He learned style deliberately, and he tells us how.

The model he imitated was *The Spectator*, a London daily of short essays by Joseph Addison and Richard Steele (1711-1712), famous for clear, polished, conversational prose. For a century, writers learned English style from it.

## Close reading

### 1. A diagnosis: correct is not the same as good

Franklin's father reads the letters his teenage son has been exchanging in a debate, and makes a precise criticism:

> though I had the advantage of my antagonist in correct spelling and pointing (which I ow'd to the printing-house), I fell far short in elegance of expression, in method and in perspicuity

**Observe.** Three faults in parallel: *elegance of expression* (word choice and style), *method* (organization), *perspicuity* (clearness). Spelling and punctuation (“pointing”) are fine.

**Function.** The distinction is the heart of this week. Correctness is necessary, but it does not persuade. The three named faults map onto the skills of this unit: style (7.A, 8.A), organization (5.B) and clarity of reasoning (5.A, 6.A). Franklin's response, “I saw the justice of his remark,” models the attitude of a writer who revises.

### 2. A method: imitation, then comparison

> With this view I took some of the papers, and, making short hints of the sentiment in each sentence, laid them by a few days, and then, without looking at the book, try'd to compleat the papers again, by expressing each hinted sentiment at length, and as fully as it had been expressed before, in any suitable words that should come to hand. Then I compared my Spectator with the original, discovered some of my faults, and corrected them.

**Observe.** Notes, a delay, a rewrite in his own words, a comparison. Each step is in sequence, linked by “and then” and “Then.”

**Function.** The exercise isolates *style* from *content*. Because Franklin keeps Addison's ideas but supplies his own words, every difference between his version and the original is a lesson in word choice and syntax. It is the most practical description of learning 8.A ever written, and you can do it with any paragraph you admire.

### 3. Building a stock of words

When Franklin found that he “wanted a stock of words,” he turned Spectator tales into verse and back again, because verse demands “words of the same import, but of different length” or sound. “Therefore” introduces the remedy: a writer cannot choose the right word without a range to choose from. Then he jumbled his notes and restored their order, which, he says, “was to teach me method in the arrangement of thoughts.” Style and organization were trained separately and on purpose.

His summary is itself a lesson in tone. He fancied that “in certain particulars of small import” he had been “lucky enough to improve the method or the language,” and hoped to become “a tolerable English writer.” Written by the most famous American author of his age, the understatement is disarming. The modest voice is a choice, and it makes us like him.

### 4. A rule: modest diffidence

The second passage is Franklin's best-known advice about persuasion. After years of winning arguments with Socratic questions, he kept only

> the habit of expressing myself in terms of modest diffidence; never using, when I advanced any thing that may possibly be disputed, the words certainly, undoubtedly, or any others that give the air of positiveness to an opinion

Instead he would say “I conceive or apprehend a thing to be so and so; it appears to me, or I should think it so or so, for such and such reasons.”

**Observe.** The rule is about *words*: a short list of forbidden ones and a menu of replacements. Notice “for such and such reasons”: the claim and its reasons stay; only the manner softens.

**Interpret.** Franklin's reason is about audience. “A positive and dogmatical manner in advancing your sentiments may provoke contradiction and prevent a candid attention.” People who feel pushed push back.

**Function.** The paragraph also argues well. Franklin names “the chief ends of conversation” (to inform, to be informed, to please, to persuade) and then shows how a positive manner defeats each in turn, in parallel clauses beginning “if you would inform” and “If you wish information.” And the passage practises its own advice: “This habit, I believe,” “may provoke,” “will probably leave you.” His drily funny warning that sensible listeners “will probably leave you undisturbed in the possession of your error” makes the cost of arrogance concrete.

Later in the *Autobiography* Franklin admits with striking candour that when he added Humility to his list of virtues, he gained little of “the reality of this virtue,” but “a good deal with regard to the appearance of it.” Whatever you think of that confession, it shows how clearly he saw tone as a tool.

## Writing lesson: revising for tone

Here is the opening of a weak speech to a school board.

> *Everyone knows that teenagers are totally clueless about money. The board needs to wake up and add a personal finance class, because honestly the current schedule is a joke.*

The writer has a good proposal and has buried it. “Everyone knows” claims a certainty no one can have. “Totally clueless” insults the very students the speaker represents. “Wake up” and “a joke” insult the board, which is the audience that must say yes. The register is informal for a formal occasion.

Here is a revision.

> *Many of us will graduate without knowing how a credit card charges interest; in a survey of last spring's seniors, fewer than a third could explain it. A one-semester personal finance course would teach students to budget, to save and to read a loan agreement. Some board members have raised a fair concern about a crowded schedule. But the course could take the place of one semester of study hall, time most students now spend without assigned work. We would never hand someone the keys to a car without teaching them to drive. Every June, we hand graduates the keys to their first credit card.*

What changed? The claim is measured (*many of us*) and supported by a number. The list of skills is parallel. The objection is stated fairly before it is answered, as Franklin would advise. The study hall is described precisely and without contempt. The driving comparison is consistent, and the final short sentence turns it into a verdict. Nothing in the revision is less confident than the original; it is confident in a way the board can accept.

A few habits to practise:

- Replace *everyone knows* and *obviously* with evidence.
- Replace insults to opponents with a fair statement of their view.
- Replace intensifiers with a specific fact or a stronger verb.
- Read your draft aloud in the voice of your audience. Wherever you would bristle, revise.

## Common mistakes

- **Mismatched register.** Slang in a formal letter, or stiff jargon in a speech to classmates. Decide on the register first.
- **Tone shifts by accident.** A measured paragraph that suddenly says *which is ridiculous*. Revision questions often test exactly this.
- **Overclaiming.** *Always, never, everyone, obviously.* One counterexample sinks the sentence.
- **Over-hedging.** Hedging every sentence makes you sound unsure of your own case. Qualify what needs qualifying, and state the rest plainly.
- **Mixed comparisons.** Starting with a ship and ending with a garden.
- **Thesaurus diction.** Swapping a plain word for a grand one you do not quite know. Franklin built his stock of words by practice, not by lookup.

## Exam connection

On **Section I**, style revision questions ask you to keep a draft's tone, choose the most precise word, or add a comparison or sentence that suits the writer's purpose. Typical stems: *The writer wants to revise the underlined portion to maintain the tone of the passage*, *Which choice most effectively conveys the writer's concern?*, *The writer wants to add a comparison that emphasizes ...*. Read two or three sentences around the underlined part, name the tone, and eliminate options that are too casual, too extreme or too timid.

On **Section II**, all three essays reward a controlled, consistent voice. In the **argument** essay (Q3), the sophistication point often goes to writing that is “vivid and persuasive,” which means deliberate word choice and syntax in service of the claim. In the **synthesis** essay (Q1), a fair, measured tone toward sources that disagree with you makes your own position more credible. Franklin's rule is a good default: state your claim firmly, state your uncertainties honestly, and never insult the reader you are trying to win.
