# Week 21 · Thesis and Structure in Theodore Roosevelt's "The Strenuous Life"

## What this week is for

Weeks 19 and 20 were about the writer behind an argument: the perspective that shapes what evidence they choose, and the attitude that shows in their tone. This week turns to the argument's spine. By the end of the week you should be able to:

- **3.B** Identify an argument's overarching thesis, state it accurately in your own words, and explain what it signals about the argument's structure.
- Tell a thesis apart from a topic, a purpose and a perspective.
- Describe a thesis fairly even when you disagree with it, and then identify where its reasoning depends on bias rather than evidence.

In plain language: by Friday you should be able to say *Roosevelt claims that a nation, like a man, must choose hard duty over ease, and his speech is built in exactly those two halves*, and point to the sentence where the halves meet.

## The skill: finding the thesis and reading its map

The **overarching thesis** is the claim the whole argument exists to prove. Every paragraph supports it, directly or through a smaller claim. It is easy to confuse with three neighbours:

| | Answers the question | Roosevelt's speech |
|---|---|---|
| **Topic** | What is it about? | National character and foreign policy |
| **Thesis** | What does it claim? | Individuals and the nation should choose effort and hard duty over ease, including duties abroad |
| **Purpose** | What does the writer want to happen? | Win support for an active, expansionist foreign policy |
| **Perspective** | Where does the writer stand? | A war veteran, former navy official and governor |

A quick test: if no reasonable person could disagree, you have found a topic, not a thesis.

Skill 3.B also asks what the thesis **indicates about structure**. Many theses contain a map: paired terms the argument will take up one at a time, a list of reasons in order, or an analogy that splits the argument into halves. Once you see the map, you can predict the route, and multiple-choice questions about how a later paragraph relates to an earlier one become much easier.

Theses do not always come first. A speech usually states its thesis early, because listeners cannot reread; it often **restates** it at the end, in new words, with something added. An essay may delay its thesis (Twain builds his case against Cooper for pages before his verdict) or leave it **implicit**, so that you assemble it from the paragraphs' claims. In every case the test is the same: does every part of the text support the sentence you have written?

## The text in context

In April 1899 Theodore Roosevelt was the governor of New York and one of the most famous men in America. A year earlier he had resigned as Assistant Secretary of the Navy to help lead a volunteer cavalry regiment, the Rough Riders, in the Spanish-American War. The war lasted less than four months. In the peace treaty, ratified in February 1899, Spain gave the United States Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines. By then American troops were already fighting Filipino forces who had fought Spain for their own independence and did not accept American rule.

Americans were divided. **Anti-imperialists**, among them a former president, labour leaders and, from 1900, Mark Twain, argued that governing people without their consent betrayed the Declaration of Independence. Roosevelt spoke for the other side. His audience at the Hamilton Club, a Republican men's club in Chicago, was made up of successful businessmen and professionals: listeners proud of their city and their own hard work, and likely to share his politics.

That situation explains the speech's strategy. Roosevelt does not begin with the Philippines, the controversial part of his argument. He begins with something his listeners already believe about themselves: that work and courage make a man worth respecting. Then he asks them to apply the same standard to the nation.

## Close readings

### 1. A thesis that announces itself

> In speaking to you, men of the greatest city of the West, men of the State which gave to the country Lincoln and Grant, men who pre-eminently and distinctly embody all that is most American in the American character, I wish to preach, not the doctrine of ignoble ease, but the doctrine of the strenuous life, the life of toil and effort, of labor and strife [...]

**The audience first.** The sentence spends almost half its length praising the listeners before it reaches its claim. Linking them to Lincoln and Grant makes the coming doctrine sound like a description of who they already are (skill 1.B from week 5).

**The thesis announced.** *I wish to preach* tells listeners a doctrine is coming. Then the claim arrives as an **antithesis**: *not the doctrine of ignoble ease, but the doctrine of the strenuous life*. One adjective, *ignoble*, makes the rejected choice shameful before any argument is offered (skill 7.A from week 20). A listener knows the speaker's position after one sentence.

### 2. The map

> A life of slothful ease, a life of that peace which springs merely from lack either of desire or of power to strive after great things, is as little worthy of a nation as of an individual.

**The preview.** *As little worthy of a nation as of an individual* names the two levels the argument will address. The paragraphs that follow are about individuals: the man who works, the rich man's son who must not waste his leisure, the maxim *It is hard to fail, but it is worse never to have tried to succeed.* The listener who accepts all of that has been prepared for the second half.

### 3. The hinge

> As it is with the individual, so it is with the nation. It is a base untruth to say that happy is the nation that has no history. Thrice happy is the nation that has a glorious history.

**Structure made visible.** The first sentence is a one-line transition that fulfils the preview: the personal half closes, the national half opens. The balanced shape (*as it is ... so it is*) makes the analogy sound like a law of nature rather than a claim that needs defending, and that is worth noticing. Whether a nation really is like a person is exactly what an opponent would question.

**The image of the alternative.** A few lines later, people who avoid risk *live in the gray twilight that knows not victory nor defeat*. The comparison makes the opposing position look not dangerous but dim and lifeless, a judgement delivered through imagery rather than evidence.

### 4. Signposts and the restated thesis

Later the speech marks its turns aloud. After arguing that expansion serves trade, Roosevelt says, *So much for the commercial side*, and adds that *From the standpoint of international honor the argument is even stronger*. The signpost tells listeners where they are and that the reasons climb from weaker to stronger.

> I preach to you, then, my countrymen, that our country calls not for the life of ease but for the life of strenuous endeavor.

**The frame.** The verb *preach* returns from the first sentence, and so does the ease/strenuous antithesis. What is new is the stakes: if Americans *seek merely swollen, slothful ease and ignoble peace*, then *the bolder and stronger peoples will pass us by*. A restated thesis usually repeats the claim and adds a reason to care.

## Perspective and bias: describing fairly, then judging

To answer a 3.B question you must state Roosevelt's thesis in terms he would accept. A hostile paraphrase (*Roosevelt wants war*) is usually a wrong answer, because it describes a claim he does not make. Describe first; evaluate second.

When you do evaluate, this unit's vocabulary helps. Roosevelt's **perspective**, as a veteran of the war he is defending and a leader of the party that ratified the treaty, explains his confidence and his stake. **Bias** is visible where the speech's claims rest on assumptions rather than evidence. Later in the speech he describes many Filipinos as unfit for self-government and calls the alternative to American control *savage anarchy*; he assigns women the single role of mothers of many children; and he dismisses opponents' appeals to liberty and the consent of the governed as timidity. None of these claims is supported, and the people they describe never speak. A careful reader can see how well the speech is built and also see what it leaves out. Both observations belong in a strong analysis.

## From reading to writing: turning a topic into a thesis

Skill 4.B, writing a thesis, returns in full next week, but reading Roosevelt is a good moment to practise the first step. Here is an invented introduction that has a topic but no thesis:

> *Since the beginning of time, people have helped each other. Many high schools now require community service. Our district is considering a requirement of forty hours. Community service is a topic that has many sides. In this essay I will talk about community service.*

The last two sentences announce a subject. Ask three questions: *What exactly do I claim? Could someone reasonably disagree? What reasons will I give, and in what order?* The answers produce a thesis:

> *The district should adopt the forty-hour requirement, because service teaches skills no classroom can and because a requirement reaches the students who would never volunteer on their own.*

Like Roosevelt's, this thesis takes a side and contains a map: two reasons, in the order the body paragraphs will follow. Next week we will add a third element, acknowledging another perspective without giving up the position.

## Common mistakes

- **Mistaking the topic for the thesis.** *The speech is about the strenuous life* names a subject. Say what it claims.
- **Mistaking the purpose for the thesis.** *Roosevelt wants support for expansion* is his goal; the thesis is the claim he offers to get it.
- **Stopping at the first sentence.** Check your statement of the thesis against the middle and the end; a thesis must fit the whole.
- **Paraphrasing unfairly.** Exam answers describe what the writer claims, in terms the writer would accept.
- **Treating perspective as proof of bias.** Roosevelt's service in the war is a perspective. Bias is shown by what his claims ignore or assume.
- **Missing the map.** Paired terms, analogies and lists in a thesis tell you how the argument is organized. Use them.

## Exam connection

Reading questions on skill 3.B appear in almost every Section I passage set: *Which of the following best states the writer's thesis?*; *The final paragraph relates to the first chiefly by...*; *The sentence in line N serves primarily to...* (often a hinge or a signpost). Distractors are typically a topic, a purpose, a detail, or a thesis stated too broadly or too narrowly.

In Section II, the **rhetorical analysis essay** asks you to explain how a writer builds an argument. Students who identify the thesis and then trace how the structure serves it, as with Roosevelt's preview, hinge and frame, write more coherent essays than students who list devices in the order they appear. Your own thesis in that essay should name the writer's thesis or purpose and the main strategies you will analyse.

## Self-check

1. State Roosevelt's thesis in one sentence he would accept. Then state his purpose and his perspective, each in one sentence.
2. Which phrase in the second paragraph previews the structure of the speech? What does it predict?
3. Why might Roosevelt begin with the individual rather than with the Philippines? What does the order achieve with this audience?
4. Explain what *So much for the commercial side* tells listeners about the order of his reasons.
5. Identify one claim in the speech that rests on bias rather than evidence, and explain how you can tell.
6. Write a thesis for your own argument about whether rest or effort matters more for students, and make it preview two reasons.
