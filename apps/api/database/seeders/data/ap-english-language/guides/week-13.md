# Week 13 · Occasion and Audience in Emerson's “The American Scholar”

## What this week is for

Last week you saw that an introduction and a conclusion must be *appropriate* to their rhetorical situation. This week asks what, exactly, they must be appropriate to. The answer is the full set of parts the CED names in skill 1.A: **exigence, audience, writer, purpose, context and message.** We add one practical term the CED folds into context: the **occasion**, the event and setting at which an argument is delivered.

Emerson's “The American Scholar” is ideal for this because it is a speech made for one room on one day. You can see the speaker reading the occasion in the first sentence and returning to it in the last paragraph. By Friday you should be able to:

- describe each part of a text's rhetorical situation precisely, with evidence from the text (1.A);
- explain how an opening and a closing are shaped by the occasion and the audience (2.A);
- revise a student's speech so that its first and last words fit the people who will hear them.

## The skill explained: reading the room

Skill 1.A asks you to **identify and describe**. Identifying is naming; describing is saying something specific. *The audience is people* identifies nothing. *The audience is the members of an academic honour society at their annual meeting, educated men who prize learning but look to Europe for it* describes. The more precisely you describe a rhetorical situation, the more of a writer's choices you can explain.

Here is how the six parts relate:

| Part | Question to ask | In “The American Scholar” |
|---|---|---|
| Writer (speaker) | Who is speaking, and how does the audience see them? | Emerson, 34, a former Unitarian minister and Harvard graduate, known as a lecturer and the author of *Nature* (1836) |
| Audience | Who is listening or reading, and what do they value? | The Phi Beta Kappa Society at Cambridge, Harvard graduates, faculty and guests |
| Context | What is the wider setting of time, place and culture? | A young, commercial nation in 1837, a year of financial panic, whose learned class read and imitated Europe |
| Exigence | What problem or need calls for this argument now? | American thought's dependence on foreign learning, and a busy people's neglect of letters |
| Purpose | What does the speaker want the audience to think or do? | To redefine the scholar as Man Thinking and to call American scholars to trust themselves |
| Message | What, in brief, is said? | The scholar is educated by nature, books and action, and must now speak for a nation that thinks for itself |

The **occasion** connects all six. A Phi Beta Kappa oration was a ceremonial event, part of Harvard's commencement season. Ceremonial speech, which the Greeks called *epideictic*, usually praises shared values. An audience at such an event expects to be honoured. Emerson honours them, and then asks more of them than they expected.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Occasion | The event and setting at which an argument is delivered. |
| Context | The wider historical and cultural situation around a text. |
| Epideictic rhetoric | Ceremonial speech that praises or blames, often affirming shared values. |
| Salutation | The formal address that opens a speech or letter. |
| Inclusive pronoun | “We,” “our,” “us,” used to place the speaker inside the audience's group. |
| Hedge | A qualifier (“perhaps,” “in a great measure”) that softens a claim. |
| Frame | A conclusion that returns to the opening's words or image. |
| Pledge | A conclusion phrased as a shared promise (“we will”). |

## The text in context

On August 31, 1837, Ralph Waldo Emerson rose to give the annual oration of the Phi Beta Kappa Society in Cambridge, Massachusetts. He was not the society's first choice that year; he was asked late. He had left the ministry five years before, travelled in Europe, and begun a career as a public lecturer. His first book, *Nature*, had appeared the year before.

The audience was the intellectual establishment of New England: Harvard graduates and faculty, ministers, lawyers, and the year's new members of the society. Many had been trained on European models and quoted European authorities. The country, meanwhile, was busy with commerce and, that summer, shaken by a financial panic. American writers were still widely regarded, at home and abroad, as imitators of British ones.

The speech argues that the true scholar is not a bookworm but **Man Thinking**, formed by three influences (nature, books used rightly, and action), and that the American scholar must now trust his own mind. Oliver Wendell Holmes, in his biography of Emerson almost fifty years later, called it the country's intellectual Declaration of Independence. That label is a later reader's, but it names the exigence well.

## Close reading

### 1. The greeting: honouring the ritual

> I greet you on the recommencement of our literary year. Our anniversary is one of hope, and, perhaps, not enough of labor.

**Observe.** The first sentence ties the speech to the society's calendar (“our literary year”). The second balances praise (“hope”) against criticism (“not enough of labor”), with a hedge (“perhaps”) between them. Both sentences use “our.”

**Interpret.** Emerson speaks as a member. The criticism is real but softened, and because it is “our” anniversary, he shares the blame.

**Function.** A ceremonial audience expects to be honoured, and Emerson honours them. But the second sentence plants the challenge that the speech will develop. This is skill 1.B (understanding an audience's values) operating inside skill 2.A (an opening fitted to its situation).

### 2. Defining the occasion by contrast

> We do not meet for games of strength or skill, for the recitation of histories, tragedies, and odes, like the ancient Greeks; for parliaments of love and poesy, like the Troubadours; nor for the advancement of science, like our contemporaries in the British and European capitals.

**Observe.** A long sentence lists three kinds of gathering the society is not: athletic and poetic festivals, courts of love, scientific academies.

**Interpret.** Each comparison is to a culture that *did* something with its gatherings. The American meeting is left, by contrast, as “simply a friendly sign of the survival of the love of letters amongst a people too busy to give to letters any more.”

**Function.** The contrast defines the occasion and diminishes it in one move. Having shown how little the meeting has done, Emerson can propose that “the time is already come, when it ought to be, and will be, something else.” The occasion itself becomes part of the exigence.

### 3. The exigence announced

> Our day of dependence, our long apprenticeship to the learning of other lands, draws to a close. The millions, that around us are rushing into life, cannot always be fed on the sere remains of foreign harvests.

**Observe.** Two images of dependence, one of labour (apprenticeship) and one of food (the sere, or withered, remains of foreign harvests). “Draws to a close” announces an ending.

**Interpret.** America's intellectual life has been an apprentice's life, learning a trade from a European master, and its readers have been fed on leftovers.

**Function.** Here the ceremonial greeting becomes a historical claim. The audience learns why this year's oration matters more than a usual anniversary speech. Notice that the exigence is stated in the first paragraph, but the thesis is not: the introduction tells the audience what problem the speech answers before telling them the answer.

### 4. Accepting the topic from custom

> In this hope, I accept the topic which not only usage, but the nature of our association, seem to prescribe to this day,--the AMERICAN SCHOLAR.

**Observe.** Emerson says he “accepts” a topic that custom (“usage”) and the society's nature “prescribe.”

**Interpret.** The phrasing makes his subject sound inevitable: the society of scholars meets, so the speaker speaks of the scholar.

**Function.** A radical argument arrives dressed as the expected one. The audience is invited to hear what follows as its own tradition renewed, not as an outsider's attack. That is how a skilled speaker fits a challenging message to a ceremonial occasion.

### 5. The conclusion: from Gentlemen to brothers

Near the end, Emerson repeats the opening salutation, a signal that the abstract discussion of the scholar is over:

> Mr. President and Gentlemen, this confidence in the unsearched might of man belongs, by all motives, by all prophecy, by all preparation, to the American Scholar. We have listened too long to the courtly muses of Europe.

And the final paragraph changes the address again:

> Not so, brothers and friends,--please God, ours shall not be so. We will walk on our own feet; we will work with our own hands; we will speak our own minds.

**Observe.** The formal “Gentlemen” becomes “brothers and friends.” Three parallel clauses begin “we will.”

**Interpret.** The listeners are no longer an audience to be addressed but partners in a vow. The images (feet, hands, minds) answer the opening's “day of dependence”: an apprentice becomes a master.

**Function.** The conclusion frames the speech. It returns to the occasion (the salutation), completes the exigence (dependence ends) and releases the audience with a pledge. The last sentence widens the stakes once more: “A nation of men will for the first time exist, because each believes himself inspired by the Divine Soul which also inspires all men.”

## From reading to writing: a speech fitted to its room

A senior is giving a three-minute speech at an assembly welcoming ninth graders. Here is a weak draft opening:

> *Good morning, ninth graders and teachers. My name is Jordan, and I am a senior. According to a survey, a lot of students say high school is stressful. Today I want to talk to you about clubs.*

What goes wrong: the vague survey adds anxiety to an audience that needs reassurance; the purpose is announced as a topic (“talk to you about clubs”), not a claim; nothing yet connects the speaker to the listeners.

A revision fitted to the occasion:

> *Good morning, ninth graders and teachers. My name is Jordan, and four years ago I sat where you are sitting, sure that no one would notice if I disappeared into the back row. Then a friend dragged me to a debate club meeting, and I have not stopped arguing since. The fastest way to stop feeling invisible here is to join one club this month, even if you join it by accident the way I did.*

What works: the speaker's experience builds common ground (writer and audience); the thesis speaks to the audience's likely fear and asks for one manageable action (purpose); the occasion, the first week of school, is present in “this month.”

A weak closing:

> *To sum up, clubs are good. The club fair is this Friday after school in the gym. Thank you for listening.*

A revision:

> *The club fair is this Friday after school in the gym, and more than forty clubs will be there. Thank you for listening. Four years from now, one of you will be standing up here, and I hope you will have a club to thank.*

The revision cuts the empty summary, keeps the concrete next step, puts the courtesy before the last line and ends on a frame: the speaker once sat in the audience, and a listener will one day stand on the stage.

## Common mistakes

- **Describing the audience vaguely.** “The audience is Americans” explains nothing. Say who, where, when and what they value.
- **Confusing occasion and exigence.** The occasion is why people have gathered; the exigence is why this argument is needed.
- **Treating the speaker as neutral.** Who speaks shapes what can be said. Emerson could challenge Harvard men because he was one.
- **Calling every opening “a hook.”** Name what the opening does: honours the occasion, defines by contrast, states the exigence, concedes.
- **Ending a speech on a routine courtesy.** Thank the audience before the last line, not as the last line.
- **Forgetting that speeches are heard.** A listener cannot reread, so a spoken thesis must be short and clear.

## Exam connection

Section I often opens a reading set with a question about the rhetorical situation: “The speaker's audience is most likely...” or “The opening sentence primarily serves to...” Answer from evidence in the passage: forms of address, pronouns, references to an occasion or a date. When the passage is a speech, look for how the speaker treats the listeners at the start and the end.

In the rhetorical analysis essay (Q2), the prompt's introduction usually describes the speaker, audience and occasion. Use that information. A thesis that links a writer's choices to a specific audience and purpose (for example, *Emerson honours a ceremonial audience in order to challenge it*) is stronger than one that lists devices. Commentary that explains *why this choice suits these listeners* is what moves an essay up the evidence-and-commentary scale.
