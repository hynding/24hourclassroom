## The skill: four tests for a thesis

A strong thesis passes four tests.

1. **It makes a claim.** Not a topic (*phones in school*), not an announcement (*in this essay I will discuss...*), not a question.
2. **It requires proof.** A reasonable, informed person could disagree. Facts (*Addams founded Hull-House in 1889*) fail this test because no one disputes them.
3. **It can be proved in the space you have.** A thesis that is **too broad** (*technology is ruining education everywhere*) promises more than any essay can deliver. Limit it by place, group, time or degree.
4. **It points the way.** A thesis that previews structure names its reasons in the order the body paragraphs will take them up. The CED says a thesis *may* preview structure; on a timed essay, it almost always should, because it doubles as your outline.

Then the unit's addition: **perspective**. The strongest arguments know what the other side sees. A **qualified thesis** builds that knowledge in:

> *Although [the other perspective's strongest point], [your position], because [your reasons].*

The concession makes the claim more precise and more credible, provided the main clause is still yours. If the sentence ends *so both sides have a point*, you have described the debate instead of joining it.

## The text in context

Jane Addams (1860–1935) co-founded Hull-House in 1889 in a crowded immigrant neighbourhood on Chicago's West Side. It was a **settlement house**: educated, mostly young residents lived there among their neighbours and ran a nursery, clubs, classes and a gymnasium. Addams spent the rest of her life there, wrote many books and received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1931.

*Democracy and Social Ethics* (1902) grew from her lectures. Each chapter tests one relationship of daily life against the standard of democracy: charity, family, household employment, industry, education and politics. In "Charitable Effort" she examines the **charity visitor**, typically a young college woman sent by a charitable society to advise a poor family and decide whether it deserved help. Many of Addams's readers had been such visitors or had paid for them.

Her **perspective** is unusual and essential to the argument. She belongs by education and class to the benefactors, and she writes to them as *we*. But she has lived for years beside the families they visit, so she can describe the charitable relation from both sides. Her **purpose** is to persuade her own class that its methods, not its intentions, are the problem.

## Close readings

### 1. A thesis built of two claims

> Probably there is no relation in life which our democracy is changing more rapidly than the charitable relation--that relation which obtains between benefactor and beneficiary; at the same time there is no point of contact in our modern experience which reveals so clearly the lack of that equality which democracy implies.

**The claims.** Two parallel superlatives, joined by *at the same time*: no relation is changing faster, and no relation shows inequality more clearly. Each half is arguable, and together they map the chapter: the first pages trace the change, the rest show the inequality it exposes.

**The qualifier.** Notice the first word, *Probably*. Addams opens her thesis with a modest hedge and then makes a very large claim. The hedge is honest (she cannot survey every relation in life) and strategic: it makes her sound measured to readers she is about to criticise.

**The consequence.** The next sentence draws the stakes: *the complacency of the old-fashioned charitable man is gone forever*. A thesis often works best when it says not only what is true but what follows.

### 2. Evidence of change

> Formerly when it was believed that poverty was synonymous with vice and laziness, and that the prosperous man was the righteous man, charity was administered harshly with a good conscience [...]

The *formerly* paragraph is the evidence for the first half of the thesis. Once the belief that poverty meant vice has gone, the harsh methods lose their justification, but, Addams argues, the methods have not changed as fast as the beliefs. That gap between what we believe and what we still do is the chapter's engine.

### 3. Irony aimed at her own side

> The daintily clad charitable visitor who steps into the little house made untidy by the vigorous efforts of her hostess, the washerwoman, is no longer sure of her superiority to the latter; she recognizes that her hostess after all represents social value and industrial use, as over against her own parasitic cleanliness and a social standing attained only through status.

**Tone.** *Daintily clad* and *parasitic cleanliness* turn the visitor's respectability upside down: her neat clothes depend on others' labour, while the untidy house is evidence of work. The irony is gentle and self-critical (skill 7.A from week 20). Addams is describing people like herself and her readers, and that is why the irony persuades rather than alienates.

### 4. Two standards, both real

> The neighborhood mind is at once confronted not only by the difference of method, but by an absolute clashing of two ethical standards.

**Perspective taken seriously.** Addams does not say the poor misunderstand charity. She says they hold a different *ethical standard*: help a neighbour at once, without questions, as the families she describes do when they take in a widow and her five children. By that standard, the visitor's investigation looks like *the cold and calculating action of a selfish man*. By the visitor's standard, the neighbours' generosity looks careless. Both sides misread the other.

This is the model for a qualified thesis. Addams grants the visitor *the best desire in the world to help* and grants the neighbours genuine kindness, then claims something neither side sees alone: the relation itself, not the people in it, has become untenable under democracy.

## From reading to writing: drafting and revising a thesis

Here is the process in five steps, worked on an invented example. A district plans to replace printed textbooks with tablets. A student's first draft ends its introduction like this:

> *Digital textbooks are a really stupid idea. There are many opinions about textbooks.*

**Step 1: Find the claim.** The first sentence has a position buried in an insult; the second has none. Strip the loaded word and say what you claim about the specific question: *The district should keep printed textbooks for core courses.*

**Step 2: Test it.** Could someone disagree? Yes: supporters point to cost and weight. Can an essay prove it? Yes, if it is limited to core courses and to this district.

**Step 3: Add the reasons, in order.** The student's plan has three body paragraphs: reading on screens, students without home internet, the cost of repairing tablets. The thesis should name them in that order:

> *The district should keep printed textbooks for core courses, because students read less carefully on screens, because tablets leave students without home internet behind, and because repairing and replacing tablets would erase the promised savings.*

**Step 4: Acknowledge the strongest other perspective.** The supporters' best point is cost and convenience. Name it fairly and answer it:

> *Although digital books are cheaper to update and easier to carry, the district should keep printed textbooks for core courses, because the savings do not outweigh the cost to how students read.*

You may not have room for both the full preview and the concession in one sentence. On a timed essay, a two-sentence thesis is fine: concession and position in the first, reasons in the second.

**Step 5: Check the tone.** *Really stupid* would cost the writer every board member who supports tablets. *The pilot's results suggest that the plan would cost students more than it saves the district* holds the same position in words the audience can accept (skill 8.A).

### One thesis, three essays

The four tests apply to every Section II essay, but each prompt asks for a slightly different claim.

- **Synthesis (Q1).** A position on the prompt's question, in your own words. The sources are evidence for your claim; do not make your thesis a list of what each source says.
- **Rhetorical analysis (Q2).** A claim about how the writer's choices serve their purpose for their audience. Weak: *Addams uses ethos, pathos and logos.* Strong: *Writing to readers like herself, Addams persuades the charitable class to question its methods by turning its symbols of virtue into signs of privilege and by presenting the poor's opposing standard as a genuine ethic.*
- **Argument (Q3).** A defensible position on the issue (agree, disagree or qualify) with the reasons that will organise the essay. *Qualify* means a position with conditions, not a refusal to choose.

## Common mistakes

- **The announcement.** *In this essay I will discuss...* earns no thesis point. Say what you claim.
- **The restated prompt.** Copying the prompt's question as a statement takes no position.
- **Both sides, no side.** *There are good and bad things about phones* describes a debate. Join it.
- **The device list.** In rhetorical analysis, naming appeals or devices without linking them to purpose is not a thesis.
- **The broken promise.** A thesis that previews reasons in a different order from the body paragraphs misleads the reader. Revise one or the other.
- **The concession that swallows the claim.** If your *although* clause is longer and stronger than your main clause, the reader will remember the other side.
- **The insult.** Loaded words make the thesis sound like a verdict before the trial.

## Exam connection

On every Section II essay, the first rubric row is the **thesis point** (0–1). It requires a defensible thesis that responds to the prompt; it can appear anywhere, but readers look first at the end of the introduction. A thesis that previews your reasoning also makes the evidence-and-commentary row easier to earn, because each body paragraph has a job announced in advance. And a thesis that engages another perspective is one of the clearest routes to the **sophistication point**, which rewards writers who explain the complexities or tensions in an issue.

On the multiple-choice section, writing questions on skill 4.B give you a draft and ask which sentence *best states the writer's position*, *most effectively previews the structure of the argument*, or *best revises the thesis to acknowledge an opposing view*. Eliminate facts, announcements and statements that describe the debate without joining it.
