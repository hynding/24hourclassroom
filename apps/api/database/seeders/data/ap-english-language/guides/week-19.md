## What this week is for

Unit 6 adds three words to your vocabulary for reading arguments: **position**, **perspective** and **bias**. By the end of the week you should be able to:

- **3.A** Identify a writer's claims and the evidence offered for each, and explain how a particular piece of evidence supports a particular claim.
- **4.A** Write a body paragraph in which a claim is supported by evidence that is introduced with its source and explained in your own commentary.
- Tell a writer's **position** (what they argue) from their **perspective** (where they argue from), and decide when a perspective has become **bias**.

In plain language: by Friday you should be able to say *Wells claims that the stated reason for lynching is a pretext, and she proves it with her opponents' own numbers*, and then write a paragraph that makes that kind of point about any text.

A note before you read. *Southern Horrors* is about lynching: the murder of Black Americans by mobs, usually without trial, which killed thousands of people between the 1880s and the 1930s. Wells describes real cases. In this course we study her pamphlet as an argument, and the guide quotes her claims and her evidence, not her accounts of violence. If the subject is hard for you, that is a reasonable response; talk to your teacher, and focus on the question this week asks: *how does a writer use evidence to defeat a justification that many people believe?*

## The skill: position, perspective, bias

**Position** is the claim a writer defends. Wells's position is that lynching must end and that the reason usually given for it is false.

**Perspective** is the vantage point from which a writer sees an issue: experience, identity, profession, community, values. Wells writes as a Black woman born into slavery in 1862, as a teacher and journalist, and, in 1892, as an editor whose newspaper had just been destroyed by a mob. That perspective gives her knowledge others lacked and a personal stake in the outcome.

The two are different things. Two writers can share a perspective and take opposite positions; two writers with very different perspectives can arrive at the same position. On the exam, a question that asks about a writer's *perspective* wants the vantage point, not the thesis.

**Bias** is a perspective that distorts. Every writer has a perspective; a writer is biased when that perspective leads them to ignore, suppress or misrepresent evidence. Calling a source biased is therefore a claim, and like any claim it needs evidence: what did the source leave out, exaggerate or assume?

**Claims and evidence**, the subject of weeks 2–4, are how you test all of this. A claim is an arguable statement; evidence is what the writer offers to make the reader accept it. AP passages use four kinds of evidence again and again: statistics and data; specific cases; testimony from witnesses or experts; and documents or authorities. For each, ask two questions: *where does it come from*, and *why would this audience believe it?*

## The text in context

In March 1892 three Black men who ran a successful grocery store in Memphis, Tennessee, were taken from jail and lynched after a quarrel with a white competitor. Ida B. Wells knew them. In May she published an editorial in her paper, the *Free Speech*, that challenged the reason Southern newspapers routinely gave for lynching: that it punished Black men for assaulting white women. While she was travelling in the North, a meeting of white businessmen was called, the paper's office was destroyed, and she was warned that she would be harmed if she came home.

She stayed in New York and wrote. A long article, "Exiled," appeared in the *New York Age* in June 1892; in October, with money raised by Black women's clubs in New York and Brooklyn, it was expanded into the pamphlet *Southern Horrors*. Its readers included Black communities in the North and white Northerners who had accepted the Southern explanation or simply said nothing.

That rhetorical situation shapes every choice in the pamphlet. Wells's **exigence** is the destruction of her paper and the murders that prompted her editorial. Her **audience** includes readers who might be inclined to dismiss a Black woman writing in exile as partisan. Her **purpose** is to replace a widely believed justification with facts, and to move readers to act. So she announces in her preface that her pamphlet is

> a contribution to truth, an array of facts, the perusal of which it is hoped will stimulate this great American Republic to demand that justice be done though the heavens fall.

She presents herself as a reporter, not an orator. *Judge me by my facts* is the implicit contract, and the rest of the pamphlet keeps it.

## Close readings

### 1. The claim inside a metaphor

> To palliate this record (which grows worse as the Afro-American becomes intelligent) and excuse some of the most heinous crimes that ever stained the history of a country, the South is shielding itself behind the plausible screen of defending the honor of its women.

**The claim.** A *screen* hides what stands behind it, and *plausible* concedes that it looks convincing. In one short phrase Wells states her overarching claim: the reason given for lynching is a cover for something else. The verbs agree with the metaphor. To *palliate* is to make something look less bad than it is; *shielding* is what someone does who expects to be attacked.

**The second claim.** The parenthesis, which a fast reader skips, carries a second claim: the violence *grows worse* as Black Americans gain education and independence. If that is true, lynching looks like a response to progress, not to crime. Wells will spend her chapters on the Memphis press and on the People's Grocery developing exactly that point. Writers often tuck a supporting claim into a parenthesis or a subordinate clause; find it, and ask how it connects to the thesis.

### 2. Evidence the opponent cannot easily dismiss

> These statistics compiled by the Chicago Tribune were given the first of this year (1892). [...] This, too, in the face of the fact that only one-third of the 728 victims to mobs have been charged with rape, to say nothing of those of that one-third who were innocent of the charge.

**Where the evidence comes from.** The figure of 728 people lynched in eight years is not Wells's own count. It comes from the *Chicago Tribune*, a major white-owned daily that no reader could accuse of writing for her side. That is a choice based on the audience's beliefs (skill 1.B from week 5): the more doubtful the reader, the more a writer gains from evidence the reader already trusts.

**What the evidence proves.** A number on its own shows scale. Wells makes the number *argue* by comparing it with another: only a third of the victims were even *charged* with the crime used to justify lynching. If the stated purpose of lynching were to punish that crime, it would have to explain most of the cases; it explains a minority, and even that minority includes innocent people. The statistic becomes a refutation. Notice also what she concedes: she accepts the Tribune's count rather than inflating it. Accuracy is part of her persuasive strategy.

### 3. Silence as evidence, and as a claim

> This cry has had its effect. It has closed the heart, stifled the conscience, warped the judgment and hushed the voice of press and pulpit on the subject of lynch law throughout this "land of liberty."

**Syntax and tone.** Four parallel verb phrases move through feeling, morality, thought and speech, so the damage seems complete; the sentence lands on *press and pulpit*, the two institutions that should have spoken. The quotation marks around the patriotic phrase are scare quotes: Wells hands the nation's name for itself back to it as a claim the facts refute. This is skill 7.A from week 17, and it previews week 20's work on tone.

**The claim.** The next sentences call respected men *cowards* and argue that their *silent acquiescence* lets lynching spread *over the whole country*. Later in the pamphlet she uses a legal term for the same idea: those who disapprove but stay silent are *particeps criminis*, partners in the crime. Here the absence of protest is itself part of her evidence that the justification works, and her claim is that it must stop working.

### 4. Letting the other side speak

In the chapter "The Malicious and Untruthful White Press," Wells reprints long editorials from the Memphis *Commercial* and *Evening Scimitar* in full. We do not quote them here, because their language is the language of the mob; you can read them in the pamphlet. What matters for this week is her method. She does not paraphrase her opponents, which would invite the charge that she had distorted them. She lets them speak, then sets beside their words the facts of particular cases, such as the three grocers whom the press described as "toughs" and "Negro desperadoes," men who were in fact *peaceful, law-abiding citizens and energetic business men*. Of the mob's own explanation she writes:

> "The Negroes are getting too independent," they say, "we must teach them a lesson."
>
> What lesson? The lesson of subordination.

Her reply is six words long, because her opponents have already done the arguing for her. This is evidence in its most powerful form: an admission from the side that has every reason to deny it.

## From reading to writing: a claim-and-evidence paragraph from sources you do not fully trust

Wells had no neutral sources. Every newspaper in Memphis was hostile to her, and she knew her own perspective would be questioned. Her solution is a model for your own paragraphs (skill 4.A): **use a source's perspective as part of the evidence.**

The pattern has four moves:

1. **Claim.** An arguable statement that the evidence can prove.
2. **Source and perspective.** Name where the evidence comes from and, when it matters, the source's stake in the issue.
3. **Evidence.** The fact, figure or quotation, short and precise.
4. **Commentary.** Explain how the evidence proves the arguable part of the claim, and why the source's perspective makes it more (or less) convincing.

Here is a weak paragraph on an invented topic, an argument that a town should repair a footbridge:

> *The footbridge over the creek is dangerous and should be fixed. A memo said it failed its safety inspection. This shows it is unsafe. Lots of people use the bridge every day, and it is also old and not very attractive.*

The claim is fine, but the evidence floats: *a memo* from whom? The commentary repeats the evidence (*this shows it is unsafe*), and the last sentence wanders into appearance, which is not the point.

A stronger version:

> *The footbridge is unsafe now, not at some point in the future, so its repair cannot wait for next year's budget. Even the council members who voted against the repair concede, in their own memo, that the bridge failed its most recent safety inspection. That admission carries unusual weight: its authors had every reason to describe the bridge as sound, and they did not. If the people most opposed to spending the money accept that the bridge has failed, the only question left is how long the town is willing to keep it open.*

What changed: the claim specifies its arguable point (*now*, *cannot wait*); the evidence is introduced with its source and that source's perspective; the commentary explains why this perspective strengthens the evidence; the final sentence pushes the reasoning forward instead of restating it.

The same move works in reverse. When a source's perspective *weakens* its evidence, say so: *the figures come from the company that makes the product, which has an obvious interest in favourable results.* Naming a perspective is not an insult; it tells the reader how much weight the evidence can bear.

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing perspective with position.** *Wells believes lynching is wrong* is her position. *Wells writes as an exiled Black editor who knew the murdered grocers* is her perspective.
- **Calling a source biased without evidence.** Bias is a claim. Show what the source omits or distorts, as Wells does when she sets the press's labels beside the facts of a case.
- **Assuming that a writer with a personal stake must be wrong.** Wells's perspective gave her urgency, but her case rests on documents a hostile reader could check.
- **Listing evidence without saying what it proves.** *Wells uses statistics* is an observation. *Wells uses the Tribune's figures because her doubtful readers could not dismiss them, and the one-third fraction turns those figures against the South's excuse* is analysis.
- **Quoting the offensive material.** In your own writing about a text like this one, describe the hostile editorials and analyse their method; you do not need to reproduce their slurs to show that you understand them.
- **Treating evidence as interchangeable.** A statistic, a case and a quotation do different jobs. Wells puts a number (scale) beside a case (a human face) beside an admission (motive).

## Exam connection

On the multiple-choice section, reading questions on claims and evidence (skill 3.A) usually take one of these forms: *The writer's central claim in the second paragraph is that...*; *The reference to X primarily serves to...*; *Which of the following best describes the function of the statistic in line N?* The right answer almost always names what the evidence *proves* within the argument, not just what it says.

Writing questions on skill 4.A give you a student draft and ask which sentence best supports a claim, which sentence should be deleted, or which revision best explains the evidence. Test every option against the *arguable part* of the claim.

In Section II, the **argument essay** (Q3) and the **synthesis essay** (Q1) both reward a writer who introduces evidence with its source and explains why it is convincing. In synthesis especially, noticing a source's perspective (who wrote it, for whom, with what stake) is one of the clearest ways to earn the sophistication point, because it shows you are weighing sources rather than stacking them.

## Self-check

1. In one sentence each, state Wells's position and describe her perspective. How are they connected, and how are they different?
2. Why does Wells attribute her statistics to the *Chicago Tribune*? What would she lose if she presented the same numbers as her own count?
3. Explain how the fraction *only one-third* turns a statistic into a refutation.
4. Find one claim Wells places in a parenthesis or subordinate clause. How does it connect to her thesis?
5. What is the difference between calling a source's perspective relevant and calling the source biased? Give an example of each from the pamphlet.
6. Rewrite the weak footbridge paragraph using a different piece of evidence, introduced with its source's perspective.
