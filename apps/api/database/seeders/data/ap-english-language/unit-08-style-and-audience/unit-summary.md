# Unit 8 Summary · Style for an Audience

Four weeks, four very different voices, and one question underneath them all: how does a writer shape words, comparisons and sentences for the people who will hear them? Roosevelt spoke to a frightened nation, Swift to a ruling class he wanted to shame, Chesterton to newspaper readers who thought philosophy was beside the point, and Kennedy to allies and adversaries at once. Style questions (skill categories 7 and 8) and audience questions (categories 1 and 2) together make up a large share of the multiple-choice section, and both feed every essay you write in May.

## The skills, as this unit used them

| Skill | What it meant in Unit 8 |
|---|---|
| **1.B** Audience beliefs, values, needs | Roosevelt names what his listeners “expect” (candor and decision), concedes the “dark realities,” and blames the “money changers” they already distrusted. Kennedy addresses “friend and foe alike.” |
| **2.B** Writing for an audience | Revising a letter for retirees on fixed incomes, a speech for a cost-conscious city council, and an orientation speech for nervous freshmen: evidence, diction, concessions and values chosen for the reader. |
| **7.A** Tone from words, comparisons, syntax | Swift's mock-modest proposer, his cookbook register and livestock comparisons; Chesterton's inverted lists and paradoxes; Roosevelt's war analogy. |
| **8.A** Using style on purpose | Revising flat claims into paradox, analogy and antithesis; keeping a deadpan or confident tone steady; choosing a fresh comparison over a cliché. |
| **7.B** Clauses and their relationships | Kennedy's antithesis, chiasmus, conditional sentences and the true reason held for last; Roosevelt's “if” clause placed for emphasis. |
| **8.B** Clear sentences | Combining choppy sentences, ranking stringy ones, fixing comma splices and dangling modifiers, and putting the key idea at the end. |

## Week 26 · Roosevelt's First Inaugural Address (1933)

**Key moves.** Roosevelt opens with what his audience expects, then reframes the crisis: “the only thing we have to fear is fear itself.” A long series of semicolon clauses names the audience's losses before any hope is offered, and a concession separates him from the failed optimists. The financiers are cast as money changers in the temple, joining economic anger to religious values. The second half answers the need for decision with “action, and action now,” a drumbeat of “It can be helped by,” and an extended war analogy that ends with the request for broad executive power.

**What to remember.** Beliefs, values and needs are different. Acknowledgment comes before reassurance. A speaker who credits the audience (“The people of the United States have not failed”) turns a request for power into the people's own choice.

## Week 27 · Swift's A Modest Proposal (1729)

**Key moves.** A sympathetic opening wins agreement; then a polite, statistical persona “humbly” proposes that poor children be sold as food. The clash between his register (pamphlet arithmetic, cookbook diction) and his subject creates the irony. Livestock comparisons expose how the poor were already treated, and the pun on landlords who have “devoured most of the parents” names the real target. The list of “other expedients” that no one may mention states Swift's real proposals and shifts the tone toward open indignation.

**What to remember.** Never confuse the persona with the author. Name tones precisely (mock-modest, deadpan, clinical). Irony is a bet on the audience, so look for the signals the writer leaves.

## Week 28 · Chesterton's Heretics (1905)

**Key moves.** Chesterton argues that a person's philosophy is the most practical thing about him, and his style does the arguing: words that have changed places (heretic and orthodox), a tea-table remark followed to absurd consequences, the paradox “Everything matters--except everything,” paired images of release (a noble hound and a worthless fish), and the parable of the lamp-post that ends in the dark.

**What to remember.** Every device should carry the claim. A paradox startles and must then be explained; an analogy clarifies but does not prove; a short sentence after long ones lands like a verdict. Wit serves an argument; flippancy dismisses it.

## Week 29 · Kennedy's Inaugural Address (1961)

**Key moves.** Nearly every important sentence is a balanced pair: “not a victory of party, but a celebration of freedom”; “United... Divided...”; “not because... not because... but because it is right”; the conditional “If a free society cannot help the many who are poor”; the chiasmus on negotiation; the three-word “But let us begin”; and the inverted command “ask not what your country can do for you.” Each pledge is addressed to a named audience.

**What to remember.** Coordination makes ideas equal; subordination ranks them and names their logic. The end of a sentence is its point of emphasis. Balance is most powerful at the moments that carry the argument.

## Essay moves practised

- **Linking a choice to an audience.** Not *Roosevelt uses an allusion* but *the allusion to the money changers joins his audience's distrust of bankers to its religious values.*
- **Choice, tone, function.** *By describing infants in the cheerful language of recipes, Swift's proposer adopts a calm tone that exposes the cruelty of purely economic thinking.*
- **Revising for a reader.** Replace jargon with concrete people and times; answer the audience's worry with evidence that fits their situation; close on a value they hold.
- **Revising for style.** Turn a flat claim into a paradox, a fresh analogy or an antithesis, and keep the register steady.
- **Combining for clarity.** Subordinate what you concede; put what you want remembered in the main clause at the end.

## Common traps

- Naming the audience without explaining what the text shows about its beliefs, values or needs.
- Reading Swift's proposer as Swift.
- Vague tone words (*negative*, *serious*, *sarcastic*).
- Listing devices without explaining what they do.
- Calling a sentence complex or compound without saying what relationship its structure shows.
- In revision questions, choosing the fanciest option instead of the one that meets the stated goal.

## What the unit exam looks like

Forty-five questions in about 90 minutes. Four passage sets: a new passage from Roosevelt (action, the war analogy, the army of the people), a new passage from Swift (the aged poor and the sixth advantage), a revision set on a student's speech to a city council, and an **unseen** passage, G. K. Chesterton's newspaper essay “On Running After One's Hat” (1908), in which a flood and a runaway hat become adventures. The Chesterton set ends with an AP-style **rhetorical analysis essay** scored on the 6-point rubric: thesis (0-1), evidence and commentary (0-4), sophistication (0-1). Eleven more questions range across the unit, including a short set on Chesterton's defence of the small community, items on Kennedy, and two short written responses.

To prepare: reread the passages in the four guides, practise naming a precise tone and proving it in one sentence, rehearse the clause patterns of Kennedy's speech, and write one timed paragraph explaining how a speaker's choice meets a specific audience's need.
