# Unit 6 Summary · Position, Perspective, Bias and Tone

Four weeks, four writers, and one question asked of every argument: *where does this writer stand, and how does that show?* Unit 6 separated a writer's **position** (the claim defended) from their **perspective** (the vantage point it comes from), taught you to treat **bias** as a claim that needs evidence, and showed how **tone** and **thesis** make a writer's attitude and argument visible. On the exam these skills run through every reading set, and they decide the thesis point and much of the evidence-and-commentary score on all three essays.

## The skills, as this unit used them

| Skill | What it meant in Unit 6 |
|---|---|
| **3.A** Claims and evidence | Wells's claim that the stated reason for lynching is a “plausible screen,” proved with the *Chicago Tribune*'s own figures; Twain's mock-legal rules as evidence against Cooper's admirers. |
| **4.A** Claim-and-evidence paragraph | Introduce evidence with its source and that source's perspective; explain why the evidence is convincing to *this* reader. |
| **3.B** Overarching thesis and structure | Roosevelt's thesis announced in his first sentence, mapped by “as little worthy of a nation as of an individual,” turned at the hinge, restated at the end. |
| **4.B** Writing a thesis | Defensible, specific, previewing; qualified with *although* when another perspective deserves acknowledgement, as Addams acknowledges both the visitor and the neighbours. |
| **7.A** Tone in a text | Twain's mock-courteous ridicule; Wells's controlled indignation; Addams's gentle, self-critical irony; Roosevelt's earnest exhortation. |
| **8.A** Tone in your own writing | Choose the tone that moves the reader you most need; let precise facts carry force; cut sarcasm and loaded words. |

## Week 19 · Ida B. Wells, *Southern Horrors* (1892)

**Key moves.** Writing in exile after a mob destroyed her Memphis newspaper, Wells calls her pamphlet “a contribution to truth, an array of facts.” She uses the *Tribune*'s count of 728 lynchings and shows that only a third of the victims were even charged with the crime used to justify them. She reprints hostile editorials so that the press's bias appears in its own words, and she lets the lynchers state their motive (“The Negroes are getting too independent”) before answering in four words: “The lesson of subordination.” Silent bystanders, she argues, are *particeps criminis*, and economic pressure, Black labour being “the backbone of the South,” can achieve what appeals to conscience have not.

**What to remember.** Perspective is not bias. Wells's experience gave her urgency, and her method, evidence a doubtful reader already trusts, answered the charge of partisanship before it was made.

## Week 20 · Mark Twain, "Fenimore Cooper's Literary Offences" (1895)

**Key moves.** Twain opens with three epigraphs praising Cooper, then regrets, with deadly politeness, that the professors spoke “without having read some of it.” Understatement (“Cooper's art has some defects”) gives way to mock precision (“114 offences against literary art out of a possible 115”). Numbered rules that *require* things turn taste into law. The ending's mock-modest hedges (“it does seem to me”) climb to a catalogue of everything the novel lacks, and the deadpan last line, “Counting these out, what is left is Art,” turns Lounsbury's praise into a punchline.

**What to remember.** Name tone precisely and notice when it is double: courteous on the surface, scornful underneath. Tone persuades only when it rests on evidence, and Twain's own jokes about Native Americans show that a writer can expose one distortion while carrying another.

## Week 21 · Theodore Roosevelt, "The Strenuous Life" (1899)

**Key moves.** Roosevelt praises his Chicago audience, then announces his thesis as an antithesis: “not the doctrine of ignoble ease, but the doctrine of the strenuous life.” The phrase “as little worthy of a nation as of an individual” previews the two halves; “As it is with the individual, so it is with the nation” is the hinge; “So much for the commercial side” signposts a turn to a stronger reason; “I preach to you, then, my countrymen” restates the opening and adds the stakes.

**What to remember.** Describe a thesis in terms the writer would accept before evaluating it. Then look for bias where claims rest on assumption: the speech's judgements about the Filipinos and about women are asserted, not argued, and the people they describe never speak.

## Week 22 · Jane Addams, *Democracy and Social Ethics* (1902)

**Key moves.** Addams's thesis pairs two superlatives: no relation is changing faster than the charitable relation, and none shows more clearly “the lack of that equality which democracy implies.” A *formerly* paragraph supplies evidence of change; the “daintily clad charitable visitor” with her “parasitic cleanliness” turns respectability into dependence; and the meeting of visitor and neighbours becomes “an absolute clashing of two ethical standards,” each misreading the other.

**What to remember.** A strong thesis can hold two perspectives. Grant what each side sees truly, keep the main clause yours, and preview the reasons in the order you will prove them.

## Vocabulary to own

Position, perspective, bias, claim, evidence (statistical, case, testimony, documentary), source credibility, loaded language, cherry-picking, tone, tone shift, connotation, understatement, hyperbole, deadpan, verbal irony, sarcasm, mock-formal diction, bathos, chiasmus, catalogue, scare quotes, persona, overarching thesis, implicit thesis, preview, hinge, signpost, restated thesis, antithesis, qualified thesis, concession, defensible claim.

## Writing moves practised

- **Evidence with its source's perspective:** *even officials who opposed the repair concede that...*
- **Commentary that answers so what?** rather than restating the evidence.
- **Revising tone:** a precise fact instead of an intensifier; an analogy that brings readers closer; short, flat sentences for controlled indignation.
- **Topic to thesis:** what do I claim, could someone disagree, what are my reasons and in what order?
- **The qualified thesis:** *although* [their strongest point], [my position], *because* [my reasons].

## Common traps

- Treating perspective and position as the same thing, or calling a source biased without showing what it distorts.
- Describing tone as *positive* or *negative*, or naming a device without its effect.
- Taking irony literally (*what is left is Art*).
- Mistaking a topic, purpose or announcement for a thesis.
- Writing a concession that swallows the claim, or a thesis that previews reasons in the wrong order.

## What the unit exam looks like

Forty-five questions in about 90 minutes. Five passage sets: new passages from Wells, Twain and Addams; a passage you have not studied, from Theodore Roosevelt's address “Citizenship in a Republic,” delivered at the Sorbonne in 1910 and famous for its man “in the arena,” followed by a 6-point rhetorical analysis essay on it; and a revision set on a student's letter about a community garden. Then ten questions across the unit, including three short written responses.

To prepare: reread the passages quoted in the four guides; practise stating each writer's position and perspective in one sentence each; describe each writer's tone with two precise words; and draft three qualified, previewing theses on issues you care about.
