# Unit 4 Summary · Introductions, Conclusions and Thesis Structure

Four weeks on the edges of an argument and the sentence that holds them together. Paine, Emerson and Woolf showed how a writer meets a reader at the start, what a writer leaves the reader with at the end, how definition and comparison can shape both, and how a thesis can map the path between them. These skills pay off everywhere on the exam: in the revision questions that ask for a better first or last sentence, in the reading questions about a passage's main claim and structure, and in every essay's thesis point.

## Week 12 · Thomas Paine, *Common Sense* (1776)

**Key moves.** The Introduction opens with a concession: Paine's sentiments are “not yet sufficiently fashionable,” because habit makes a wrong look right, and “Time makes more converts than reason.” It names the exigence (abuse of power by king and Parliament), promises to avoid the personal, and ends on the widest stakes: “The cause of America is in a great measure the cause of all mankind.” The main pamphlet concludes with a simile of a man putting off unpleasant business; the Appendix ends with the hand of friendship and the end of Whig and Tory.

**What to remember.** An introduction orients, positions and points ahead; a conclusion gathers, extends and releases. A resistant audience needs objections defused and trust earned before the claim.

## Week 13 · Ralph Waldo Emerson, “The American Scholar” (1837)

**Key moves.** The oration greets the society “on the recommencement of our literary year,” gently calls the anniversary “not enough of labor,” defines the meeting by what it is not, and announces the exigence: “Our day of dependence, our long apprenticeship to the learning of other lands, draws to a close.” The topic is accepted from custom. Near the end the salutation returns, “Gentlemen” becomes “brothers and friends,” and three clauses make a pledge: walk, work and speak for ourselves.

**What to remember.** Distinguish occasion (why people have gathered) from exigence (why this argument now). A speaker can honour a ceremony in order to challenge it. A conclusion that answers its opening gives a frame.

## Week 14 · Virginia Woolf, *The Common Reader* (1925)

**Key moves.** The common reader is defined against the critic and the scholar, then by his instinct to build a whole. “The Modern Essay” sets aside history and defines the essay by function: it exists to give pleasure, and “we must never be roused.” “Modern Fiction” stipulates what “materialists” means and grants Bennett's craftsmanship (not a draught between the window frames) before asking whether life will live there.

**What to remember.** A definition is often a claim; a comparison argues best when its cases are matched. Name the method, then explain what it lets the writer do. Avoid the dictionary opening.

## Week 15 · Ralph Waldo Emerson, “Self-Reliance” (1841)

**Key moves.** The thesis arrives in the first paragraph: believing your own thought is genius. It returns as a command (“Trust thyself”), as a social maxim (“Whoso would be a man, must be a nonconformist”), and through an answered objection (“A foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds”). A mid-essay sentence previews the final part, and the essay ends: “Nothing can bring you peace but yourself. Nothing can bring you peace but the triumph of principles.”

**What to remember.** Find the claim the maxims serve. A closed thesis is a promise to keep; topic sentences carry its reasons in order; the conclusion returns to it changed.

## Vocabulary to own

Exigence, occasion, context, purpose, audience, message, epideictic rhetoric, salutation, inclusive pronoun, hedge, concession, stakes, call to action, frame, pledge, definition by function, definition by negation, stipulative definition, extended definition, analogy, matched comparison, antithesis, classification, overarching thesis, restatement, open thesis, closed (blueprint) thesis, implied thesis, topic sentence, maxim.

## Essay moves practised

- **The short, specific introduction.** Two to four sentences: the situation made concrete, then a defensible thesis.
- **The conclusion that adds.** Gather the argument into one idea and state its significance; never copy the thesis.
- **The definition in dispute.** Open or anchor a paragraph with the definition the argument turns on, applied at once to the case.
- **The matched comparison.** Two cases alike in every relevant way but one, so the reader must justify the difference.
- **The thesis system.** Thesis, topic sentences and conclusion aligned, so the argument can be followed from those sentences alone.

## Common traps

- Universal openers and dictionary definitions.
- “In this essay I will discuss,” which names a topic and makes no claim.
- Describing the audience vaguely, or confusing occasion with exigence.
- Naming a method of development without explaining its function.
- Choosing a memorable maxim instead of the overarching thesis.
- Ending on a courtesy, a vague request or a copied thesis.

## What the unit exam looks like

Forty-five questions in about 90 minutes. Four passage sets: an **unseen** passage from Woolf's “The Patron and the Crocus” (1925), on why choosing an audience is part of writing well; the opening of the first section of *Common Sense*, where Paine defines society and government against each other; Emerson's definition of the scholar as Man Thinking; and a revision set on a student's letter to the editor about painting a mural on a water tower. Eleven review questions range across the unit, with two short written responses. The exam ends with an AP-style **rhetorical analysis essay** on the Woolf passage, scored out of 6: thesis (0-1), evidence and commentary (0-4), sophistication (0-1).

To prepare: reread the quoted passages in the four guides, practise describing a rhetorical situation in six specific sentences, rewrite one weak introduction and conclusion of your own, and write a thesis for a timed rhetorical analysis in under five minutes.
