# Unit 2 Summary · Audience, Purpose and Thesis

Three speeches, four weeks, and two questions every argument has to answer: *who am I talking to?* and *what exactly am I trying to prove?* Frederick Douglass, Booker T. Washington and Susan B. Anthony each faced an audience with power over them and built a thesis to fit that room. The unit taught you to explain how a writer shows an understanding of the audience, to find and describe a thesis and the structure it signals, and to write theses of your own that require defense. On the exam these skills run through all three essays and through both kinds of multiple-choice question: reading questions on passages and writing questions on drafts.

## The skills, as this unit used them

| Skill | What it meant in Unit 2 |
|---|---|
| **1.B** Audience in an argument | Douglass measuring the nation by the Declaration its citizens celebrated; Washington speaking to employers in the language of interest; Anthony addressing potential jurors as “Fellow-citizens.” Name the belief, value or need, then the choice that uses it. |
| **2.B** Audience in your writing | Common ground first, the reader's concern answered early, evidence the reader trusts, and no sentence that mocks the person you need to persuade. |
| **3.A** Claims and evidence (review) | Anthony's founding documents as evidence; Douglass's Virginia statutes, which concede “the manhood of the slave”; Washington's one-third figures. |
| **4.A** Paragraphs (review) | Evidence matched to the paragraph's claim; irrelevant details cut in revision. |
| **3.B** Reading a thesis | Stated (Anthony), figurative (Washington's bucket), delayed or stated last (Douglass). Then the structure it signals: a legal case, one command applied to two audiences, a series of refusals. |
| **4.B** Writing a thesis | Arguable and supportable; a list or pattern preview that matches the body; no restated prompts, device lists, announcements or vague praise. |

## Week 5 · Douglass, “What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?” (1852)

**Key moves.** Invited to speak at an Independence Day event in Rochester, Douglass first praises the founders, then turns: “why am I called upon to speak here to-day?” His questions make a sympathetic but complacent audience test its assumption that the holiday includes everyone. He shows that he shares their value of gratitude (“I am not that man”), splits the pronouns (“This Fourth of July is *yours*, not mine”), and borrows the authority of Psalm 137 before naming his subject: “My subject, then, fellow-citizens, is AMERICAN SLAVERY.”

**What to remember.** Understanding an audience shapes the route, not the destination. Douglass never softens his claim; he builds it on his listeners' own creed and scripture.

## Week 6 · Washington, Atlanta Exposition Address (1895)

**Key moves.** A Black speaker before a divided Southern crowd delivers his thesis as a parable: a thirsty ship told four times, “Cast down your bucket where you are.” He applies the command first “To those of my race” and then “To those of the white race,” so the thesis carries its own structure. The bargain is compressed in one image: “separate as the fingers, yet one as the hand in all things essential to mutual progress.”

**What to remember.** A thesis can be an image. Translate it (seek progress where you are, through economic cooperation, with social equality set aside), test it against every section, and say what structure it signals.

## Week 7 · Anthony, “Is It a Crime for a United States Citizen to Vote?” (1873)

**Key moves.** Indicted for voting, Anthony gave the same speech across two counties to the men who might judge her. Her opening is a model thesis: “It shall be my work this evening to prove to you” that she “not only committed no crime, but, instead, simply exercised my *citizen's right*.” It is contested, it names its ground (the National Constitution), and its grammar previews two claims. Her evidence is her listeners' heritage: “the grand documents left us by the fathers,” and the Preamble read to mean “we, the whole people.”

**What to remember.** A defensible thesis is arguable and supportable. Repair weak ones: topic, fact, restated prompt, device list, announcement, vague praise.

## Week 8 · Thesis, structure and audience together

**Key moves.** Douglass answers the friend who wants him to “argue more, and denounce less” by raising one proposition after another and dismissing each as conceded, until his thesis arrives as a conclusion: “At a time like this, scorching irony, not convincing argument, is needed.” Anthony reduces her case to a syllogism: “Are women persons?” Each placement fits its audience: jurors need a clear claim, a divided crowd a memorable one, a complacent crowd careful preparation.

**What to remember.** Thesis, structure and audience are one decision. In your own writing, run the match check: every previewed reason gets a paragraph, every paragraph serves a reason.

## Vocabulary to own

Audience (primary, secondary), beliefs, values, needs, common ground, shared premise, anticipated objection, pandering, allusion, exigence, overarching thesis, supporting claim, topic, explicit thesis, implicit thesis, figurative thesis, delayed thesis, thesis about method, preview (list and pattern), parable, injunction, apophasis (paralipsis), syllogism, dilemma, defensible, comparison thesis, synthesis thesis, thesis-body match.

## Essay moves practised

- **Opening for a skeptical reader.** Name a shared goal, then the request, then the reader's concern and your answer.
- **Turning a topic into a thesis.** Ask a question, answer it, add the reasons.
- **Turning a device list into a thesis.** Replace each label with a specific choice and what it does to this audience.
- **Previewing structure.** List the reasons in order, or signal a pattern (*Although A, B*; *not only A but B*).
- **Comparing and synthesising.** One claim about the relationship between texts; a position on the issue, never on the sources.

## Common traps

- Stopping at identifying the audience instead of explaining what the writer knew about it.
- Treating praise of an audience as flattery rather than as a premise.
- Mistaking a supporting claim, or the first sentence, for the thesis.
- Assuming an unstated thesis means there is none.
- Restating the prompt, listing ethos, pathos and logos, or announcing a topic.
- Previewing reasons the body never develops.
- Keeping biography or summary paragraphs that analyse no choice.

## What the unit exam looks like

Forty-five questions in about 90 minutes. Five sets: new passages from Douglass (his answer to the title question), Washington (the close of the address) and Anthony (the “obligarchy of sex” and the pronoun dilemma); a revision set on a student's argument for the town newsletter; and an **unseen** speech, Patrick Henry's address to the Second Virginia Convention (1775), which ends with an AP-style **rhetorical analysis essay** on the 6-point rubric: thesis (0-1), evidence and commentary (0-4), sophistication (0-1). Six questions range across the unit, including a short written response on thesis placement.

To prepare: reread the passages quoted in the four guides; for each speech, write one sentence naming its audience's beliefs and one stating its thesis; practise the match check on an old essay; and write a thesis for an unfamiliar speech in five minutes.
