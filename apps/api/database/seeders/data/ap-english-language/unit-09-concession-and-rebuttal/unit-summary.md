# Unit 9 Summary · Concession, Rebuttal and Refutation

Three weeks on the move that separates a strong argument from an adequate one: taking the other side seriously. The unit read three writers who had to persuade audiences inclined to disagree with them (Mill in a book, Madison in a newspaper, Lincoln on a debate platform) and practised the two halves of the CED's qualification skill: explaining how a writer limits and defends a claim (3.C), and doing it yourself (4.C). The skills matter in every part of the May exam. Section I passages often contain a sentence that anticipates a reply, the revision sets ask you to add a concession or qualify a claim, and on the argument essay a fair counterargument answered well is one of the most dependable routes to the sophistication point.

## The skills, as this unit used them

| Skill | What it meant in Unit 9 |
|---|---|
| **3.C** Explain how claims are qualified | Mill's long objection framed by “such reasoners may say”; Madison's “in some degree true” and “chiefly, if not wholly”; Lincoln conceding that Douglas is “perfectly logical” before exposing his premise. Find the concession, the turn and the answer, and say what each does. |
| **4.C** Qualify a claim | Narrow scope (most, some), measure certainty (probably, likely), add conditions (if, when, for students who), and adjust a claim to answer an objection or an alternative perspective, without hedging it into nothing or switching sides. |

## Week 30 · Mill, *On Liberty* (1859), chapter 2

**Key moves.** Mill argues that silencing any opinion assumes we cannot be wrong. He then writes out the strongest objection for his opponents: rulers act on fallible judgment whenever they tax or make war, so forbidding dangerous opinions is no different. After a paragraph in their voice comes a nine-word reply: “I answer that it is assuming very much more.” Confidence earned by surviving every challenge differs from confidence used to forbid challenge. Later he explains why the method matters: “He who knows only his own side of the case, knows little of that.”

**What to remember.** A rebuttal is only as strong as the objection it answers. Mill grants a premise (we must act on fallible opinions) and attacks the inference, which is often the strongest form of refutation. Attribution tags keep a long counterargument from being mistaken for the writer's own view.

## Week 31 · Madison, *The Federalist* No. 10 (1787)

**Key moves.** Writing for New Yorkers who admired their state constitution and feared a large republic, Madison praises the state constitutions and then limits the praise; he grants that citizens' complaints are “in some degree true”; he sorts the evidence, conceding that some distresses were wrongly blamed on government; and he concedes that destroying liberty would end faction, since “Liberty is to faction what air is to fire,” before rejecting that remedy as worse than the disease. He even concedes a limit to his own preference for size: “It must be confessed that in this, as in most other cases, there is a mean.”

**What to remember.** Most qualification happens inside a claim, in a word or a clause. Rebutting a remedy on cost, while granting that it would work, can be stronger than denying that it would work. The writing lesson was the five-part counterargument paragraph: name the objection, give its reason, concede, turn and rebut, and qualify the thesis in light of it.

## Week 32 · Lincoln, replies to Douglas (1858)

**Key moves.** At Ottawa Lincoln answers a charge he cannot disprove with a burden-of-proof rebuttal: “A man cannot prove a negative,” but whoever makes a charge must prove it; the repeated, ironic “conscientious” keeps the tone courteous on the surface. At Galesburg he concedes that each of Douglas's positions is logical and shows that each rests on one unstated premise, that slavery is not wrong; then he qualifies his own position so that Douglas cannot paint him as a radical. At Alton he reframes the contest (“false issues” against “the real issue”) and carries his concession and rebuttal in one analogy, a cancer that cannot be cut out at once but must not be spread.

**What to remember.** Choose the rebuttal by asking what the objection rests on: counter-evidence for a factual claim, burden of proof for an unsupported charge, exposure for a hidden assumption, reductio for a principle with absurd consequences, reframing for an objection aimed at the wrong question.

## Vocabulary to own

Counterargument, alternative perspective, concession, rebuttal, refutation, counterclaim, qualifier, scope and certainty qualifiers, conditional qualifier, absolute terms, over-hedging, straw man, steel man, attribution tag, turn signal, burden of proof, unstated premise (assumption), reductio ad absurdum, reframing, tu quoque (pointing out that opponents do not meet their own standard, which weakens an objection's force but does not refute it), devil's advocate.

## Essay moves practised

- **The fair statement.** State the objection so its holders would accept your summary, with a specific reason attached.
- **Concede, turn, answer.** *It is true that... but...*; put the concession in the dependent clause and your claim in the main clause.
- **The qualified thesis.** *Although* or *While* plus a firm claim, often with a condition that absorbs the strongest objection.
- **Matching the rebuttal to the objection.** Answer it where it rests.
- **Revision habits.** Hunt for always, never, every and everyone; replace dismissals (*They are wrong*) with concessions; mark the turn with *even so*, *still* or *nevertheless*.

## Common traps

- Quoting a sentence from a writer's statement of the objection as if it were the writer's own view.
- Calling every counterargument a refutation; a rebuttal can also argue that an objection matters less.
- Treating a concession as weakness, or conceding without answering.
- Answering a straw man, or insulting opponents instead of addressing their reasons.
- Over-hedging a claim until it says nothing, or "qualifying" it by reversing it.

## What the unit exam looks like

Forty-four questions in about 95 minutes. Four passage sets: an **unseen** passage, Alexander Hamilton's answer to the objection that the Constitution lacked a bill of rights (*The Federalist* No. 84, 1788), in which he points out his opponents' inconsistency, answers with counter-evidence, turns the objection into a counterclaim and qualifies it, and ends by reframing the issue; a new passage from the end of Mill's chapter 2 on the manners of controversy and the “real morality of public discussion”; Lincoln at Alton, where he qualifies a generalisation and tests a listener's professed belief against conduct; and a revision set on a student's draft about open-campus lunch. Ten questions range across the unit, including two short written responses (contrasting Mill's and Hamilton's treatment of opponents, and writing a qualified thesis with a concession and rebuttal). The exam ends with an AP-style **argument essay** on Mill's claim that a person who knows only his own side knows little of that, scored on the 6-point rubric: thesis (0-1), evidence and commentary (0-4), sophistication (0-1).

To prepare: reread the close readings in the three guides, practise naming the kind of rebuttal in each passage, revise three of your own old thesis statements into qualified ones, and write one timed counterargument paragraph.
