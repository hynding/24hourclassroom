# Unit 3 Summary · Line of Reasoning and Methods of Development

Three weeks, three writers, and the question underneath every argument: how do claims join up? Units 1 and 2 taught you to find a claim, weigh its evidence, read an audience and state a thesis. Unit 3 asked how a writer leads a reader from a premise to a conclusion (the **line of reasoning**), and what each step is made of (the **methods of development**: narration, description, comparison, definition, cause and effect, classification). Reasoning and organization questions are about 13-16% of the multiple-choice section, and the evidence-and-commentary row of every essay (4 of 6 points) rewards a sustained line of reasoning, so these skills pay off on both halves of the exam.

## The skills, as this unit used them

| Skill | What it meant in Unit 3 |
|---|---|
| **5.A** Describe a line of reasoning and judge it | Thoreau starting from a motto his audience accepts and arriving, step by step, at “the true place for a just man is also a prison.” Ask whether each step follows, whether any is missing, and whether the last claim matches the thesis. |
| **6.A** Build a line of reasoning with commentary | Revising a list of reasons for Sunday library hours into a chain of claims joined by *so*, *granted* and *therefore*, with commentary that says what each piece of evidence implies. |
| **5.C** Recognise methods of development and their purposes | Wollstonecraft's flower simile carrying a causal claim; her soldiers offered “As a proof”; her redefinition of “masculine women.” Name the method and the job it does for the reader. |
| **6.C** Use methods of development in your own argument | Choosing the method a reader needs: description or narration for a reader who has not seen the problem, definition for a disputed term, cause and effect for a problem thought natural, comparison for cases thought unrelated, classification for a crowded subject. |
| **3.A, 4.A** Claims and evidence (spiral) | Separating a claim from its support; choosing specific, relevant, credible evidence for an exact claim. |

## Week 9 · “Civil Disobedience” (Henry David Thoreau, 1849)

**Key moves.** Thoreau opens with agreement (“I heartily accept the motto”), then narrows his demand to “a better government.” He argues that majorities rule because they are “physically the strongest,” not because they are right, so conscience must outrank law: “we should be men first, and subjects afterward.” The machine-and-friction analogy supplies a test: tolerate the friction of government, but if a law “requires you to be the agent of injustice to another,” break it and “Let your life be a counter friction to stop the machine.” He refutes Paley's rule of expediency with the plank and the drowning man, and only then reaches his conclusion about prison and a “peaceable revolution.”

**What to remember.** A line of reasoning is a chain of dependent claims, not a list. A radical conclusion persuades when it arrives last, after premises the audience has already granted. Answering the strongest opposing view is itself a step.

## Week 10 · *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (Mary Wollstonecraft, 1792)

**Key moves.** Her readers believed women's weakness was natural; the only answer to a claim about nature is a claim about cause. So she argues that “the neglected education of my fellow creatures is the grand source of the misery I deplore.” The flower simile (“flowers that are planted in too rich a soil”) lets readers see the effect and places its cause in cultivation. The soldiers comparison proves the cause: same superficial education, same faults, different sex. Redefinitions sharpen it: “masculine women” split into a meaning she rejects and one she embraces; “innocence, as ignorance is courteously termed.” Her promise of “things, not words!” makes her plain style part of the argument.

**What to remember.** Name the method and its purpose together. A comparison is judged by the respect in which its terms are alike; a causal claim needs a mechanism or a way to rule out other causes.

## Week 11 · *The Souls of Black Folk* (W. E. B. Du Bois, 1903)

**Key moves.** Du Bois answers the unasked question (“How does it feel to be a problem?”) with a story, the visiting card refused “peremptorily, with a glance,” then generalises the story into a definition: double-consciousness, “this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others.” A causal argument overturns a judgment: what looks like weakness “is the contradiction of double aims,” shown through the artisan with “but half a heart in either cause.” In “Of the Meaning of Progress,” description of Josie and the narrative of his return become an argument, sealed by the questions about measuring progress and the final ride “in the Jim Crow car.”

**What to remember.** Methods combine and prepare each other: story, then definition, then cause. In your own writing, choose the method your reader needs, keep it brief and specific, and follow it with commentary that says what it shows.

## Vocabulary to own

Line of reasoning, list vs line, premise, deductive and inductive reasoning, gap, turn, emphatic order, commentary, logical transition, concession, refutation, analogy, paradox, methods of development, narration, description, comparison and contrast, definition, redefinition, stipulative definition, cause and effect, mechanism, correlation, classification, rhetorical question, metaphor, double-consciousness.

## Essay moves practised

- **Turning a list into a line.** Make each claim depend on the last; replace *also* and *another reason* with transitions that name a relation.
- **Commentary that answers “so what?”** Say what the evidence implies, for whom, and how it advances the thesis; never just restate it.
- **Answering an objection as a step.** Grant what is true, measure it, and turn it back toward the thesis.
- **Method follows need.** Diagnose what the reader lacks, then describe, narrate, define, compare, trace a cause or classify.
- **Analysing a writer's line of reasoning** as the structure of a rhetorical analysis essay: *first he wins agreement, then he...*.

## Common traps

- Mistaking the order of evidence for the line of reasoning.
- Calling an analogy (Thoreau's machine) the thesis.
- Naming a method without its purpose.
- Reading a simile as decoration when it carries a causal claim.
- Treating correlation as cause.
- Leaving a story, picture or comparison without commentary.
- Mentioning an objection and stopping.

## What the unit exam looks like

Forty-five questions in about 90 minutes. Five passage sets: Thoreau's account of his night in jail; Wollstonecraft on flattery, virtue and her own plain style; Du Bois on the first decades after Emancipation; an **unseen** passage, the introduction to Jacob Riis's *How the Other Half Lives* (1890), followed by an AP-style 6-point **rhetorical analysis essay** on it; and an invented student letter about free bus passes for revision. Expect stems such as *Which best describes the line of reasoning...*, *The paragraph is developed primarily through...*, *The comparison serves to...*, and *The writer wants to...*. Five questions range across the unit, including two short written responses.

To prepare: trace each writer's line of reasoning in four steps; for each method of development, name a purpose and an example from the unit; and practise writing two sentences of commentary after a piece of evidence until they explain rather than repeat.
