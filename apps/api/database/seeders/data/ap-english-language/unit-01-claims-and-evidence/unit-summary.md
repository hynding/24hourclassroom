# Unit 1 Summary · Claims, Evidence and the Rhetorical Situation

Four weeks, four founding American arguments, and the habits the rest of the year is built on: reading the situation a text answers, separating claims from evidence, and writing paragraphs in which every sentence does a job. The rhetorical situation and claims and evidence together account for roughly 24 to 30 percent of the multiple-choice section of the AP exam, and every essay in Section II is built from the claim-evidence-commentary paragraph, so these skills pay off directly in May.

## The skills, as this unit used them

| Skill | What it meant in Unit 1 |
|---|---|
| **1.A** The rhetorical situation | Lincoln at a new cemetery in a war not yet won; Congress explaining itself to "the opinions of mankind"; Franklin before objectors at a signing; Lincoln again, facing a victorious crowd tempted toward vengeance. Name writer, audience, exigence, purpose, context and message, then explain how they shape choices. |
| **3.A** Claims and evidence | Self-evident truths as premises; the king's tyranny as the claim the grievances prove; Franklin's lifetime of changed opinions as evidence for doubt; "All knew" as a claim presented as common knowledge; the North's share in the guilt as a claim implied, never stated. |
| **4.A** The argument paragraph | An arguable claim first; specific evidence that proves its arguable part; commentary after every piece of evidence; a closing link. In analysis, the claim names a choice, its effect and its purpose. |

## Week 1 · The Gettysburg Address (Abraham Lincoln, 1863)

**Key moves.** The exigence is a cemetery dedication; the context is a civil war whose outcome was uncertain. Lincoln dates the nation from 1776 and its "proposition that all men are created equal," framing the war as a test. He then denies the ceremony its purpose ("we cannot dedicate, we cannot consecrate, we cannot hallow this ground") and transfers the duty of dedication to the audience: "It is for us, the living." The speech has no "I" and names no enemy, which suits a solemn occasion and a nation-wide secondary audience.

**What to remember.** Exigence is not context, and message is not topic. State purpose as action on an audience. Listing components earns nothing; connecting them to choices is analysis.

## Week 2 · The Declaration of Independence (1776)

**Key moves.** The first sentence names the audience and promises reasons. The preamble's self-evident truths are the major premise of a syllogism; the claim that the king's history is one of "repeated injuries and usurpations" is the minor premise, and "To prove this, let Facts be submitted to a candid world" introduces the grievances as evidence. The concession that governments should not change "for light and transient causes" sets the standard of proof; the long list of *He has* charges meets it by showing a pattern. Failed petitions are evidence that separation is a last resort.

**What to remember.** A claim is something a reasonable person could dispute. Evidence must be relevant to the exact claim and sufficient for the audience, and it works through an assumption (warrant) that commentary should make visible.

## Week 3 · Franklin's speech at the close of the Constitutional Convention (1787)

**Key moves.** Franklin opens by confessing he does "not entirely approve of this constitution," standing with the objectors before asking them to move. His long life of changing opinions is personal evidence for the claim that one should doubt one's own judgment; the French lady who meets "nobody but myself that is always in the right" makes the point laughable and recognisable; reasoning about assemblies, which bring "all their prejudices, their passions," supports the claim that no better constitution is possible. He models the sacrifice ("Within these walls they were born, and here they shall die") and asks each objector to "doubt a little of his own infallibility."

**What to remember.** The speech has the shape of a paragraph: claim, varied evidence, commentary, closing request. In your own paragraphs, integrate quotations, explain each piece of evidence, and cut anything that does no work.

## Week 4 · The Second Inaugural Address (Abraham Lincoln, 1865)

**Key moves.** A victory speech that refuses to celebrate. Parallel clauses assign blame while sounding balanced ("one of them would make war [...] and the other would accept war"). "All knew" presents the claim that slavery caused the war as consensus, but evidence follows. Shared scripture denies either side God's exclusive favour, and "let us judge not, that we be not judged" checks the audience's self-righteousness. The hardest claim, that the woe falls on "both North and South," is offered as a supposition and a question. The conclusion turns reasoning into a program: "With malice toward none; with charity for all."

**What to remember.** Turn observations into analytical claims (choice, effect, purpose). Look for implicit claims and claims dressed as common knowledge. The same speaker in a new situation says different things: Gettysburg never names slavery; the Second Inaugural does.

## Writing moves practised

- **Opening with the situation.** Name the exigence, audience and purpose in the first sentence of an analysis, or the decision and its date in an argument.
- **Matching evidence to the claim.** Underline the arguable word and choose evidence that proves it.
- **Commentary after every piece of evidence.** Answer *so what?* with an explanation, not a summary.
- **Analytical claims.** *By [choice], the writer [effect] in order to [purpose]*, made specific to the text.
- **Revision.** Cut irrelevant details, replace vague assertions with evidence, and address the audience's likely concerns.

## Common traps

- Listing the six components without connecting any of them to a choice.
- Confusing exigence with context, or topic with message.
- Treating premises as evidence, or facts as claims.
- Naming a kind of evidence (*facts*, *an anecdote*) without saying what it proves.
- Dropped quotations followed by *This shows that...*
- Observations posing as claims (*Lincoln uses religious language*).
- Reading the Second Inaugural as a celebration of victory: it is a victory speech that refuses to gloat.

## What the unit exam looks like

Forty-four questions in about 90 minutes. Four passage sets: new passages from the Declaration (the later grievances, the appeal to "our British brethren" and the conclusion) and from Franklin's speech (the joke about the two churches, the builders of Babylon, the argument about opinion); one **unseen** passage, from Thomas Jefferson's First Inaugural Address (1801), in which a winner of a bitter election tells the nation "We are all Republicans, we are all Federalists"; and a revision set on a student's letter to a county commission. The Jefferson passage ends with an AP-style **rhetorical analysis essay** scored on the 6-point rubric: thesis (0-1), evidence and commentary (0-4), sophistication (0-1). Eleven questions range across the unit, including two short written responses.

To prepare: reread the quoted passages in the four guides; practise naming the six components of an unfamiliar speech in one minute; sort the sentences of any paragraph into claim, evidence and commentary; and write one timed analytical paragraph on a passage you have not seen, starting from its situation.
