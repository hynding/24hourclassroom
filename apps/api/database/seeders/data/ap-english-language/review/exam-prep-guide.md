# AP English Language · Exam Prep Guide

**Exam:** Wednesday 12 May 2027, morning session · **Format:** two sections, three hours and fifteen minutes, taken digitally in the College Board's Bluebook app · **Built from:** the College Board AP English Language and Composition Course and Exam Description and the year's work in this course.

This guide is for weeks 33 and 34. Week 33 is for review with this guide; week 34 is the full practice exam, which follows the May 2027 paper's shape exactly. Weeks 35 and 36 are buffer weeks, and the exam falls the week after.

The whole course has been preparing you for one kind of reading and one kind of writing. You read arguments, speeches and essays and asked what a writer wanted, from whom, and how the choices on the page served that aim. You wrote arguments of your own and revised drafts sentence by sentence. The exam asks for exactly those two things, under time pressure, on texts you have never seen.

## The format

| Section | What | Time | Weight | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I · Multiple choice | 45 questions in five sets | 60 minutes | 45% | About 23-25 reading questions on published nonfiction passages and about 20-22 writing questions on student drafts. No penalty for a wrong answer, so answer every question. |
| II · Free response | 3 essays | 2 hours 15 minutes, including a 15-minute reading period | 55% | Q1 synthesis (six sources, at least one visual or quantitative; use at least three), Q2 rhetorical analysis, Q3 argument. Each essay is scored out of 6. The College Board suggests about 40 minutes of writing for each. |

**Digital testing.** You read and answer on screen in Bluebook, and you type your essays. Practise with the tools before May: the highlighter and annotation tool for marking claims and shifts, the option eliminator for crossing out wrong answers, and the clock (you can hide it, but check it at the end of each set). In Section I you can move back and forth among questions until time is called. In Section II the reading period comes first; then the three essays share the remaining two hours and you can move among them, so you control the pacing. Typing makes it easy to write long and say little. Plan before you type.

**What “unseen” means.** None of the passages on the exam will be texts you studied. The exam tests skills, not memory of particular speeches, which is why every unit exam in this course included a passage you had not read. Question 3 is the only place where the texts and events you know count directly, as evidence for your own argument.

## How the multiple-choice section is weighted

The CED sorts Section I questions into eight skill categories, four for reading (the odd numbers) and four for writing (the even numbers). The ranges below are the share of the section each category carries.

| Category | Course codes | Reading or writing | Share of Section I |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rhetorical situation | 1.A, 1.B | Reading: exigence, audience, purpose; how an argument meets an audience's beliefs and needs | 11-14% |
| Rhetorical situation | 2.A, 2.B | Writing: introductions and conclusions; revising for an audience | 11-14% |
| Claims and evidence | 3.A, 3.B, 3.C | Reading: claims, evidence, thesis, qualification and counterargument | 13-16% |
| Claims and evidence | 4.A, 4.B, 4.C | Writing: adding evidence, revising a thesis, qualifying a claim | 13-16% |
| Reasoning and organization | 5.A, 5.B, 5.C | Reading: line of reasoning, organization, methods of development | 13-16% |
| Reasoning and organization | 6.A, 6.B, 6.C | Writing: commentary, transitions, methods of development | 13-16% |
| Style | 7.A, 7.B, 7.C | Reading: word choice, comparisons, syntax, clauses, grammar and mechanics | 11-14% |
| Style | 8.A, 8.B, 8.C | Writing: tone, sentence combining, conventions | 11-14% |

Claims and evidence, and reasoning and organization, are the heaviest pairs: together they carry more than half the section. If you have time to sharpen only one habit before May, make it this one: after the first reading of any passage, say in one sentence what the writer claims and why, and after the first reading of any draft, find the thesis and number the reasons that support it.

## The reading-set routine

Twelve minutes a set is the average (60 minutes for five sets). Reading sets usually take longer than writing sets, so bank time on the drafts.

1. **Read the headnote first.** The italic line above a passage gives the author, the date and often the occasion: an inaugural address, a lecture to a night school, a book-length essay. It is free context for questions about the rhetorical situation. Use it.
2. **Read the whole passage once, steadily, for the argument (three to four minutes).** Do not stop to puzzle over a hard sentence. Mark only the moves: the claim, a turn (“But,” “Yet”), a concession, a new kind of evidence, the conclusion.
3. **Name the situation in one sentence.** *A re-elected president, speaking to a nation near the end of a civil war, lays the guilt of slavery on North and South alike, so that his call for charity toward the South will sound like justice rather than weakness.* Most purpose, audience, tone and thesis questions are already half answered.
4. **Answer in order, but skip and mark anything that stalls you.** A question you skip costs nothing until the end of the section; a question you stare at for three minutes costs you two others.
5. **Go back to the lines every time.** A quoted phrase in the stem is an instruction to reread that sentence and the sentences on either side. The answer is almost always in the context.
6. **Eliminate before you choose.** Cross out options that are false to the text, too extreme (*condemns*, *ridicules*, *despairs* when the tone is measured), true but beside the point of the stem, or true of a different part of the passage.
7. **Watch the stem's verbs.** “Primarily serves to” asks for function, not meaning. “Most nearly means” asks for the sense in context. “Best describes” asks for the most accurate option, not merely an acceptable one.

**Three reading traps.** The *vehicle trap* takes the image for the claim: Lincoln's “wen or cancer” at Alton is built on a picture of illness, but it argues nothing about medicine. The *extreme trap* turns a real attitude up too far: when Madison praises the state constitutions and then limits the praise, he is qualifying, not condemning. The *half-right trap* names a true detail that does not answer the question: the sentence that sets up a concession is not the sentence that supports the claim. Every multiple-choice rationale in this course names the trap a wrong option sets; reread those for the items you missed.

## The writing-set routine (revision questions)

A writing set prints a student draft with numbered sentences and asks how the writer should revise it. These questions reward the same thinking as your own essays, applied to someone else's paragraph.

1. **Read the headnote for the task and the audience.** “Addressed to the county commission” or “for the school newspaper” tells you what tone, evidence and concessions will work.
2. **Skim the draft for its skeleton.** Find the thesis (often weak on purpose, since one question will ask you to fix it) and note which sentences hold each reason. Write the numbers down: *thesis 3; waste 8-11; skills 12-15; objection 16-18.*
3. **Read the stem's goal and hold every option to it.** “The writer wants to...” is the most important phrase on the screen. An option can be well written and still fail the stated goal. If the goal is to address a budget-minded audience, the right answer mentions cost, however charming the alternatives.
4. **Know the recurring question types:**
   - *Revise the thesis:* choose the version that is arguable and, where asked, previews the body paragraphs in order.
   - *Add evidence:* choose the specific, relevant fact that supports the exact claim named, not a related fact, a personal anecdote or an opposing view.
   - *Add commentary:* choose the sentence that explains what the evidence proves and links it to the thesis, usually with a word like *therefore* or *because*.
   - *Transitions:* name the relationship (addition, contrast, cause, example, concession) before you look at the options.
   - *Combine sentences:* decide how the ideas relate, then pick the punctuation or conjunction that states that relationship. A colon explains; *so* shows result; *although* concedes.
   - *Concession and rebuttal:* the best version grants what is true in the objection and then turns to the writer's position; dismissing or insulting opponents is never right.
   - *Keep or delete:* ask what the sentence does for the argument and the reader, not whether it is interesting.
   - *Conventions:* agreement, pronoun case, apostrophes, comma splices. Read the sentence aloud in your head with each option.
5. **Prefer the precise and the measured.** Correct options are usually specific (numbers, names of places, sources) and keep the draft's tone. Wrong options are often vague (*a lot*, *many things*), exaggerated, or a shift into slang or melodrama.

## Section II: the 6-point rubric, row by row

All three essays are scored on the same three rows. Learn them as a checklist, because readers score with them in hand.

**Row A · Thesis (0 or 1).** One point for a defensible thesis that responds to the prompt. For synthesis and argument, it takes a position; for rhetorical analysis, it analyzes the writer's choices in light of the purpose. It can be more than one sentence and can sit anywhere, but put it in your first paragraph so the reader finds it. It earns nothing if it restates the prompt, only summarizes, or takes no position. A thesis that also previews your line of reasoning helps Row B, though Row A does not require it.

**Row B · Evidence and Commentary (0 to 4).** This is two thirds of the score.
- **4:** specific evidence supports all claims in a line of reasoning, and commentary consistently explains how the evidence supports the thesis (in rhetorical analysis, how the writer's choices serve the purpose for the audience).
- **3:** specific evidence and a line of reasoning, with commentary that explains some or most of the evidence; some links are thinner.
- **2:** some specific evidence, but commentary largely summarizes or repeats it.
- **1:** mostly general evidence, little or no commentary.
- **0:** no relevant evidence, or a restatement of the thesis.

In the synthesis essay, the number of sources matters: an essay that uses only one source scores 0 in Row B, one that uses two cannot score above 1, and a 2 or higher needs at least three. Cite them as Source A, Source B and so on, or by their descriptions.

The jump from 2 to 3 is the one most students need, and it comes from explaining *function*. In *The Federalist* No. 10 (1787), James Madison writes: “Liberty is to faction what air is to fire, an aliment without which it instantly expires.” A weak sentence of commentary identifies the device: *Madison uses an analogy to compare liberty to air.* A middling one paraphrases: *Madison says liberty causes faction.* A strong one explains what the choice does for this audience: *By likening liberty to the air that feeds a fire, Madison grants his readers' fear that free government breeds faction while reminding them that no one would abolish air; the comparison makes the cure of ending liberty sound as absurd as giving up breathing.* Name the device if it helps, but always say what it does and for whom.

**Row C · Sophistication (0 or 1).** One point for writing that shows a complex understanding, sustained across the essay, not in one sentence. The ways it is usually earned:
- explaining the **significance or tensions** in an argument (a speaker who concedes his opponent's logic in order to defeat his premise; a writer who grants that a remedy would work and rejects it anyway);
- explaining the **rhetorical situation** with real precision (why this audience, at this moment, needed these choices);
- **qualifying** a position and weighing alternatives, in synthesis and argument, rather than treating the question as one-sided;
- a **vivid, controlled style** that persuades throughout.

What does not earn it: a sweeping opening about “society today,” a list of rhetorical terms, or one clever last line tacked onto an ordinary essay. The point is earned on top of solid Row B work; essays that score 2 or lower in Row B rarely earn Row C.

## The three essays

**Q1 · Synthesis.** You get a short introduction to an issue, six sources (articles, memos, interviews, and at least one chart, table or other visual), and a prompt asking for your position. The 15-minute reading period is for this essay: read every source and its headnote, and note in the margin what each one claims, what kind of evidence it offers and whom it speaks for. Then decide your position *before* you start writing, and let it be qualified if the sources pull both ways. The commonest failure is the source tour: a paragraph on Source A, a paragraph on Source B, and so on, with your own argument nowhere. Organise by your reasons instead, and bring two or three sources into each paragraph so they talk to one another. When a table appears, read it as an argument: what rose, what fell, and what the numbers do not show. A sentence such as *Library visits rose by about a third after the late hours began (Source C), but overtime costs doubled in the same period, which suggests that the extra hours cost more than the budget memo assumed* is synthesis; *Source C is a table about library visits* is not.

**Q2 · Rhetorical analysis.** A nonfiction passage, usually 600-900 words, with a headnote that names the writer, the occasion and often the audience. The prompt asks you to analyze the rhetorical choices the writer makes to achieve a purpose. Start with the rhetorical situation: who is speaking, to whom, when, and what they want the audience to think or do. Your thesis names that purpose and the main strategies that serve it. Build each body paragraph on a move in the argument (the concession, the turn, the call to action), not on a device. *Lincoln reminds his listeners that both sides “read the same Bible,” so that the guilt of the war belongs to the whole nation and not to the South alone* is a paragraph; *Lincoln uses biblical allusion* is not. Track how the passage moves, and treat its order as evidence: in his Second Inaugural (1865) the closing appeal, “With malice toward none, with charity for all,” only works because of everything that comes before it.

**Q3 · Argument.** A prompt offers a claim, often a short quotation, about a question of values or public life, and asks you to argue your position on it. Evidence can come from history, current events, science, reading or your own carefully described experience. The two traps are the hypothetical (*Imagine a student who...*) standing in for real evidence, and the string of examples with no commentary. Choose two or three examples you know well, and for each explain what about it proves your point. Qualified positions often score best, because they invite the weighing that earns Row C: *Competition can push students to excel, but only when the rules are fair and losing carries no lasting penalty; a contest rigged against some players teaches them nothing but resignation.*

**Timing each essay.** For synthesis, use the reading period to read and plan, then write for about 40 minutes. For the other two, plan for six to eight minutes (thesis, three body points, the evidence for each), write for about thirty, and keep two or three for rereading the thesis and fixing errors that obscure meaning. If time runs short on the last essay, write the thesis and one fully developed paragraph rather than three thin ones.

## A stock of evidence for Question 3

Go into the exam with a handful of examples you can explain in depth. The texts you studied this year are part of that stock: you can describe their arguments and their consequences in your own words, and a well-explained example from a speech or essay often reads better than a vague appeal to *history*.

| Area | Examples from this course you can describe | Concepts they support |
|---|---|---|
| Speeches at a turning point | Lincoln's Gettysburg Address and Second Inaugural; Franklin's speech closing the 1787 Convention; the inaugurals of Franklin Roosevelt and John F. Kennedy | leadership in crisis, compromise, unity and division, duty |
| Arguments for rights and justice | Douglass on the Fourth of July; Susan B. Anthony's 1873 speech; Stanton's Declaration of Sentiments; Wollstonecraft on education; Ida B. Wells on lynching | protest, the gap between ideals and practice, courage, evidence as a weapon |
| Conscience and the individual | Thoreau's Civil Disobedience; Emerson's Self-Reliance; Mill's On Liberty | dissent, conformity, freedom of thought, the limits of law |
| Persuasion and public debate | Paine's Common Sense; The Federalist; the Lincoln-Douglas debates; Swift's A Modest Proposal | how arguments change minds, satire, the uses of fear and reason |
| Reflection and character | Bacon's and Montaigne's essays; Franklin's Autobiography; Booker T. Washington's Atlanta address; Theodore Roosevelt on the strenuous life | ambition, self-improvement, compromise, effort and failure |

Add two or three examples of your own from current events, science or personal experience. Practise writing a thesis on three different prompts with the same examples; the exam rewards students who can bend what they know toward a new question.

## Test day

- **Before.** Bluebook installed and the device charged; the exam app checked in advance as the College Board instructs. Eat breakfast. Do not learn anything new.
- **Section I.** About twelve minutes a set; less on drafts, more on passages. Headnote, then a reading for the argument, then the one-sentence situation, then the questions. Skip and mark; return. With two minutes left, answer every blank question: there is no penalty for a wrong answer.
- **Section II.** Use the reading period on the synthesis sources and a quick look at the other two prompts. Start with the essay you feel surest of. Plan each before typing. Put the thesis in the first paragraph. Quote short, explain long. Watch the clock at the 40- and 80-minute marks of the writing time.
- **If a passage baffles you.** Start with the questions that quote specific lines: they make you reread small pieces, and the passage usually comes into focus. For an essay, a modest, defensible thesis beats a grand one you cannot support.

## Traps that cost points

- Touring the sources in synthesis instead of arguing with them, or citing fewer than three.
- Device-spotting in rhetorical analysis: naming a strategy without saying what it does for the audience.
- A thesis that repeats the prompt's words without a position.
- Hypothetical or vague evidence in the argument essay.
- Choosing a revision because it sounds good rather than because it meets the stated goal.
- Taking an image for the claim, or a measured tone for an extreme one, in a reading set.
- Spending fifteen minutes on one Section I set and rushing the last one.
