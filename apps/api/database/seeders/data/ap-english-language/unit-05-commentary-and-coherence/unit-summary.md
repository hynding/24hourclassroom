# Unit 5 Summary · Commentary, Organization and Transitions

Three weeks and three writers who built arguments to last: Francis Bacon, Samuel Johnson and Benjamin Franklin. The unit moved from the large scale to the small and back to your own writing: how an argument holds together (organization and transitions), how word choice, comparisons and syntax create a tone, and how to choose those things yourself so that your argument persuades its audience. Reasoning and organization carry about 13-16% of the multiple-choice section, style about 11-14%, and the evidence-and-commentary row of every essay rewards a line of reasoning a reader can follow in a voice the reader can trust.

## The skills, as this unit used them

| Skill | What it meant in Unit 5 |

| **5.A** Line of reasoning | Bacon's “Of Revenge” as a chain of compact, separate reasons; Franklin testing a “positive, assuming manner” against each of the ends of conversation in turn. |

## Week 16 · Bacon's *Essays* (1625)

**Key moves.** “STUDIES serve for delight, for ornament, and for ability” names three uses, and the next sentence takes them up in the same order: a preview the paragraph follows. A second triad mirrors the first (“sloth,” “affectation,” “the humor of a scholar”), so the turn from praise to warning needs no transition word. When Bacon does use connectives they carry exact meanings: “For” gives a reason, “but” marks a limit, “that is” restates a metaphor literally. In “Of Revenge,” “Therefore” turns the claim that no one does wrong for its own sake into a personal conclusion, and the essay's line of reasoning is a series of short, linked reasons.

**What to remember.** Coherence comes from order and repetition as much as from transitions. A transition is a small claim about how ideas relate, so a false *therefore* or a careless *however* damages an argument.

## Week 17 · Johnson's *Rambler* No. 134 (1751) and *Idler* No. 30 (1758)

**Key moves.** In the Rambler, Latinate diction (“remonstrances,” “pertinacious,” “seducements”) raises procrastination to a serious moral subject; personification makes delay an enemy “always renewing its attacks, and though often vanquished, never destroyed”; the cumulative sentence “Thus life is languished away ...” imitates the cycle of resolving and failing; the antithesis “To act is far easier than to suffer” delivers a verdict; the Tantalus allusion withdraws sympathy from those who will not lift their hands. Johnson's “we” keeps the tone grave but sympathetic. In the Idler, the same balanced syntax becomes a weapon: Wotton's ambassador inverted into “a man without virtue, who writes lies at home for his own profit,” a list of qualifications filled with vices, and a closing parallel of soldiers and scribblers.

**What to remember.** Tone is attitude, not subject. Name it precisely (often with two words), quote the choices that create it, and say what it does for the argument.

## Week 18 · Franklin's *Autobiography* (first published 1791)

**Key moves.** Franklin's father faulted not his spelling but his “elegance of expression,” “method” and “perspicuity.” Franklin answered with deliberate practice: rewriting Spectator essays from notes and comparing, turning prose into verse to build “a stock of words,” and reordering jumbled notes to learn method. Later he dropped “certainly” and “undoubtedly” for “I conceive” and “it appears to me,” because a dogmatic manner may “provoke contradiction and prevent a candid attention.” His paragraph practises its own advice.

**What to remember.** Style is a persuasive choice made for an audience. Keep the claim and the reasons; adjust the manner. State firmly what your evidence proves, qualify what it does not, and never insult the reader you need.

## Vocabulary to own

Unity, coherence, topic sentence, transitional element, restatement, concession, summary transition, list transition, preview, known-new order, tone, register, denotation, connotation, Latinate diction, parallelism, antithesis, chiasmus, periodic and cumulative sentences, personification, allusion, mock solemnity, understatement, intensifier, hedge, diffidence.

## Essay moves practised

- A body paragraph whose topic sentence names its reason and whose last sentence links forward.
- Transitions between paragraphs that name the idea just proved and the idea coming next.
- Rhetorical analysis sentences in the pattern *choice + quality + attitude + purpose*.
- Revising a draft's tone for a formal audience without weakening its position.

## Common traps

- Building an argument on *also* and *another reason*.
- Using a causal transition for two facts that merely happened together.
- Writing *the author uses diction and syntax* instead of quoting and explaining.
- Confusing a grave subject with a grave tone.
- Overclaiming (*obviously, everyone knows*) or over-hedging (*maybe, I guess*).
- Mixing comparisons in one paragraph.

## What the unit exam looks like

Forty-four questions: new passages from Bacon (“Of Adversity”), Johnson (Idler No. 31) and Franklin, a passage you have not studied from Joseph Addison's *Spectator* No. 10 (1711), the paper Franklin taught himself to write from, and a nine-question revision set on a student's argument. Then eight questions across the unit, including two short responses, and a 6-point rhetorical analysis essay on the Addison passage.
