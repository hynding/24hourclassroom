# Exam Prep Guide · AP Calculus BC

**Use it:** in week 33, before the practice final in week 34
**Covers:** all ten units, every one of which the AP Calculus BC exam tests
**Framework:** AP Calculus AB and BC Course and Exam Description; exam format from AP Central, May 2027 exam

This guide explains the May 2027 AP Calculus BC exam and its calculator rules, shows how the practice final rehearses it, explains how free responses are scored, and collects the facts and justification sentences to know without looking anything up. Units 1–8 are summarised briefly (the AB exam-prep guide covers them in full); the BC-only topics get the detail. It ends with a two-week review plan. If you are taking calculus as a regular course, the whole practice final is your final exam.

## The AP exam in May 2027

The exam is on **Monday, May 10, 2027**, in the first session of the day. It is a hybrid digital exam: the multiple-choice and free-response questions appear in the Bluebook app, multiple-choice answers are entered there, and free-response answers are **handwritten** in a paper booklet.

| Section | Part | Questions | Time | Calculator | Share of score |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I: Multiple choice | A | 29 | 62 minutes | not allowed | 50% for all of Section I |
| I: Multiple choice | B | 13 | 38 minutes | graphing calculator required | (Part A and Part B together) |
| II: Free response | A | 2 (questions 1 and 2) | 30 minutes | graphing calculator required | 50% for all of Section II |
| II: Free response | B | 4 (questions 3 to 6) | 60 minutes | not allowed | (Part A and Part B together) |

Section I is 42 questions in 1 hour 40 minutes; Section II is 6 questions in 1 hour 30 minutes, 15 minutes and 9 points each. Each section is half the score. The multiple-choice section used to have 30 + 15 questions; from May 2027 it is 29 + 13, so older practice books have the wrong shape. There is no penalty for a wrong multiple-choice answer, so never leave one blank.

### Calculator rules

On the calculator parts you are expected to use a graphing calculator, and the exam assumes it can do four things. Practise each until it takes seconds:

1. **Graph a function** in any viewing window, to see where it is positive and where two graphs cross.
2. **Solve an equation numerically**: find a zero, or the intersection of two graphs, to at least three decimal places.
3. **Calculate the numerical derivative of a function at a point**, such as G′(5) for G(t) = 90t·e^(−0.25t).
4. **Calculate the value of a definite integral numerically**, such as the arc length ∫ from 0 to 1.5 of √(1 + x⁶) dx.

BC adds three calculator habits on top of the AB ones:

- **Radian mode, always.** A calculator left in degree mode gives wrong values for every trigonometric function; question 30 of the practice final has a trap that only a degree-mode calculator falls into.
- **Store, don't copy.** Intersection values such as θ or t are used again as limits of integration. Store them in the calculator; rounding 1.4296 to 1.43 before integrating can push an answer outside the accepted three-decimal range.
- **Write mathematics, not keystrokes.** On a free response, "(1/2) ∫ from π/6 to 5π/6 of ((3 + 2 sin θ)² − 16) dθ ≈ 6.022" earns the setup point; "fnInt(…)" does not. Show the integral or derivative, with its limits, then the value.

Check AP Central's calculator policy before May for the approved models.

## How the practice final maps onto it

The practice final has 70 questions. **Questions 1–48 are a full mock AP exam** in the order and timing above:

- Questions 1–29: Section I, Part A. No calculator. All ten units, roughly in proportion to the BC weightings.
- Questions 30–42: Section I, Part B. Graphing calculator required; each prompt starts "(Calculator.)". Several need a numerical derivative or integral, an equation solved numerically, or a Lagrange or alternating series error bound evaluated.
- Questions 43–44: Section II, Part A, with a calculator. Question 43 is a rate-in/rate-out context (grain loaded into a silo while it is removed at a constant rate), with a candidates test for the least amount; question 44 is a polar question on a limaçon and a circle: an area between two polar curves, the meaning of dr/dθ, the slope of a tangent line, and an integral set up for the other region.
- Questions 45–48: Section II, Part B, no calculator. An accumulation function defined by a graph, ending with an improper integral; a differential equation with a tangent-line approximation, a concavity argument, Euler's method and separation of variables; an area, volume and perimeter question whose perimeter needs an arc length integral; and a power series with its interval of convergence, its derivative as a geometric series, a Taylor polynomial and an alternating series error bound.

About two fifths of questions 1–48 test BC-only content, which matches the real exam: Units 9 and 10 alone are between a quarter and about a third of the score (25–35%), and the BC topics inside Units 6–8 add more. Each free-response question is scored out of 9 with scoring notes in the style of the AP rubrics. Sit questions 1–48 in one block with the AP timings (3 hours 10 minutes, plus a 10-minute break after question 42). Don't convert your total into a 1–5 score (cut points are not published in advance); note the topic of every lost point.

**Questions 49–70** are not AP format: select-all, true/false, fill-in-the-blank, numeric and short-answer items across all ten units, opening with four questions on one particle moving in the plane. Do them on another day, in about 45 minutes.

## The free-response questions and how they are scored

Six questions, 9 points each. The contexts change every year; the shapes are steady. On the BC exam, some questions are shared with AB (usually a rates question, a table, a graph of f′ or a differential equation) and some are BC-only:

- **Rates in and out or a table of values** (calculator). Integrate a rate to get an amount, compare rates to decide whether an amount is increasing, run a candidates test, estimate a derivative or an integral from data.
- **Parametric, vector or polar** (often calculator). For motion in the plane: velocity vector, speed, position from an initial point plus an integral of each component, total distance as the integral of speed. For polar: area with (1/2) ∫ r² dθ, intersections as limits, the sign of dr/dθ as moving toward or away from the origin, and slopes through x = r cos θ and y = r sin θ.
- **Graph of f or f′ with an accumulation function.** Values from signed areas, extrema and concavity from the graph, and on BC an improper integral or a limit by L'Hospital's Rule tacked on.
- **Differential equations.** Slopes, a tangent-line approximation with a concavity check, Euler's method, separation of variables, and sometimes a logistic equation's limit and fastest growth.
- **Area, volume, arc length.** Area between curves, cross sections, washers about a line that is not an axis, and on BC an arc length or perimeter.
- **Series** (almost always the last question, no calculator). A Taylor polynomial from given derivatives or a known series, an interval of convergence with endpoint tests, and an error bound: the alternating series bound or the Lagrange bound.

**How the 9 points work.** Each point is attached to one specific thing, and the scoring notes in this course name it:

- **Setup points** go to the correct integral or derivative expression, with its limits and constants: the 1/2 in a polar area, the π in a volume, the square root in an arc length.
- **Answer points** go to the value: three decimal places on calculator questions, exact on no-calculator questions (16π and 125/6 need no decimal approximation).
- **Justification points** go to a reason that uses the right evidence in a complete sentence, and on series questions to naming the test and checking its conditions.
- **Units and meaning points** go to an interpretation that names the quantity, the time and the units.
- **Consistency.** A wrong answer carried correctly into a later part can still earn the later points, so always finish a question.

## Know these cold: Units 1–8 in brief

The AB exam-prep guide has these unit by unit; this is the short list BC questions lean on.

- **Justification sentences.** IVT: "f is continuous on [a, b] and k is between f(a) and f(b), so there is a c in (a, b) with f(c) = k." MVT: "f is continuous on [a, b] and differentiable on (a, b), so there is a c in (a, b) with f′(c) = (f(b) − f(a))/(b − a)." Relative extremum: "f′ changes from positive to negative at x = 2, so f has a relative maximum there." Inflection: "f″ changes sign at x = 3" (or f′ changes from increasing to decreasing). Absolute extremum: a candidates test with the endpoints and every critical point.
- **Derivatives.** Chain rule every time; implicit differentiation with dy/dx on every y term; (f⁻¹)′(b) = 1/f′(a) where f(a) = b; d/dx arctan x = 1/(1 + x²).
- **Motion.** Speeding up when velocity and acceleration have the same sign. Total distance is ∫ |v(t)| dt.
- **L'Hospital's Rule** for 0/0 or ∞/∞, stating the form each time.
- **Accumulation.** g(x) = g(a) + ∫ from a to x of g′(t) dt; d/dx ∫ from a to u(x) of f(t) dt = f(u(x)) · u′(x).
- **Integration by parts (6.11).** ∫ u dv = uv − ∫ v du. Choose u to simplify when differentiated (a polynomial, ln x, arctan x) and dv to be easy to integrate. For ∫ x sec² x dx: u = x, v = tan x, result x tan x + ln|cos x| + C.
- **Partial fractions (6.12).** Linear factors only on the AP exam: 2/(x² − 1) = 1/(x − 1) − 1/(x + 1).
- **Improper integrals (6.13).** Replace the infinite limit, or the bad point, with a variable and take a limit: ∫ from a to ∞ of f(x) dx = lim (b→∞) ∫ from a to b of f(x) dx. Write the limit on the page; "plugging in ∞" loses the point. ∫ from 1 to ∞ of 1/xᵖ dx converges exactly when p > 1.
- **Euler's method (7.5).** New y = old y + (step) · (slope at the old point). For a concave-up solution, Euler steps fall below the curve.
- **Logistic models (7.9).** dP/dt = kP(1 − P/L): solutions starting between 0 and L increase toward L; the growth rate is greatest at P = L/2, where the graph of P has its inflection point; a solution starting above L decreases toward L.
- **Area and volume.** ∫ (top − bottom) dx or ∫ (right − left) dy; washers π ∫ (R² − r²) with radii measured from the axis of revolution.
- **Arc length (8.13).** For y = f(x) on [a, b]: ∫ from a to b of √(1 + (f′(x))²) dx. If the integrand blows up at an endpoint, write the curve as x = g(y) instead.

## Know these cold: Unit 9

- **Parametric derivatives.** dy/dx = (dy/dt)/(dx/dt). Horizontal tangent where dy/dt = 0 and dx/dt ≠ 0; vertical tangent where dx/dt = 0 and dy/dt ≠ 0.
- **Second derivative.** d²y/dx² = (d/dt (dy/dx))/(dx/dt). The most common slip is stopping after d/dt.
- **Parametric arc length.** ∫ from a to b of √((dx/dt)² + (dy/dt)²) dt.
- **Vector-valued motion.** For r(t) = ⟨x(t), y(t)⟩: velocity v(t) = ⟨x′(t), y′(t)⟩, acceleration a(t) = ⟨x″(t), y″(t)⟩, **speed = √(x′(t)² + y′(t)²)**. At rest when both components of velocity are 0.
- **Position from velocity.** x(b) = x(a) + ∫ from a to b of x′(t) dt, and the same for y; never forget the initial position.
- **Distance versus displacement.** Total distance travelled is ∫ from a to b of speed dt; the distance between the start and end points is the magnitude of the displacement vector, and it is smaller unless the path is straight.
- **Polar to rectangular.** x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ. Slope: dy/dx = (dy/dθ)/(dx/dθ), using the product rule on r(θ) sin θ and r(θ) cos θ.
- **dr/dθ.** When r > 0, dr/dθ > 0 means the point is moving away from the origin as θ increases, and dr/dθ < 0 means it is moving toward the origin. The sentence must mention the origin (distance from the pole), not "the curve is decreasing".
- **Polar area.** (1/2) ∫ from α to β of r² dθ, over an interval that traces the region exactly once. Between two curves: (1/2) ∫ (R² − r²) dθ, with the limits from where the curves meet (set the two r's equal). Never square the difference of the radii.

## Know these cold: Unit 10

- **Geometric series.** ∑ arⁿ converges to (first term)/(1 − r) exactly when |r| < 1. Check where the sum starts before naming the first term.
- **nth term test.** If the terms do not tend to 0, the series diverges. Terms tending to 0 prove nothing.
- **p-series and harmonic series.** ∑ 1/nᵖ converges when p > 1 and diverges when p ≤ 1. The harmonic series ∑ 1/n diverges; the alternating harmonic series converges.
- **Integral test.** For f positive, continuous and decreasing, ∑ f(n) and ∫ f(x) dx both converge or both diverge. State the three conditions.
- **Comparison tests.** Direct comparison needs an inequality between positive terms in the right direction; limit comparison needs lim aₙ/bₙ to be positive and finite.
- **Alternating series test.** ∑ (−1)ⁿ bₙ with bₙ > 0 converges when bₙ decreases (at least eventually) and bₙ → 0. Name both conditions.
- **Ratio test.** L = lim |aₙ₊₁/aₙ|: converges absolutely if L < 1, diverges if L > 1, says nothing if L = 1. It is the standard tool for power series and for terms with factorials or nth powers.
- **Absolute and conditional convergence.** Conditional means ∑ aₙ converges but ∑ |aₙ| diverges, as for ∑ (−1)ⁿ/√n.
- **Alternating series error bound.** If an alternating series satisfies the test, |S − Sₙ| is at most the first omitted term, bₙ₊₁. Name the bound when you use it.
- **Taylor polynomials.** Pₙ(x) = f(a) + f′(a)(x − a) + f″(a)(x − a)²/2! + ⋯ + f⁽ⁿ⁾(a)(x − a)ⁿ/n!. The coefficient of (x − a)ᵏ is f⁽ᵏ⁾(a)/k!; reading a derivative back from a coefficient means multiplying by k!.
- **Lagrange error bound.** |f(x) − Pₙ(x)| ≤ max|f⁽ⁿ⁺¹⁾(z)| · |x − a|ⁿ⁺¹/(n + 1)!, with the maximum taken over z between a and x. Show the maximum you used.
- **Radius and interval of convergence.** Use the ratio test to get |x − a| < R, then **test both endpoints separately**, usually with the alternating series test, a p-series or the nth term test. The endpoint analysis is a point on its own.
- **Maclaurin series to know.** eˣ = 1 + x + x²/2! + x³/3! + ⋯ (all x); sin x = x − x³/3! + x⁵/5! − ⋯ (all x); cos x = 1 − x²/2! + x⁴/4! − ⋯ (all x); 1/(1 − x) = 1 + x + x² + x³ + ⋯ (|x| < 1).
- **Building new series.** Substitute (sin(2x), e^(−x²)), multiply by a power of x, differentiate or integrate term by term. Differentiating and integrating keep the radius, but the endpoints must be checked again.

## A two-week review plan

**Week 33 (this week): rebuild the toolkit.**

- Day 1: Units 1–5 in one pass with the AB unit summaries. Write every justification sentence above from memory, then fifteen minutes of mixed derivative drills.
- Day 2: Unit 6 and the BC integration techniques. Twenty integrals, choosing between substitution, parts and partial fractions before starting each, then three improper integrals with the limit written out.
- Day 3: Units 7 and 8. One Euler table, one separation of variables from start to finish, one logistic question, one volume about a line that is not an axis, one arc length set-up.
- Day 4: Unit 9. One particle-in-the-plane question (speed, position, distance) and one polar area between two curves, set up by hand before the calculator.
- Day 5: Unit 10. A sheet of fifteen series: name the test and its conditions for each. Then write three Taylor polynomials, one interval of convergence with both endpoints, and one error bound of each kind. Confirm radian mode on the calculator.

**Week 34: the practice final.**

- Day 1: Sit questions 1–48 under AP timing, in one sitting, with the break after question 42.
- Day 2: Score it with the explanations and the 9-point scoring notes. For every lost point, write one line: what the question tested and what you will do differently. Count your losses by unit and compare with the weightings above.
- Day 3: Re-teach yourself the two topics that cost you the most points, using their weekly guides and flashcards.
- Day 4: Questions 49–70, timed at 45 minutes.
- Day 5: Redo every free-response question you scored under 6 out of 9, writing full justifications, then stop. Rest matters more than one more set.

Until the exam, alternate a timed multiple-choice block with one free-response question every other day, often a series or polar one.

## Test-day reminders

- Bring a charged, approved graphing calculator and pencils and a pen for the booklet.
- In Section I, mark any question that takes longer than three minutes and come back to it. Answer everything.
- In Section II, read every part of a question before writing, show each setup, name every test you use with its conditions, and give exact answers on the no-calculator part.
