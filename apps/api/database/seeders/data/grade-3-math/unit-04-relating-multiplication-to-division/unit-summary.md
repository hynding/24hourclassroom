# Unit 4 · Relating Multiplication to Division · Unit summary

**Weeks:** 12–16 · **Standards:** 3.OA.A.2, 3.OA.B.6, 3.OA.A.4, 3.NBT.A.3, 3.OA.B.5, 3.OA.C.7, 3.OA.A.3

## The big ideas

1. **Division splits a total into equal groups.** Sometimes you know how many groups and find how many go in each (sharing). Sometimes you know how many go in each group and find how many groups (making groups). Both are division: 24 ÷ 4 = 6.
2. **Every division is a multiplication with a missing factor.** 42 ÷ 6 asks "6 times what is 42?" Because 6 × 7 = 42, the answer is 7. The numbers 6, 7 and 42 make a fact family of four equations.
3. **Multiples of 10 are numbers of tens.** 30 is 3 tens, so 4 × 30 is 12 tens, which is 120.
4. **Big factors can be split.** 6 × 14 is 6 × 10 plus 6 × 4. A rectangle cut into two parts shows why.
5. **Bigger divisions use the same ideas.** Share the tens and then the ones, or split the total into friendly parts, or skip-count on a number line. Always check with multiplication.
6. **Word problems have three numbers.** Groups, size of each group and total. Two are given; find the third.

## Strategies and examples

| Strategy | When it helps | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Deal out one at a time | Sharing a small total | 15 strawberries on 3 plates: 5 on each |
| Circle groups | Making groups | 24 pencils in cups of 4: 6 cups |
| Think of the missing factor | Any division fact | 54 ÷ 9: 9 × 6 = 54, so 6 |
| Fact family | Turning one fact into four | 4 × 7 = 28, 7 × 4 = 28, 28 ÷ 4 = 7, 28 ÷ 7 = 4 |
| Think in tens | One-digit number times a multiple of 10 | 7 × 50 = 35 tens = 350 |
| Split a factor | Products like 6 × 14 | 6 × 10 + 6 × 4 = 60 + 24 = 84 |
| Share tens, then ones | Dividing when the tens share evenly | 48 ÷ 4: 1 ten and 2 ones each, so 12 |
| Friendly parts | Dividing when they do not | 52 ÷ 4: 40 ÷ 4 = 10, 12 ÷ 4 = 3, so 13 |
| Skip-count on a number line | Seeing how many groups | 54 ÷ 6: 9 jumps of 6 |
| Tape diagram | Any equal-groups story | 7 parts of 8 make 56 |

## Words to know

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Division | Splitting a total into equal groups. |
| Quotient | The answer to a division. |
| Factor | A number you multiply. |
| Product | The answer to a multiplication. |
| Fact family | Multiplication and division equations with the same three numbers. |
| Multiple of 10 | A number you reach counting by 10s. |
| Distributive property | Splitting a factor into parts, multiplying each part, then adding. |
| Unknown | The number you are looking for, shown with ?, a box or a letter. |

## Watch out for

- **Mixing up the total.** In 20 ÷ 5, the total is 20. It does not mean 20 groups of 5.
- **Adding or subtracting instead.** For 36 ÷ ? = 4, the answer is not 32 or 40. Think 4 × ? = 36: the answer is 9.
- **Forgetting the tens.** 3 × 90 is 27 tens, which is 270, not 27.
- **Multiplying only one part.** 6 × 14 is 6 × 10 + 6 × 4, not 6 × 10 + 4.
- **Counting tick marks instead of jumps.** From 0 to 54 by 6s there are 10 tick marks but 9 jumps.
- **Stopping one step early.** Read the question again. Does it ask for the total, or how many in each group?

## Practice set

1. 36 crackers are shared equally by 4 friends. How many does each friend get?
2. 40 students make teams of 5. How many teams?
3. Write the fact family for 6, 9 and 54.
4. Find the unknown: 8 × ? = 64.
5. 6 × 70 = ?
6. Use splitting to find 5 × 16.
7. 96 ÷ 4 = ?
8. A bus has 9 rows with 4 seats in each row. How many seats?
9. 63 pencils go in boxes of 7. Write an equation with an unknown and solve it.

*Answers:* (1) 9 crackers. (2) 8 teams. (3) 6 × 9 = 54, 9 × 6 = 54, 54 ÷ 6 = 9, 54 ÷ 9 = 6. (4) 8. (5) 42 tens = 420. (6) 5 × 10 + 5 × 6 = 50 + 30 = 80. (7) 96 = 80 + 16; 80 ÷ 4 = 20 and 16 ÷ 4 = 4, so 24. (8) 9 × 4 = 36 seats. (9) 63 ÷ 7 = ? or 7 × ? = 63; 9 boxes.

## For grown-ups at home

This unit links the multiplication children learned in the fall to division. The most useful habit is saying each division as a multiplication question: "48 ÷ 6 is 6 times what?" Short, regular practice with multiplication facts pays off here, because every fact your child knows also gives two division facts.
