# Week 04 · Turn-Around Facts and Patterns in the Times Table

**Standards:** 3.OA.B.5 Use properties of operations to multiply and divide · 3.OA.D.9 Find and explain patterns in numbers
**Built from:** IM K–5 Math Grade 3, Unit 1, Section C, in our own words

## What you will learn

- See why 3 × 5 and 5 × 3 have the same product.
- Use a fact you know to find its **turn-around fact**.
- Read a **multiplication table**.
- Find and explain patterns, like even and odd products.

## Words to know

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Turn-around fact | The same two factors in the other order, like 2 × 8 and 8 × 2. |
| Commutative property | The rule that you can multiply in either order and get the same product. |
| Multiplication table | A grid that shows the products of the factors along its top and side. |
| Pattern | Something that repeats or changes in the same way each time. |
| Even number | A number that splits into 2 equal groups with none left over. |
| Odd number | A number that has 1 left over when you split it into 2 equal groups. |

## Turn an array

Draw an array with 2 rows of 6 dots. That is 2 × 6 = 12.

Now turn your paper on its side. The rows become columns. You now see 6 rows of 2 dots. That is 6 × 2.

Did any dots appear or disappear? No. There are still 12. So **2 × 6 = 6 × 2**.

This works for every pair of factors. You can multiply in either order and get the same product. Grown-ups call this the **commutative property**. We can call them **turn-around facts**.

**Try it.** 3 × 7 = 21. What is 7 × 3? It is the turn-around fact, so it is also 21.

## Why turn-around facts help

Turn-around facts cut the facts you need to learn almost in half. If you know 2 × 9 = 18, you also know 9 × 2 = 18.

Groups can look different even when the totals match. 4 bags of 3 grapes and 3 bags of 4 grapes both have 12 grapes. The bags are not the same, but the total is.

A common mistake is to think the bigger first number means a bigger product. Kai says 8 × 3 is more than 3 × 8 because 8 comes first. Both are 24. Draw both arrays and count to check.

## The multiplication table

A multiplication table is a grid. One factor is along the top. The other is down the side. The product sits where the row and the column meet.

To find 4 × 5, go across the row for 4 and down the column for 5. They meet at **20**.

The table matches on both sides of the line from 1 × 1 down to the far corner. That is because every fact has a turn-around fact. 3 × 6 and 6 × 3 are both 18.

## Patterns in the table

Look along the rows and you will spot patterns.

- **The 2s row:** 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12. All even. Any number times 2 is even, because it is 2 equal groups.
- **The 5s row:** 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30. The products end in 5 or 0, taking turns.
- **The 10s row:** 10, 20, 30, 40. Every product ends in 0.
- **The 4s row:** each product is double the one in the 2s row. 2 × 6 = 12 and 4 × 6 = 24.

When you explain a pattern, say what changes and why. "Each product in the 5s row is 5 more than the one before, because we add one more group of 5."

**Try it.** Is 7 × 3 even or odd? Both factors are odd. 7 × 3 = 21, and 21 is odd. Odd times odd is always odd.

## For grown-ups at home

Turn-around facts are a big help with learning facts. If your child knows 2 × 7, ask what 7 × 2 is and why. Draw an array on paper and turn it together. You can also print a blank 10 by 10 grid and fill in a multiplication table over a few days, looking for patterns as you go.

## Check yourself

1. Write the turn-around fact for 5 × 9 = 45.
2. 6 × 4 = 4 × ?
3. Is 2 × 7 even or odd? How do you know?
4. What comes next: 4, 8, 12, 16, ___?

*Answers:* (1) 9 × 5 = 45. (2) 6. (3) Even: 14, and any number × 2 is even. (4) 20, because the pattern adds 4.
