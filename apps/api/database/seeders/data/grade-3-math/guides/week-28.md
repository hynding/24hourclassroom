# Week 28 · Designing With Area and Perimeter

**Standards:** 3.MD.D.8 Solve problems about perimeter · 3.G.A.1 Sort shapes by their attributes, including quadrilaterals
**Built from:** IM K–5 Math Grade 3, Unit 7, Section D, in our own words

## What you will learn

- Find **hidden sides** on a plan where every corner is a square corner.
- Find the perimeter and the area of a shape made of rectangles.
- Decide whether a job needs **perimeter** or **area**.
- Design a shape that follows a rule, and name it.

## Words to know

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Plan | A drawing of something from above, with its side lengths. |
| Perimeter | The length all the way around a shape. |
| Area | How many unit squares cover a shape. |
| Design rule | What a design must do, such as "perimeter of 20 m". |

## Finding hidden sides

Picture a playground plan shaped like a big rectangle with its top right corner cut out. Every corner is a square corner. The bottom is 12 m, the left side is 6 m, the top is 8 m and the short right side is 2 m. Two sides, a and b, have no numbers.

Think about going straight across. The bottom is 12 m wide, so the top of the playground must also add up to 12 m. The top has the 8 m side and side a, so a = 12 − 8 = 4 m.

Now go straight up. The left side is 6 m tall. On the right, the 2 m side and side b stack up to the same height, so b = 6 − 2 = 4 m.

Then the perimeter is every side added once: 12 + 2 + 4 + 4 + 8 + 6 = 36 m.

## Area by splitting into rectangles

To find the area, cut the plan into rectangles you can multiply. One way: a tall rectangle 8 m by 6 m (8 × 6 = 48 square meters) and a short one 4 m by 2 m (4 × 2 = 8 square meters). 48 + 8 = 56 square meters.

A common mistake is to multiply the two longest sides, 12 × 6 = 72. That is the area of the whole box around the playground, including the corner that was cut out.

## Perimeter or area?

Ask: does the job go **around the edge** or **cover the inside**?

| Job | Measure |
|---|---|
| A fence around a yard | Perimeter |
| Ribbon around a bulletin board | Perimeter |
| Tiles on a floor | Area |
| Paint on a wall | Area |

**Try it.** Fringe along the edge of a rug is perimeter. The cloth that covers the floor is area.

## Designing to a rule

A design rule tells you what must be true. Then you choose the rest.

- **Rule: perimeter 20 m, whole-meter sides.** Length + width = 10, so you could pick 1 by 9, 2 by 8, 3 by 7, 4 by 6 or 5 by 5. If you want the most room, pick 5 by 5 (25 square meters).
- **Rule: area 24 square feet, least border.** The factor pairs are 1 by 24, 2 by 12, 3 by 8 and 4 by 6. Their perimeters are 50, 28, 22 and 20 ft, so the 4 by 6 rectangle, the one closest to a square, needs the least border.

Shape names still matter in a design. A 5 m by 5 m bed with square corners is a square, so it is also a rectangle and a rhombus. A flower bed with four 6 ft sides that leans over is a rhombus. Its perimeter is still 6 + 6 + 6 + 6 = 24 ft, because leaning over changes the angles, not the side lengths.

## For grown-ups at home

Plan something real together: a sandbox, a pet pen, a poster with a border, or a new arrangement of a bedroom rug. Set a rule ("we have 16 feet of trim") and let your child list the rectangles that fit, then pick the best one and explain why. Sketching on grid paper helps. Ask, "Does this job go around the edge or cover the inside?" whenever you measure something.

## Check yourself

1. A plan has square corners. Its bottom is 12 m and the top is made of a 7 m side and a hidden side. How long is the hidden side?
2. Which needs area: putting a border on a quilt, or buying cloth to cover the whole quilt?
3. Design a rectangle with whole-number sides and a perimeter of 14 m. Give its area.
4. A rhombus has sides of 9 cm. What is its perimeter?

*Answers:* (1) 12 − 7 = 5 m. (2) The cloth to cover the quilt. (3) Any pair that adds to 7: 1 by 6 (area 6), 2 by 5 (area 10) or 3 by 4 (area 12). (4) 4 × 9 = 36 cm.
