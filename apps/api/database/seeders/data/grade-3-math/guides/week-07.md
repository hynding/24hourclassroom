# Week 07 · Splitting Rectangles and Finding the Area of Bigger Shapes

**Standards:** 3.MD.C.7 Connect area to multiplication and addition · 3.OA.B.5 Use properties of operations to multiply and divide
**Built from:** IM K–5 Math Grade 3, Unit 2, Section C, in our own words

## What you will learn

- Split a rectangle into two smaller rectangles and add their areas.
- Use a split to find a hard product, like 6 × 7, from two easier ones.
- Find the area of a shape made of rectangles.
- Find a missing side length on a shape like an L.

## Words to know

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Split (decompose) | Break a number or a shape into smaller parts. |
| Factor | A number you multiply. In 6 × 7, the factors are 6 and 7. |
| Product | The answer to a multiplication. |
| Expression | Numbers and signs with no equal sign, like 6 × 5 + 6 × 2. |

## Split a rectangle, add the parts

Picture a rectangle 4 rows tall and 9 squares long. Draw a line down after the fifth column. Now you have two smaller rectangles:

- a blue part, 4 by 5, with area 4 × 5 = 20
- a green part, 4 by 4, with area 4 × 4 = 16

The two parts cover the whole rectangle with no gaps and no overlaps. So the whole area is **20 + 16 = 36** square units. That means 4 × 9 = 36.

We can write the split as one equation:

**4 × 9 = 4 × 5 + 4 × 4**

Notice that the 4 stays in **both** parts. Only the 9 was broken apart, into 5 + 4.

## Use a split to multiply

Splitting helps when you do not know a fact yet. Break one factor into numbers you know well. Fives and twos are handy.

**Try it.** Find 6 × 7. Break the 7 into 5 + 2.

- 6 × 5 = 30
- 6 × 2 = 12
- 30 + 12 = 42, so 6 × 7 = 42

You can check by splitting another way: 6 × 4 + 6 × 3 = 24 + 18 = 42. Same answer!

## Area of shapes made of rectangles

Many real shapes, like a patio, a room or a garden, are not one rectangle. But you can often cut them into rectangles.

1. Draw a line to split the shape into rectangles that touch but do not overlap.
2. Find each rectangle's area by multiplying its side lengths.
3. Add the areas.

**Try it.** A patio is shaped like an L. The bottom part is 7 meters long and 2 meters wide, and the part on top of its left end is 3 meters wide and 4 meters tall.

- Bottom: 7 × 2 = 14 square meters
- Top: 3 × 4 = 12 square meters
- Total: 14 + 12 = **26 square meters**

There is usually more than one way to cut. Cutting the same patio up and down gives a 3 by 6 part on the left and a 4 by 2 part on the right: 18 + 8 = 26. Same area.

## Finding a missing side

Sometimes a side is not labeled. Use the sides you know. If the whole bottom of the L is 7 meters and the top part is 3 meters wide, the step across must be 7 − 3 = 4 meters.

## Watch out

- **Overlaps.** If your two rectangles share squares, those squares get counted twice. In the L above, a 3 by 6 part and a 7 by 2 part overlap in the corner, and 18 + 14 = 32 is too big.
- **Forgetting to multiply a part.** 5 × 8 is not 5 × 4 + 4. Each part needs its own multiplication: 5 × 4 + 5 × 4 = 40.
- **Adding sides.** Adding all the side lengths finds the distance around a shape, not its area.

## For grown-ups at home

Breaking a factor apart is the idea behind many mental math tricks, and it will come back in division and with bigger numbers. Ask your child to show 7 × 8 as 7 × 5 + 7 × 3 on squared paper, or to find the area of an L-shaped room by cutting it into two rectangles. Ask, "Can you cut it a different way and get the same answer?"

## Check yourself

1. Finish the equation: 3 × 8 = 3 × 5 + 3 × ___.
2. Use 5s and 1s to find 9 × 6.
3. A shape is made of a 2 by 6 rectangle and a 4 by 4 rectangle that do not overlap. What is its area?

*Answers:* (1) 3, because 8 = 5 + 3. (2) 9 × 5 + 9 × 1 = 45 + 9 = 54. (3) 12 + 16 = 28 square units.
