# Week 03 · Arrays, Story Problems and Unknown Numbers

**Standards:** 3.OA.A.1 Understand multiplication as equal groups · 3.OA.A.3 Solve multiplication and division word problems within 100 · 3.OA.A.4 Find the unknown number in a multiplication or division equation
**Built from:** IM K–5 Math Grade 3, Unit 1, Sections B and C, in our own words

## What you will learn

- Read an **array** as equal rows and write a multiplication for it.
- Show multiplication with equal jumps on a number line and with a tape diagram.
- Solve story problems about equal groups.
- Find an **unknown** number in an equation like 4 × ? = 28.

## Words to know

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Array | Things set out in equal rows and equal columns. |
| Row | A line that goes across, from left to right. |
| Column | A line that goes up and down. |
| Tape diagram | A bar cut into equal parts. Each part is one group. |
| Unknown | A number we do not know yet. We write it as ? or a box. |
| Equation | A math sentence with an equal sign, like 3 × 5 = 15. |

## Arrays

Look at a muffin tray. The cups are in neat lines. That is an **array**.

An array has rows that go across and columns that go down. Every row has the same number of things, so each row is an equal group.

Say a tray has 3 rows with 4 muffins in each row. That is 3 groups of 4, or **3 × 4**. Count by 4s three times: 4, 8, 12. There are 12 muffins.

**Try it.** A box of paints has 2 rows with 6 paints in each row. Write the multiplication and find the total. It is 2 × 6. Count 6, 12. There are 12 paints.

## Jumps and tapes

A **number line** can show multiplication too. Start at 0 and make equal jumps. Each jump is one group.

Five jumps of 3 land on 3, 6, 9, 12, 15. So 5 × 3 = 15.

Be careful to count the jumps, not the numbers you land on. The 0 where you start is not a jump.

A **tape diagram** is a long bar cut into equal parts. Each part is one group, and the whole bar is the total. A tape with 4 parts of 5 shows 4 × 5 = 20.

## Story problems

Many stories are about equal groups. Use three steps.

1. **How many groups?** Look for words like "4 vans" or "6 shelves".
2. **How many in each group?** Look for "in each", "on every" or "per".
3. **Multiply,** then check that your answer makes sense.

**Try it.** There are 6 shelves with 5 books on each shelf. How many books? 6 groups of 5. Count by 5s: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30. There are 30 books.

## Unknown numbers

Sometimes we know the total but not one of the factors. We write a **?** for the number we do not know.

**Example.** Rosa puts 24 photos on a wall in 4 equal rows. How many photos in each row?

The equation is **4 × ? = 24**. Ask: "4 groups of what make 24?" Count by 6s four times: 6, 12, 18, 24. So the unknown is **6**.

The ? can be in any spot. In ? × 3 = 21, we ask how many 3s make 21. Count 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21. That is 7 threes, so ? = 7. The equal sign can come first too: 18 = 2 × ? means 2 groups of 9 make 18.

A common mistake is to add or subtract the numbers you see. For 4 × ? = 24, some students write 20, because 24 − 4 = 20. Check by multiplying: 4 × 20 is much more than 24. The answer must make the equation true.

## For grown-ups at home

Arrays are everywhere: egg cartons, window panes, muffin tins, rows of chairs. Ask your child how many rows, how many in each row, and how many in all. For unknowns, try a "missing number" game: "I put 15 grapes into 3 equal bowls. How many in each bowl?" Let your child draw or skip-count to find out.

## Check yourself

1. An array has 5 rows of 2. Write the multiplication and the product.
2. Six jumps of 4 on a number line land on what number?
3. Solve 3 × ? = 27.
4. 8 bikes each have 2 wheels. How many wheels?

*Answers:* (1) 5 × 2 = 10. (2) 24. (3) 9, because 3 groups of 9 make 27. (4) 8 × 2 = 16 wheels.
