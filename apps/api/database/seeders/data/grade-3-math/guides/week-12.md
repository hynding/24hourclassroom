# Week 12 · What Is Division? Sharing and Making Groups

**Standard:** 3.OA.A.2 Understand division as sharing or making groups
**Built from:** IM K–5 Math Grade 3, Unit 4, Section A, in our own words

## What you will learn

- Use **division** to split a total into equal groups.
- Solve **sharing** problems: you know how many groups, and you find how many go in each group.
- Solve **making groups** problems: you know how many go in each group, and you find how many groups.
- Read and write division with the ÷ sign, and match a division to a picture or a story.

## Words to know

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Division | Splitting a total into equal groups. |
| ÷ | The division sign. Read 12 ÷ 3 as "12 divided by 3". |
| Equal groups | Groups that all have the same number of things. |
| Quotient | The answer to a division. In 12 ÷ 3 = 4, the quotient is 4. |
| Share equally | Give every group the same amount. |

## Division as sharing

Ravi has 15 strawberries and 3 plates. He wants every plate to have the same number. He puts one strawberry on each plate, then another on each plate, and keeps going until all 15 are used up. Now each plate has 5 strawberries.

We write this as **15 ÷ 3 = 5**.

- 15 is the total: all the strawberries.
- 3 is the number of groups: the plates.
- 5 is the quotient: how many on each plate.

In a sharing problem, you know how many groups there are. You find how many go in each group.

**Try it.** 20 stickers are shared equally by 4 friends. Deal them out one at a time: each friend gets 5. So 20 ÷ 4 = 5.

## Division as making groups

Lena has 24 pencils. She puts 4 pencils in each cup. How many cups does she fill?

This time she knows the size of each group, 4. She makes groups of 4 until the pencils are gone: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24. That is 6 groups, so she fills 6 cups.

We write this as **24 ÷ 4 = 6**.

- 24 is the total.
- 4 is how many go in each group.
- 6 is the quotient: how many groups.

In a making-groups problem, you know how many go in each group. You find how many groups.

**Try it.** 18 students make teams of 3. Count by 3s: 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18. That is 6 teams, so 18 ÷ 3 = 6.

## Pictures that show division

- **Circles or plates.** Draw the groups first, then deal the objects into them.
- **Circling.** Draw all the objects, then circle groups of the right size and count the circles.
- **Tape diagram.** Draw one long bar for the total and cut it into equal parts. A bar for 20 cut into 4 equal parts shows 20 ÷ 4, and each part is 5.

The same equation can tell both kinds of story. 21 ÷ 3 = 7 can mean 21 cars shared into 3 boxes (7 in each box), or 21 cars packed 3 to a box (7 boxes).

## Checking with multiplication

Division and multiplication are partners. Multiplication puts equal groups together. Division takes a total apart into equal groups. To check 32 ÷ 4 = 8, multiply back: 4 groups of 8 make 32. It works!

## A common mistake

The first number in a division is always the **total**. 20 ÷ 5 does not mean 20 groups of 5. That would be 100 things! It means 20 things split into 5 equal groups, or 20 things put in groups of 5. Before you start, ask: "What is the total? Do I know the number of groups, or the size of each group?"

## For grown-ups at home

This week begins division. Children solve it by acting it out first: dealing out cards, sharing snacks onto plates, or packing toys into bags. Let them use real objects. Ask two questions as they work: "How many groups are there?" and "How many are in each group?" Snack time is a good moment: "We have 12 crackers for 4 people. How many does each person get?"

## Check yourself

1. 28 shells are shared equally into 4 buckets. How many shells go in each bucket?
2. 16 socks are put into pairs (groups of 2). How many pairs?
3. What is the quotient in 45 ÷ 5 = 9?
4. Write a sharing story for 12 ÷ 2.

*Answers:* (1) 7 shells, because 4 × 7 = 28. (2) 8 pairs. (3) 9. (4) Any story where 12 things are shared equally by 2, such as 12 crackers shared by 2 friends: each friend gets 6.
