# Week 21 · Measuring to the Half Inch and Quarter Inch, and Line Plots

**Standard:** 3.MD.B.4 Measure lengths to the nearest half and fourth inch and show them on line plots
**Built from:** IM K–5 Math Grade 3, Unit 6, Section A, in our own words

## What you will learn

- Read the marks on a ruler: whole inches, half inches and quarter inches.
- Measure an object to the nearest half inch or quarter inch.
- Find a length when the object does not start at 0.
- Show many measurements on a **line plot** and answer questions from it.

## Words to know

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Inch | A unit of length. A small paper clip is about 1 inch long. |
| Half inch | One of 2 equal parts of an inch, written 1/2 inch. |
| Quarter inch | One of 4 equal parts of an inch, written 1/4 inch. Also called a fourth of an inch. |
| Measurement | A number and a unit that tell how long something is, like 3 1/2 inches. |
| Line plot | A number line with an X above it for each thing measured. |

## Reading a ruler

Look closely at a ruler. Between 0 and 1 there are marks of three sizes.

- The **longest** marks are the whole inches: 1, 2, 3 and so on.
- The **middle** mark sits halfway between two whole inches. It is a **half inch**.
- The **shortest** marks split each half in two again. Now each inch has 4 equal parts, so each part is a **quarter inch**.

You have seen these fractions on number lines. A ruler is just a number line for length. Counting from 3, the marks go 3 1/4, 3 1/2, 3 3/4, then 4. We read 3 1/4 as "three and one-fourth": 3 whole inches and 1/4 of an inch more.

Notice that 2/4 inch and 1/2 inch land on the same mark. They are equivalent fractions, so they are the same length.

**Try it.** What mark comes just before 6? It is one quarter inch less than 6, so it is 5 3/4.

## Measuring an object

1. Line up one end of the object with the **0** mark. On many rulers, 0 is a little way in from the edge, so do not start at the edge.
2. Look at the other end of the object.
3. Find the mark it is closest to. To measure to the nearest **half inch**, use only the whole and half marks. To measure to the nearest **quarter inch**, you may use every mark.

A quarter-inch measurement is more exact, because the marks are closer together.

**Try it.** A marker starts at 0. Its tip lines up with the first short mark after 3. It is 3 1/4 inches long.

## When the object does not start at 0

Sometimes a ruler is broken, or the object is placed further along. Then the end number is not the length. Count the spaces from the start to the end instead.

A piece of yarn starts at 1 and ends at 4 1/2. From 1 to 4 is 3 inches. From 4 to 4 1/2 is another 1/2 inch. The yarn is 3 1/2 inches long.

**Common mistake:** reading only the end number. The yarn above ends at 4 1/2, but it is not 4 1/2 inches long, because the inch from 0 to 1 is not under it.

## Line plots

When a class measures many things, a line plot shows all the lengths at once.

1. Draw a number line that goes from the shortest length to the longest, with equal steps such as quarter inches.
2. Label it, and name the unit: "Length in inches".
3. Put one X above the line for each measurement.

Say a class measured leaves: 2 1/2, 2 3/4, 2 1/2, 3, 2 1/4 and 2 1/2 inches. The line plot has 3 X's above 2 1/2 and 1 X above each of the other lengths.

Now you can ask questions:

- **How many were measured?** Count every X. Here, 6 leaves.
- **Which length came up most?** Find the tallest stack. Here, 2 1/2 inches.
- **How many are longer than 2 1/2 inches?** Count only the X's to the right of 2 1/2. Here, 2 leaves. The X's right on 2 1/2 are not longer.

## For grown-ups at home

This week joins fractions to real measuring. Give your child a ruler and a handful of things to measure: pencils, spoons, leaves, toy cars. Ask them to say each length out loud ("five and one-half inches") and to show you which mark they used. Then make a line plot together on scrap paper. If your ruler also shows centimeters, point out which side shows inches.

## Check yourself

1. On a ruler, which mark is halfway between 7 and 8?
2. A stick starts at 2 and ends at 5 1/4. How long is it?
3. A line plot has 4 X's above 1 1/2, 2 X's above 1 3/4 and 1 X above 2. How many things were measured?

*Answers:* (1) 7 1/2 inches. (2) From 2 to 5 is 3 inches, plus 1/4 inch: 3 1/4 inches. (3) 4 + 2 + 1 = 7 things.
