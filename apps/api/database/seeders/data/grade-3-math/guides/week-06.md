# Week 06 · Area of Rectangles: Multiply the Side Lengths

**Standard:** 3.MD.C.7 Connect area to multiplication and addition
**Built from:** IM K–5 Math Grade 3, Unit 2, Section B, in our own words

## What you will learn

- See that a rectangle covered in unit squares is an **array**: equal rows.
- Find the area of a rectangle by **multiplying** its side lengths.
- Finish a rectangle that is only partly tiled.
- Use **square feet** and **square meters** for real places.
- Find a missing side when you know the area.

## Words to know

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Side length | How long one side of a shape is, like 6 feet. |
| Array | Objects in equal rows, like squares in a tiled rectangle. |
| Square foot | A square 1 foot on each side. |
| Square meter | A square 1 meter on each side. |
| Equation | A number sentence with an equal sign, like 4 × 6 = 24. |

## A tiled rectangle is an array

Last week you counted squares one by one. Look closely at a rectangle covered in unit squares. Every row has the **same number** of squares.

That means you do not have to count them all. A rectangle with 4 rows of 6 squares has 6 + 6 + 6 + 6 squares. That is 4 groups of 6, or **4 × 6 = 24**. The area is 24 square units.

**Try it.** A rectangle has 3 rows of 8 squares. Its area is 3 × 8 = 24 square units. (It is a different shape from the 4 by 6 rectangle, but the area is the same.)

## Multiply the side lengths

Now look at the sides of the rectangle. The short side is as long as 4 squares and the long side is as long as 6 squares. So the side lengths are 4 units and 6 units.

The side lengths tell you the number of rows and the number of squares in each row. So:

**side length × side length = area**

This works even when no squares are drawn. A garden that is 8 feet long and 2 feet wide would hold 2 rows of 8 one-foot squares. Its area is 2 × 8 = **16 square feet**.

**Try it.** A poster is 5 inches by 9 inches. Its area is 5 × 9 = 45 square inches.

## Finishing a partly tiled rectangle

Sometimes only the top row and the left column of squares are drawn. That is enough! The top row shows how many squares go in each row. The left column shows how many rows there are. A top row of 8 and a left column of 4 means 4 × 8 = 32 square units.

## Choosing a unit

Pick a unit that fits the size of the thing you measure.

- Small things, like a sticker or a card: **square centimeters** or **square inches**.
- Bigger things, like a rug, a garden or a classroom floor: **square feet** or **square meters**.

Always write the unit. "16" alone does not say whether you mean 16 square inches or 16 square feet.

## Finding a missing side

If you know the area and one side, think of a multiplication with a missing number. A stage has an area of 24 square meters and one side of 4 meters. Ask: 4 × what = 24? Since 4 × 6 = 24, the other side is **6 meters**.

## Watch out

The most common mistake is to **add** the side lengths. A 4 by 6 rectangle is not 10 square units. Picture the squares: 4 rows of 6 is 24. Another mistake is writing "feet" when you mean "square feet". Feet measure how long; square feet measure how much space.

## For grown-ups at home

Your child is learning why multiplication gives the area of a rectangle, not just the rule. Measure a placemat, a tile floor or a window in whole inches or feet and ask, "How many squares would cover it? How do you know without counting?" Tile floors and sheet cakes cut into squares are great real arrays.

## Check yourself

1. What is the area of a rectangle 4 meters by 8 meters?
2. A rectangle shows a top row of 6 squares and a left column of 3 squares. What is its area when it is full?
3. A rectangle has an area of 28 square inches. One side is 4 inches. How long is the other side?

*Answers:* (1) 4 × 8 = 32 square meters. (2) 3 × 6 = 18 square units. (3) 4 × 7 = 28, so 7 inches.
