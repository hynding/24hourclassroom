# Week 04 · Equivalent Fractions

**Standard:** 4.NF.A.1 Explain why fractions are equivalent and make equivalent fractions
**Built from:** IM K–5 Math Grade 4, Unit 2, Section B, in our own words

## What you will learn

- Explain what makes two fractions **equivalent**.
- Make equivalent fractions by cutting parts into smaller equal parts.
- Use the rule: **multiply the numerator and the denominator by the same number**.
- Check equivalent fractions with area diagrams and number lines.

## Words to know

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Equivalent fractions | Different fractions that name the same amount, like 2/5 and 4/10. |
| Area diagram | A rectangle split into equal parts, with some parts shaded to show a fraction. |
| Numerator | The top number: how many equal parts we count. |
| Denominator | The bottom number: how many equal parts make the whole. |

## Same amount, new name

Draw a rectangle and split it into 5 equal columns. Shade 2 columns. The shaded part is 2/5.

Now draw one line across the middle of the rectangle. Every column is cut into 2 equal pieces. Count again:

- The whole now has **10** pieces.
- The shaded part now has **4** pieces.

So the shaded part is 4/10. But we did not shade anything new or erase anything! The amount is exactly the same. That means **2/5 = 4/10**. They are **equivalent fractions**.

## The multiplying rule

Look at what happened to the numbers:

- The denominator went from 5 to 10: 5 × 2.
- The numerator went from 2 to 4: 2 × 2.

Cutting every part into 2 pieces multiplies **both** numbers by 2. Cutting every part into 3 pieces multiplies both by 3, and so on.

**Rule:** to make an equivalent fraction, multiply the numerator and the denominator by the same number.

| Start | Multiply both by | Equivalent fraction |
|---|---|---|
| 1/4 | 3 | 3/12 |
| 2/3 | 4 | 8/12 |
| 5/6 | 2 | 10/12 |
| 3/10 | 10 | 30/100 |

**Why does it work?** When there are twice as many parts in the whole, each part is half as big. So you need twice as many of them to cover the same amount. The two changes balance out.

## Finding a missing number

**Try it.** 3/4 = ?/8.

Ask: what was the denominator multiplied by? 4 × 2 = 8. Do the same to the numerator: 3 × 2 = 6. So 3/4 = 6/8.

You can also go the other way by dividing. 9/12 = ?/4. The denominator was divided by 3 (12 ÷ 3 = 4), so divide the numerator by 3: 9 ÷ 3 = 3. So 9/12 = 3/4.

Tenths and hundredths work the same way. Each tenth is cut into 10 hundredths, so 4/10 = 40/100 and 7/10 = 70/100.

## Checking on a number line

Draw two number lines from 0 to 1, the same length, one above the other. Split the top one into fifths and the bottom one into tenths. The mark for 3/5 on the top line sits right above the mark for 6/10 on the bottom line. Fractions that land on the same point are equivalent.

You can also check two fractions by rewriting them with the same denominator. Are 4/6 and 6/8 equivalent? Write both in twelfths. 4/6 = 8/12 (multiply both numbers by 2). 6/8 is the same as 3/4 (divide both numbers by 2), and 3/4 = 9/12. Since 8/12 and 9/12 are different, 4/6 and 6/8 are **not** equivalent.

## Watch out

**Adding is not the same as multiplying.** It is tempting to add the same number to the top and bottom: 1/2 → 2/3. But 1/2 and 2/3 are not equal. Two thirds is more than one half. Only multiplying (or dividing) both numbers by the same number keeps the amount the same.

**Change both numbers.** 2/10 is not equivalent to 2/5. If you change the denominator, the numerator must change by the same factor.

## For grown-ups at home

Folding paper is a great way to see equivalent fractions. Fold a sheet in half and shade one half. Fold it in half again the other way and open it: now there are 4 parts and 2 are shaded. Fold once more: 8 parts, 4 shaded. Ask your child what stayed the same and what changed. Measuring cups also help: two 1/4 cups fill the same amount as one 1/2 cup.

## Check yourself

1. Write a fraction in eighths that is equivalent to 1/2.
2. 2/3 = ?/12
3. Is 3/5 equivalent to 6/10?
4. Is 1/3 equivalent to 2/4? Why or why not?

*Answers:* (1) 4/8. (2) 8. (3) Yes: multiply both numbers by 2. (4) No: 2/4 equals 1/2, and 1/3 is less than 1/2. Adding 1 to the top and bottom does not make an equivalent fraction.
