# Week 29 · Reasoning With Fractions and Mixed Numbers

**Standards:** 4.NF.B.3 Add and subtract fractions and mixed numbers with like denominators · 4.NF.A.2 Compare fractions with different numerators and denominators
**Built from:** IM K–5 Math Grade 4, Unit 9, Section A, in our own words

## What you will learn

- Add and subtract fractions that have the same denominator, and say why the denominator stays the same.
- Add and subtract **mixed numbers**, trading a whole for pieces when you need to.
- Break a fraction into a sum of smaller fractions in more than one way.
- Compare two fractions using 1/2, the missing piece, or a common denominator.

## Words to know

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Numerator | The top number. It counts the pieces. In 5/8 it is 5. |
| Denominator | The bottom number. It names the size of the pieces. In 5/8 it is 8. |
| Mixed number | A whole number and a fraction written together, like 2 3/4. |
| Decompose | Break a number into parts that add up to it. 5/6 = 2/6 + 3/6. |
| Benchmark | A friendly number you compare with, such as 0, 1/2 or 1. |
| Common denominator | The same denominator for two fractions, so you can compare or add them. |

## Adding and subtracting pieces of the same size

When the denominators match, the pieces are the same size. You only count pieces.

3/8 + 4/8 = 7/8, because 3 eighths and 4 eighths make 7 eighths. The answer is not 7/16. Adding eighths to eighths cannot make the pieces smaller.

Subtracting works the same way: 9/10 − 4/10 = 5/10.

You can also **decompose** a fraction. 5/6 is 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6. It is also 2/6 + 3/6, or 1/6 + 4/6. Any parts work if they are all sixths and their numerators add to 5.

**Try it.** A loaf is cut into 12 slices. Sam eats 2/12 and Rina eats 5/12. Together they eat 7/12, so 12/12 − 7/12 = 5/12 of the loaf is left.

## Mixed numbers

A mixed number like 2 3/4 means 2 wholes and 3 fourths more. Each whole is 4 fourths, so 2 3/4 = 8/4 + 3/4 = 11/4. Going the other way, 7/5 = 5/5 + 2/5 = 1 2/5.

**Adding.** Add the wholes, then add the fractions. If the fractions make more than a whole, turn that whole into a whole number.

1 5/6 + 2 4/6: wholes 1 + 2 = 3, sixths 5/6 + 4/6 = 9/6 = 1 3/6. Then 3 + 1 3/6 = 4 3/6.

**Subtracting.** Sometimes the top fraction is too small. Trade 1 whole for a full set of pieces, just like trading a ten for 10 ones.

4 1/8 − 1 3/8: you cannot take 3/8 from 1/8. Trade 1 whole for 8/8, so 4 1/8 becomes 3 9/8. Now 3 9/8 − 1 3/8 = 2 6/8.

A number line helps too. To find 2 3/4 − 1 1/4, start at 1 1/4 and count up: 1 whole gets you to 2 1/4, and 2 more fourths get you to 2 3/4. The difference is 1 2/4.

**Try it.** Ana has 3 1/4 feet of ribbon and cuts off 1 3/4 feet. Trade a whole: 3 1/4 = 2 5/4. Then 2 5/4 − 1 3/4 = 1 2/4 feet.

## Comparing fractions four ways

Only compare fractions of the same-size whole. Then pick the way that fits.

1. **Same denominator.** The pieces are the same size, so more pieces is more. 7/10 is greater than 3/10.
2. **Same numerator.** The count is the same, so bigger pieces win. 5/8 is greater than 5/12, because eighths are bigger than twelfths.
3. **Benchmark 1/2.** Half of 8 is 4, so 3/8 is less than 1/2. Half of 6 is 3, so 4/6 is more than 1/2. That means 4/6 is greater than 3/8.
4. **Missing piece or common denominator.** 2/3 and 3/4 are each one piece short of 1. A missing fourth is smaller than a missing third, so 3/4 is greater. Or make the denominators match: 3/5 = 6/10, so 7/10 is greater than 3/5.

**A common mistake.** Some students add the denominators: 1/4 + 3/4 = 4/8. Look at the answer. 4/8 is one half, but you started with 3/4, which is already more than one half. Adding cannot make a number smaller. The right answer is 4/4, which is 1 whole. Another mistake is thinking 1/8 is bigger than 1/6 because 8 is bigger than 6. More pieces in a whole means each piece is smaller.

## For grown-ups at home

Fractions make sense when your child can picture them. Cut a sandwich, fold a strip of paper, or measure with a cup marked in fourths. Ask "Is that more or less than half?" before any exact answer. When subtracting mixed numbers, ask "Do you have enough pieces to take away?" so trading a whole becomes a habit. Praise checking: an answer to 3/4 + 1/4 should be 1, and your child can see that.

## Check yourself

1. 5/12 + 4/12
2. Write 3 2/5 as a fraction.
3. 5 1/4 − 2 3/4
4. Which is greater, 4/10 or 4/8? Explain.
5. Write 7/8 as a sum of two fractions in two different ways.

*Answers:* (1) 9/12. (2) 17/5, because 3 wholes are 15 fifths and 2 more make 17. (3) Trade a whole: 4 5/4 − 2 3/4 = 2 2/4. (4) 4/8, because eighths are bigger pieces than tenths, and both have 4 pieces. (5) For example 3/8 + 4/8 and 1/8 + 6/8.
