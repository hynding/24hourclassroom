# Week 14 · Times As Many: Comparing by Multiplying

**Standards:** 4.OA.A.1 Understand multiplication as a comparison ("times as many") · 4.OA.A.2 Solve word problems that compare by multiplying or dividing
**Built from:** IM K–5 Math Grade 4, Unit 5, Section A, in our own words

## What you will learn

- Read and write **"times as many"** statements, such as "24 is 4 times as many as 6."
- Turn a comparison into an **equation** and back again.
- Draw a **tape diagram** that compares two amounts.
- Solve comparison stories where the larger amount, the smaller amount or the number of times is unknown.
- Tell a "times as many" story from a "how many more" story.

## Words to know

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Compare | Look at two amounts and say how they are related. |
| Multiplicative comparison | A comparison that uses multiplication: one amount is some number of times as many as another. |
| Times as many | Copies of an amount. "3 times as many as 5" means 3 × 5, which is 15. |
| Additive comparison | A comparison that uses adding or subtracting: "how many more" or "how many fewer". |
| Tape diagram | Bars made of equal boxes that show how amounts are related. |
| Unknown | The number a story asks you to find, shown with ?, a box or a letter. |

## Two ways to compare

Suppose Malik has 4 marbles and Sofia has 12 marbles. There are two true ways to compare them.

- **By subtracting:** Sofia has 12 − 4 = 8 **more** marbles than Malik.
- **By multiplying:** Sofia has 3 **times as many** marbles as Malik, because 3 × 4 = 12.

Both sentences are true. They answer different questions. "How many more?" asks for a difference. "How many times as many?" asks how many copies of the smaller amount fit in the larger one.

## Statements and equations

Every multiplication equation can be read as a comparison. Look at 35 = 5 × 7. It says:

- 35 is 5 times as many as 7, **and**
- 35 is 7 times as many as 5.

The larger amount is always the one that is "times as many". The other two numbers are the smaller amount and the number of times.

**Try it.** Write an equation for "54 is 6 times as many as 9." *Answer:* 54 = 6 × 9.

## Draw a tape diagram

A tape diagram makes a comparison easy to see. Draw one box for the smaller amount. Then draw the larger amount as a bar made of that same box, repeated.

Lin has 6 crayons. Dev has 4 times as many crayons. Draw Lin's bar as one box with 6 in it. Draw Dev's bar as 4 boxes, each with 6 in it. Put a question mark over Dev's whole bar. Now the equation is easy to see: 4 × 6 = ?, and Dev has 24 crayons.

The diagram also shows "how many more". Dev's bar is 3 boxes longer than Lin's, so Dev has 3 × 6 = 18 more crayons.

## Three kinds of comparison stories

In every comparison story there are three numbers: the smaller amount, the number of times, and the larger amount. The story gives two and asks for the third.

| What is unknown | Example | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| The larger amount | A cat weighs 5 kg. A dog weighs 6 times as much. How much does the dog weigh? | 6 × 5 = ? (30 kg) |
| The smaller amount | A rope is 40 feet long. That is 5 times as long as a string. How long is the string? | 5 × ? = 40 or 40 ÷ 5 = ? (8 feet) |
| The number of times | Ben has 42 stamps and Ivy has 6. How many times as many stamps does Ben have? | ? × 6 = 42 or 42 ÷ 6 = ? (7 times) |

If the larger amount is unknown, multiply. If the larger amount is given, divide, or find the missing factor.

**Try it.** A tree is 56 feet tall. That is 7 times as tall as a bush. How tall is the bush? *Answer:* 56 ÷ 7 = 8 feet, because 7 × 8 = 56.

## Common mistakes

- **Adding instead of multiplying.** "5 times as many as 6" is 5 × 6 = 30, not 5 + 6 = 11.
- **Multiplying when you should divide.** "A rope is 40 feet, 5 times as long as a string" asks for the shorter amount. 40 × 5 = 200 would make the string longer than the rope. Divide: 40 ÷ 5 = 8.
- **Mixing up "times as many" and "more".** If Ari has 5 cards and Jo has 3 times as many, Jo has 15 cards. That is 10 more, not 3 more.

## For grown-ups at home

Look for comparisons in daily life and say them both ways: "Your sister is 12 and you are 4. She is 8 years older, and she is 3 times as old." Ask your child to sketch a quick tape diagram before solving a story. If the answer for the smaller amount comes out bigger than the larger one, that is a sign to check whether to multiply or divide.

## Check yourself

1. Write two comparison sentences for 28 = 4 × 7.
2. Kofi has 9 toy cars. His cousin has 5 times as many. How many toy cars does his cousin have?
3. A bookshelf holds 63 books. That is 7 times as many books as a small box holds. How many books does the box hold?
4. Nora has 30 beads and Sam has 6. How many times as many beads does Nora have? How many more beads does she have?

*Answers:* (1) 28 is 4 times as many as 7, and 28 is 7 times as many as 4. (2) 5 × 9 = 45 toy cars. (3) 63 ÷ 7 = 9 books. (4) 30 ÷ 6 = 5 times as many; 30 − 6 = 24 more.
