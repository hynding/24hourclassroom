# Week 07 · Adding and Subtracting Fractions With the Same Denominator

**Standard:** 4.NF.B.3 Add and subtract fractions and mixed numbers with like denominators
**Built from:** IM K–5 Math Grade 4, Unit 3, Section B, in our own words

## What you will learn

- Add fractions that have the same denominator by joining parts of the same whole.
- Subtract fractions that have the same denominator by taking parts away.
- **Decompose** a fraction: write it as a sum of smaller fractions, in more than one way.
- Take a fraction away from 1 whole.

## Words to know

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Like denominators | The same bottom number, such as 3/6 and 2/6. The parts are the same size. |
| Sum | The answer to an addition problem. |
| Difference | The answer to a subtraction problem. |
| Decompose | Break a number into parts that add up to it. 4/5 = 1/5 + 3/5. |
| Whole | One full thing. In fifths, 1 whole is 5/5. |

## Adding parts of the same whole

A pan of corn bread is cut into 6 equal pieces. At lunch the class eats 2 pieces, and at snack time they eat 3 more pieces. How much of the pan did they eat?

Each piece is 1/6 of the pan. They ate 2 sixths and then 3 sixths. That is 5 sixths in all:

**2/6 + 3/6 = 5/6**

When the denominators are the same, the pieces are the same size. So you just count pieces: add the numerators and keep the denominator.

You can see it on a number line, too. Split 0 to 1 into fourths. Start at 1/4 and jump forward 2 fourths. You land on 3/4, so 1/4 + 2/4 = 3/4.

Sums can be more than 1. 4/5 + 3/5 = 7/5. Since 5/5 is 1 whole, 7/5 is 2 fifths more than 1.

**Try it.** What is 5/12 + 6/12? *11/12.*

## Taking parts away

Subtracting works the same way. You had 7 tenths of a jar of paint and used 2 tenths. Take away 2 parts from 7 parts:

**7/10 − 2/10 = 5/10**

On a number line, start at the first fraction and jump back (to the left). Start at 7/8 and jump back 4 eighths: you land on 3/8.

Subtraction also answers "how much more" questions. Kim's book bag is 9/10 full and Lou's is 6/10 full. Kim's bag is 9/10 − 6/10 = 3/10 more full.

**Try it.** What is 11/12 − 5/12? *6/12.*

## Taking a fraction away from 1 whole

What is 1 − 3/8? You cannot count eighths in "1" until you write it in eighths. One whole is 8 eighths, so:

**1 − 3/8 = 8/8 − 3/8 = 5/8**

The same idea works for any denominator: 1 = 2/2 = 3/3 = 4/4 = 10/10 = 100/100.

**Try it.** What is 1 − 4/10? *10/10 − 4/10 = 6/10.*

## Decomposing a fraction

To decompose a fraction means to write it as a sum of smaller fractions with the same denominator. There is often more than one way. Here are three ways to decompose 4/5:

- 1/5 + 1/5 + 1/5 + 1/5 (four unit fractions)
- 1/5 + 3/5
- 2/5 + 2/5

Each sum is right because the numerators add up to 4 and every part is a fifth.

Decomposing helps with mental math. To find 4/6 + 5/6, break 5/6 into 2/6 + 3/6. Then 4/6 + 2/6 = 6/6 = 1, and 1 + 3/6 makes 1 and 3/6.

**Try it.** Write 6/10 as a sum of two fractions in two different ways. *For example, 1/10 + 5/10 and 3/10 + 3/10.*

## A common mistake

Many students add the denominators too: 2/6 + 3/6 = 5/12. Check with a picture. Two sixths and three sixths of a pan cover most of the pan, but 5/12 is less than half a pan! The pieces are still sixths, so the sum is 5/6.

## For grown-ups at home

Use things that come in equal parts: a pizza cut into 8 slices, a carton of 12 eggs, or a chocolate bar with 10 squares. Ask, "If you eat 3 of the 8 slices and I eat 2, what fraction of the pizza did we eat? What fraction is left?" Encourage your child to say the size of the part out loud ("3 eighths plus 2 eighths is 5 eighths"). Saying the unit helps them see why the denominator does not change.

## Check yourself

1. What is 3/10 + 5/10?
2. What is 6/8 − 1/8?
3. What is 1 − 5/6?
4. Write 5/12 as a sum of three fractions.
5. Is 2/4 + 3/4 more or less than 1 whole?

*Answers:* (1) 8/10. (2) 5/8. (3) 6/6 − 5/6 = 1/6. (4) For example, 1/12 + 2/12 + 2/12 (any three twelfths whose numerators add to 5). (5) More: 2/4 + 3/4 = 5/4, and 4/4 is 1 whole.
