# Week 30 · Multiplying and Dividing Big Numbers With Confidence

**Standards:** 4.NBT.B.5 Multiply up to four digits by one digit, and two digits by two digits · 4.NBT.B.6 Divide up to four digits by one digit, with remainders
**Built from:** IM K–5 Math Grade 4, Unit 9, Section B, in our own words

## What you will learn

- Multiply a one-digit number by a number with up to four digits, using place value.
- Multiply two two-digit numbers with an area model and **partial products**.
- Divide by a one-digit number with **partial quotients**, and say what a remainder means.
- Check an answer by estimating and by using the opposite operation.

## Words to know

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Product | The answer when you multiply. |
| Partial product | One piece of a product. In 23 × 4, the partial products are 80 and 12. |
| Area model | A rectangle split into parts so each part is an easy multiplication. |
| Quotient | The answer when you divide. |
| Partial quotient | One chunk of a quotient. You add the chunks at the end. |
| Remainder | What is left over when no more full groups fit. 47 ÷ 5 = 9 R 2. |

## Multiplying by one digit

Break the bigger number into its places, multiply each place, then add.

3 × 2,104 = 3 × 2,000 + 3 × 100 + 3 × 4 = 6,000 + 300 + 12 = 6,312.

Say the place names out loud: "3 times 2 thousands is 6 thousands." That keeps the zeros where they belong.

**Try it.** 5 × 1,328. The parts are 5 × 1,000 = 5,000, 5 × 300 = 1,500, 5 × 20 = 100 and 5 × 8 = 40. Add: 5,000 + 1,500 + 100 + 40 = 6,640.

## Two-digit times two-digit

Split **both** factors into tens and ones and draw a rectangle with four parts. For 38 × 27, split 38 into 30 + 8 and 27 into 20 + 7.

| × | 30 | 8 |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | 600 | 160 |
| 7 | 210 | 56 |

Add the four partial products: 600 + 160 + 210 + 56 = 1,026.

Tens times tens make hundreds. 30 × 20 is 3 tens × 2 tens = 6 hundreds = 600. In the same way, 60 × 40 = 24 hundreds = 2,400.

**Try it.** 18 × 24. Split 18 into 10 + 8: 10 × 24 = 240 and 8 × 24 = 192. Then 240 + 192 = 432. This uses two partial products instead of four, which is fine as long as nothing is left out.

## Dividing with partial quotients

Division asks "how many groups?" Take out big, easy chunks first, then smaller ones, until what is left is less than the number you divide by.

945 ÷ 7:

- 7 × 100 = 700. 945 − 700 = 245 left.
- 7 × 30 = 210. 245 − 210 = 35 left.
- 7 × 5 = 35. 35 − 35 = 0 left.

Add the chunks: 100 + 30 + 5 = 135. As an area model, this is a rectangle 7 tall with parts of area 700, 210 and 35. The widths 100, 30 and 5 add to the quotient.

When the last step does not reach 0, the leftover is the **remainder**. 75 ÷ 4: 4 × 18 = 72, and 3 are left, so 75 ÷ 4 = 18 R 3. The remainder must be smaller than 4. If it is not, you can take out another group.

Big round numbers can be divided by thinking in units: 2,400 ÷ 6 is 24 hundreds ÷ 6 = 4 hundreds = 400.

**Try it.** 1,624 ÷ 8. 8 × 200 = 1,600, leaving 24, and 8 × 3 = 24. The quotient is 203. There are no tens, so write the 0: 203, not 23.

## Checking your work

- **Estimate first.** 39 × 52 is about 40 × 50 = 2,000. An answer like 20,280 is far too big.
- **Multiply back.** To check 75 ÷ 4 = 18 R 3, find 4 × 18 + 3 = 72 + 3 = 75.

**A common mistake.** In 38 × 27, some students multiply only 30 × 20 and 8 × 7 and get 600 + 56 = 656. That forgets 8 × 20 and 30 × 7. An area model with four boxes shows every part, so nothing gets lost. In division, a common slip is stopping with a remainder that is too big, such as 75 ÷ 4 = 17 R 7. Seven is more than 4, so one more group of 4 still fits.

## For grown-ups at home

Ask your child to estimate before they calculate, then compare. Let them choose their method: area models, partial products and partial quotients all show place value, and the standard method comes from them. Real numbers help: "There are 24 cans in a case. How many cans in 15 cases?" or "We have 150 crackers for 6 school lunches. How many crackers go in each lunch?"
