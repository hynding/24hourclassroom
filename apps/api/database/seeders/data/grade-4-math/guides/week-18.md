# Week 18 · Multiplying by a One-Digit Number

**Standard:** 4.NBT.B.5 Multiply up to four digits by one digit, and two digits by two digits
**Built from:** IM K–5 Math Grade 4, Unit 6, Section B, in our own words

## What you will learn

- Multiply a one-digit number by tens, hundreds and thousands.
- Split a bigger number by **place value** and draw an **area diagram**.
- Write and add **partial products** to multiply numbers with up to four digits.
- Estimate a product to check that an answer makes sense.

## Words to know

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Factor | A number you multiply. In 4 × 326, the factors are 4 and 326. |
| Product | The answer to a multiplication. |
| Expanded form | A number written as the sum of its place values: 326 = 300 + 20 + 6. |
| Area diagram | A rectangle whose side lengths are the factors and whose area is the product. |
| Partial product | The product of one part, such as 4 × 20 = 80 inside 4 × 326. |
| Estimate | A number close to the answer, found with rounded numbers. |

## Multiplying by tens, hundreds and thousands

Think about what each digit is worth.

- 6 × 40: 40 is 4 tens. 6 × 4 tens = 24 tens = **240**.
- 7 × 300: 300 is 3 hundreds. 7 × 3 hundreds = 21 hundreds = **2,100**.
- 5 × 6,000: 6 × 5 = 30, so 30 thousands = **30,000**.

Be careful when the basic fact already ends in 0. 8 × 50 = 40 tens = **400**, with two zeros.

## Area diagrams

Look at 4 × 326. Draw a rectangle with one side 4. Split the long side into 300, 20 and 6. Each part is a smaller rectangle that is easy to multiply.

- 4 × 300 = 1,200
- 4 × 20 = 80
- 4 × 6 = 24

The whole area is 1,200 + 80 + 24 = **1,304**. So 4 × 326 = 1,304.

The diagram does not need to be drawn to scale. It is a map that keeps your parts organised.

## Partial products in a column

You can write the same work without a drawing. Line up the parts by place value, then add.

**Try it.** 3 × 2,148.

| Part | Partial product |
|---|---|
| 3 × 8 | 24 |
| 3 × 40 | 120 |
| 3 × 100 | 300 |
| 3 × 2,000 | 6,000 |
| Total | 6,444 |

When a digit is 0, its partial product is 0. For 6 × 507: 6 × 500 = 3,000 and 6 × 7 = 42, so 6 × 507 = 3,042.

**Try it.** 7 × 68. 7 × 60 = 420 and 7 × 8 = 56. 420 + 56 = 476.

## Estimate first, then check

Round the bigger factor to a friendly number. For 6 × 712, think 6 × 700 = 4,200. The exact answer must be a little more than 4,200, and it is: 6 × 712 = 4,272. If you got 427 or 42,720, the estimate tells you something went wrong.

## Common mistakes

- **Multiplying only one part.** 4 × 326 is not 4 × 300 + 26. Every part gets multiplied by 4.
- **Losing place value.** 4 × 3 = 12, but 4 × 300 = 1,200. Say the digit's value out loud: "4 times 3 hundreds".
- **Forgetting to add.** The partial products are steps, not the answer. Add them all at the end.

## For grown-ups at home

Children may solve 4 × 326 in a way that looks different from how you learned it. Partial products and area diagrams show *why* multiplication works, and they lead straight to the standard method later on. Ask your child to explain one partial product: "Where did the 80 come from?" A good answer is "4 times 2 tens is 8 tens". Shopping is a handy practice ground: "4 tickets at $125 each, about how much?"

## Check yourself

1. 9 × 70 = ?
2. Use partial products: 5 × 418.
3. Draw or describe an area diagram for 6 × 253, and find the product.
4. Estimate 8 × 395. Is 316 a sensible answer?

*Answers:* (1) 630. (2) 2,000 + 50 + 40 = 2,090. (3) A rectangle with side 6 and the other side split into 200, 50 and 3; the parts are 1,200, 300 and 18, so 1,518. (4) 8 × 400 = 3,200, so 316 is far too small (the product is 3,160).
