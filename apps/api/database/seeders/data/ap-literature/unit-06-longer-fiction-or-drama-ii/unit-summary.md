# Unit 6 Summary · Longer Fiction or Drama II

**Exam weight:** longer fiction or drama is 15-18% of the multiple-choice section, and a play like *Hamlet* is ideal evidence for the Q3 literary argument essay.
**Text:** William Shakespeare, *Hamlet, Prince of Denmark* (written about 1600; First Quarto 1603, Second Quarto 1604-05, First Folio 1623), one act a week, weeks 17-21. Read it at [Project Gutenberg #1524](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1524) or, with line numbers and notes, at the [Folger Shakespeare](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/hamlet/).
**Quotations and line numbers:** every quotation in this unit follows Project Gutenberg's text (#1524); act.scene.line references are given as numbered in the Folger edition, because Gutenberg's text has none, and other editions differ, so in an essay cite act and scene and quote the words.

## The skills, as this unit used them

| Skill | What it meant in *Hamlet* |
|---|---|
| 1.E Choices and speech reveal complexity | Hamlet scorns "seems" and then chooses to play mad; vows "bloody" thoughts, not deeds; spares a praying king and kills an unseen one. Laertes, Claudius and Gertrude are read the same way. |
| 3.D Contrasts | The two kings ("Hyperion to a satyr"), the court's festivity and Hamlet's black, public verse and private soliloquy, seeming and being. |
| 4.D Reliability | No narrator, so reliability is about reporters (Ophelia, Gertrude), interpreters (Polonius), self-presenters (Hamlet to the spies) and sources (the Ghost). |
| 1.C Contrasting characters | Foils who are sons with dead fathers: Fortinbras, Pyrrhus, Laertes. Horatio against the hired friends Rosencrantz and Guildenstern. |
| 5.C Symbol | The Mousetrap, poison in the ear, the portraits, the recorder, the arras, Ophelia's flowers, Yorick's skull. |
| 3.B Sequence | Aside before the Mousetrap; Mousetrap, prayer, closet; the order of deaths in the duel. |
| 3.A Plot order | What is reported rather than staged (Ophelia's encounter, the sea fight, the drowning); what is told late (the forged commission, in 5.2); Hamlet's absence through Act IV. |
| 7.C, 7.D Commentary and evidence | Choosing moments from across five acts and explaining how each advances a line of reasoning. |

## Week by week

**Week 17, Act I.** The court scene sets Claudius's balanced public verse ("With mirth in funeral, and with dirge in marriage") against Hamlet's first soliloquy ("O that this too too solid flesh would melt"), with its unweeded garden, its god and satyr, and its shrinking "little month." Hamlet says he knows "not seems." The Ghost reveals murder, commands revenge and forbids harm to Gertrude. Hamlet chooses to "put an antic disposition on" and closes the act with a couplet that accepts and resents the task: "The time is out of joint:—O cursed spite, / That ever I was born to set it right!"

**Week 18, Act II.** A play of spies. Ophelia's report of Hamlet's silent visit is reliable as description and undecided as interpretation; Polonius turns it into "the very ecstasy of love." Hamlet performs in prose for Polonius and the schoolfellows ("Words, words, words"; "I am but mad north-north-west"). Foils gather: Fortinbras turned toward Poland, Pyrrhus who "Did nothing" and then struck, the Player weeping for Hecuba. Alone, Hamlet condemns himself, doubts that the Ghost is honest, and plans the Mousetrap.

**Week 19, Act III.** Claudius's aside confirms his guilt to the audience before the play. "To be, or not to be" generalises Hamlet's crisis into "us." The Mousetrap, a mirror and a trap, makes the King rise. The sequence that follows is the play's turning point: Claudius cannot repent while he keeps "My crown, mine own ambition, and my queen"; Hamlet spares him to damn him later; the prayer, we learn, was empty; minutes later Hamlet kills Polonius through the arras.

**Week 20, Act IV.** Consequences, with the hero offstage. Claudius orders Hamlet's death in England. Hamlet's last soliloquy, "How all occasions do inform against me" (in the Second Quarto only), ends with a vow of bloody *thoughts*. Ophelia goes mad and hands out flowers. Laertes storms in ("To hell, allegiance!") and is turned by Claudius. Hamlet's escape comes by letter. The unbated sword, Laertes's poison and the King's reserve cup are planned in full view. Gertrude's willow speech turns Ophelia's death into an elegy and leaves accident or suicide unresolved.

**Week 21, Act V and the whole play.** The graveyard makes death physical: Yorick's skull, Alexander "smelt so." Hamlet names himself "Hamlet the Dane." He tells of the forged commission and credits "a divinity that shapes our ends." "the readiness is all." In the duel every plot turns on its maker ("I am justly kill’d with mine own treachery"), the King's guilt is exposed, Hamlet kills him, asks Horatio to "tell my story" and dies: "the rest is silence." Fortinbras inherits.

## Conventions you must be able to use

- **Soliloquy:** a character alone speaking his thoughts; sincere by convention, though not always fair. "To be, or not to be" is spoken with Ophelia on stage and the King and Polonius hidden.
- **Aside:** a remark the audience hears and the other characters, by convention, do not. Claudius's in 3.1 and, in many editions, Hamlet's "A little more than kin, and less than kind."
- **Blank verse and prose:** verse for public and serious speech and for soliloquy; prose for informal talk, servants and gravediggers, letters, and Hamlet's "antic" performance. A shift between them is evidence.
- **Couplets:** rhymed pairs close scenes and speeches ("The play’s the thing / Wherein I’ll catch the conscience of the king"). The Mousetrap's old-fashioned couplets mark it as a play inside the play.
- **Play within the play and dumb show:** a mimed preview, then the spoken play; Claudius breaks at the spoken poisoning.

## Vocabulary

Soliloquy, aside, blank verse, couplet, foil, antic disposition, reported action, dramatic irony, reversal, symbol, memento mori, providence, unbated, Quarto, Folio, groundlings, arras, closet (a private room).

## Essay moves practised

- **Q2-style passage analysis on drama:** the first soliloquy, the Act II soliloquy, the willow speech, and Macbeth's soliloquy as an unseen passage. Track how imagery, syntax and structure convey a complex state of mind.
- **Paragraphs on sequence:** the Mousetrap, prayer and closet in order.
- **Q3 literary argument:** hesitation before a decisive act. Choose a beginning, a turning point and an end; write commentary that explains each moment's function and returns to the thesis.
- **Evidence banks:** delay (1.5, 2.2, 3.3, 4.4, 5.2); appearance and reality (1.2, 1.5, 3.1, 3.2, 3.4, 5.1); foils (Fortinbras, Pyrrhus, Laertes).

## Common traps

- Treating a soliloquy as objective truth, or "Frailty, thy name is woman!" as the play's view.
- Saying Hamlet suspects murder in 1.2, that the Ghost wants Gertrude punished, or that Gertrude sees the Ghost in 3.4.
- Thinking Hamlet spares Claudius out of mercy, or knew Polonius was behind the arras.
- Saying Hamlet reaches England, or that Claudius proposed the poison on the blade (Laertes did).
- Citing line numbers as universal, or treating "How all occasions" as being in every text.
- Summarising instead of commenting; quoting "To be, or not to be" when it is not the best evidence for the claim.

## What the unit exam looks like

Forty-five questions in about 75 minutes. Four passage sets: Claudius's first speech (1.2), the advice to the players (3.2), Claudius at prayer (3.3), and an unseen passage, Macbeth's soliloquy from *Macbeth* 1.7, read the way the AP exam reads a passage you have not studied. Then multiple choice, select-all, true/false and fill-in items on the whole play, two short answers on commentary and reliability, and an AP-style essay analysing Macbeth's deliberation, scored on the 6-point rubric (thesis 0-1, evidence and commentary 0-4, sophistication 0-1).
