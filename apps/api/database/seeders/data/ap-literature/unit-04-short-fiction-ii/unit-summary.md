# Unit 4 Summary · Short Fiction II

**Exam weight:** short fiction is 42-49% of the multiple-choice section, and Q2 (prose fiction analysis) is one of the three essays · **Weeks:** 11-13 · **Big Ideas:** Setting, Character, Narration, Literary Argument

## Week by week

**Week 11 · Nathaniel Hawthorne, "Young Goodman Brown" (1835).** A young Puritan leaves his wife, Faith, at sunset for an errand in the forest, meets a fellow-traveller with a serpent staff, and finds (or dreams he finds) his catechism teacher, his minister, his deacon and finally his wife at a witch-meeting. Key moves: the threshold at the meeting-house corner; the road that "closed immediately behind"; "an unseen multitude"; the clearing as a parody of a church service; "My Faith is gone!"; the narrator's hedges ("Some affirm," "Be it so if you will"); the return to an unchanged village that Brown can no longer trust; "his dying hour was gloom." Setting does the arguing: a faith that needs other people to look good cannot survive suspecting them.

**Week 12 · Charlotte Perkins Gilman, "The Yellow Wall-Paper" (1892).** A young mother on a rest cure prescribed by her physician husband, John, keeps a secret journal in a barred upstairs room whose yellow paper she comes to see as a cage with a creeping woman behind it. Key moves: the parenthesis about "dead paper"; praise that contains complaint ("careful and loving, and hardly lets me stir"); John's pet names and the third-person "Bless her little heart!"; the sentence cut off by "a stern, reproachful look"; the descriptions of the paper growing more violent; syntax breaking into exclamations; the ending in which she has "got out" and John faints across her path. Perspective and relationship are inseparable: we trust her account of John while doubting her account of the paper.

**Week 13 · Katherine Mansfield, "Miss Brill" (1920).** A solitary Englishwoman spends her Sunday in a French public garden, decides that everyone there is acting in a play and that she has a part, and then overhears a young couple call her "that stupid old thing" and laugh at her fur. Key moves: her self-corrections ("no, not sad, exactly"); the bench-sitters from "cupboards"; the ermine toque as a rehearsal of her humiliation; simile hardening into belief ("like a play" to "They were all on the stage"); the lovers as "hero and heroine"; the section break; the honey-cake she does not buy; "her room like a cupboard"; "something crying." Contrast reveals what she cannot see.

## Vocabulary to own

Setting and function of setting; moral landscape; threshold; frame; allegory; ambiguity; hedging; perspective; diction; syntax; journal (epistolary) form; unreliable narrator (and degrees of reliability); dramatic irony; rest cure; condescension; foil (by likeness and by difference); close third person; free indirect discourse; epiphany; pathos.

## The essay moves practised: Q2, prose fiction analysis

1. **Read the prompt's last clause first.** It names what to analyze ("the complex relationship," "the contrast between the two characters and its effect"). Your thesis answers that clause.
2. **Thesis with tension.** Name both sides of the complexity: love and control, joy and fragility, confidence and the setting's warnings. If no reader could disagree, it is summary.
3. **Line of reasoning.** Two to four stages that move through the passage (opening, turn, close) or through kinds of evidence (one character's speech, the other's narration, the setting).
4. **Evidence from both sides.** In relationship and contrast prompts, quote both characters, and the narrator's framing of them.
5. **Commentary that names and explains.** Technique, then effect, then link to the claim: "the slide into the third person turns her into a child being discussed in her own presence."
6. **Sophistication.** Hold an ambiguity (dream or real; sincere or self-pleasing sympathy), explain how the narration lets us inside and beyond a character at once, or set the passage in the larger arc of the story.
7. **Control.** Embed quotations in your own sentences; avoid "this quote shows"; avoid comma splices.

## Common traps

- Describing a setting instead of explaining its function.
- Treating Hawthorne's ending as settled ("it was just a dream," or "everyone is evil").
- Calling John a villain, or Gilman's narrator simply "crazy," and losing the complexity the prompt asks for.
- Reading free indirect thought as the narrator's verdict ("No doubt somebody would have noticed").
- Finding only the obvious foil and missing the foils by likeness.
- Listing devices in a thesis instead of making a claim.
- Quoting text that is not in the passage you were given.

## What the unit exam looks like

Forty-five items in about 75 minutes, plus a 40-minute essay. Four passage sets in Section I style: the ending of "Young Goodman Brown," the opening description of the room in "The Yellow Wall-Paper," the ending of "Miss Brill," and an **unseen** passage from Mansfield's "Her First Ball" (1921), in which a girl at her first dance is told by an older partner what her future holds. Then stand-alone items across the unit (point of view, setting, foils, thesis, evidence, integrating quotations), one short answer on contrasting characters, and a full Q2 essay on the unseen passage scored on the AP 6-point rubric: thesis (0-1), evidence and commentary (0-4), sophistication (0-1). Expect stems like "serves primarily to," "is best described as," "the contrast between X and Y chiefly." For the essay, annotate for the contrast, write a thesis with tension, and explain each piece of evidence.
