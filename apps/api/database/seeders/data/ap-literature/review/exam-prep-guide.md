# AP English Literature · Exam Prep Guide

**Exam:** Wednesday 5 May 2027 · **Format:** two sections, three hours, taken digitally in the College Board's Bluebook app · **Built from:** the College Board AP English Literature and Composition Course and Exam Description and the year's work in this course.

This guide is for weeks 33 and 34. Week 33 is for review with this guide; week 34 is the full practice exam, which follows the real exam's shape exactly. Week 35 is a buffer, and the exam falls in week 36.

## The format

| Section | What | Time | Weight | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I · Multiple choice | 55 questions in five passage sets, four options each | 1 hour | 45% | At least two sets on prose fiction (drama counts as prose here) and at least two on poetry. Every passage is unseen. No penalty for a wrong answer, so answer every question. |
| II · Free response | 3 essays | 2 hours | 55% | Q1 poetry analysis, Q2 prose fiction analysis, Q3 literary argument. Each essay is scored out of 6. The College Board suggests about 40 minutes for each. |

**Digital testing.** You read the passages and answer on screen in Bluebook, and you type your essays. Practise with the tools before May: the highlighter and annotation tool for marking shifts and key words, the option eliminator for crossing out wrong answers, and the clock (you can hide it, but check it at the end of each set). In Section I you can move back and forth between questions until time is called; once the section ends you cannot return to it. In Section II the three essays share the two hours and you can move among them, so you control the pacing. Typing changes how essays go wrong: it is easy to write long and say little. Plan before you type.

**What "unseen" means.** None of the passages in Section I or in Questions 1 and 2 will be texts you studied. The exam tests skills, not memory of books, which is why every unit in this course ended with an exam that included a passage you had not read. Question 3 is the only place where the works you studied count directly.

## How the multiple-choice section is weighted

The CED sorts Section I questions into seven skill categories. The ranges below are the share of the section each carries; they tell you where the points are.

| Category | Skills (course codes) | Share of Section I |
|---|---|---|
| Character | 1.A-1.E: what details reveal about a character, change, contrast, relationships, choices | 16-20% |
| Setting | 2.A-2.C: details of place and time, and their function | 3-6% |
| Plot and structure | 3.A-3.F: order of events, sequence, structure, contrast, significant events, conflict | 16-20% |
| Narrator or speaker | 4.A-4.D: who tells it, point of view, perspective in diction and syntax, reliability | 21-26% |
| Word choice, imagery and symbols | 5.A-5.D: literal and figurative meaning, word function, symbol, image | 10-13% |
| Comparison | 6.A-6.D: simile, metaphor, personification, allusion | 10-13% |
| Literary argument | 7.A-7.E: claims, theses, commentary, evidence, composition | 10-13% |

Narrator or speaker is the heaviest single category: roughly one question in four asks who is speaking, from what position, with what attitude. If you have time to sharpen only one habit before May, make it this one: after the first reading of any passage, say in one sentence who is speaking and how they feel about what they describe.

The literary argument questions in Section I look like the essay in miniature: which of these is the most defensible thesis, which detail best supports this claim, which sentence best explains how the evidence supports the claim. The rule for them is the rule for your essays: a defensible claim is arguable, specific and supportable from the text, and the best evidence fits every part of the claim, not just one word of it.

## The passage-set routine for Section I

Twelve minutes a set is the average (60 minutes for five sets); poetry sets are often shorter but harder, so bank time on prose.

1. **Read the headnote first.** The italic line above a passage gives the author, date and sometimes the situation. It is free context: a 19th-century novel, a speaker addressing a dead friend, a play's second act. Use it.
2. **Read the whole passage once, steadily, for gist (two to three minutes).** Do not stop to puzzle over a hard line. On a poem, read to the end of each sentence, not each line. Mark only shifts: a "but," a change of tense or speaker, a new stanza that turns, a move from description to reflection.
3. **Name the speaker and the attitude.** One sentence in your head: "An older man, rueful and ironic, remembers a boyhood infatuation." Most narrator, tone and thesis questions are already half answered.
4. **Answer in order, but skip and mark anything that stalls you.** A question you skip costs nothing until the end of the section; a question you stare at for three minutes costs you two other answers.
5. **Go back to the lines every time.** Line references in the stem are an instruction to reread those lines and the lines on either side. The answer is almost always in the context, not the quoted phrase alone.
6. **Eliminate before you choose.** Cross out options that are false to the text, too extreme ("bitter," "despairing," "condemns" when the tone is mixed), true but beside the point of the stem, or true of a different part of the passage. Then choose between what remains by asking which option accounts for the whole of the quoted phrase.
7. **Watch the stem's verbs.** "Primarily serves to" asks for function, not meaning. "Most nearly means" asks for the sense in context, which is often not the first dictionary meaning (in Hamlet's "the native hue of resolution," "native" means "natural," not "born in a place"). "Best described as" asks for the most accurate option, not merely an acceptable one.

**Three distractor patterns to recognise.** The literal trap reads a figure as fact (when Dickinson's speaker says Death "kindly stopped for me," the poem is not about a carriage ride). The extreme trap takes a real tone and turns it up (gentle irony becomes contempt). The half-right trap names a true detail that does not answer the question (the detail that shows a character's pride when the claim is about pride and what it costs). Every multiple-choice rationale in this course's quizzes names the trap a wrong option sets; reread those for the items you missed.

## Section II: the 6-point rubric, row by row

All three essays are scored on the same three rows. Learn them as a checklist, because graders read with them in hand.

**Row A · Thesis (0 or 1).** One point for a defensible thesis that responds to the prompt and makes an interpretive claim about the text. It can be more than one sentence and can sit anywhere, but put it in your first paragraph so the grader finds it. It earns nothing if it restates the prompt ("Dickinson uses figurative language to convey the speaker's attitude toward death"), only describes the text, or claims something the text will not support. A thesis that also previews a line of reasoning helps Row B, though Row A does not require it.

**Row B · Evidence and Commentary (0 to 4).** This is two thirds of the score.
- **4:** specific, well-chosen evidence throughout supports a line of reasoning, and commentary consistently explains how the evidence supports the thesis and how the author's choices create meaning.
- **3:** specific evidence and a line of reasoning, with commentary that explains most of the evidence; some links are thinner.
- **2:** some specific evidence, but commentary is largely summary or paraphrase, or explains the evidence only loosely.
- **1:** mostly general evidence, little or no commentary.
- **0:** no relevant evidence.
The jump from 2 to 3 is the one most students need: it comes from explaining *function* ("in 'the native hue of resolution / Is sicklied o'er with the pale cast of thought,' Hamlet makes thinking a sickness that drains the colour from a resolute face, so his own delay becomes a disease he can diagnose but not cure") rather than identifying a device ("Shakespeare uses a metaphor") or retelling content ("Hamlet thinks too much"). Name the device if it helps, but always say what it does.

**Row C · Sophistication (0 or 1).** One point for writing that shows a complex understanding, sustained across the essay, not in one sentence. The ways it is usually earned:
- explaining a **tension or complexity** in the text (a narrator who pities and judges a character at once, as in "Miss Brill"; a speaker who grieves and consoles in the same breath, as in "Spring and Fall");
- explaining how **form enacts meaning** (a sonnet whose closing couplet overturns its first twelve lines; a story that holds back its irony until the last sentence);
- weighing **alternative interpretations** and arguing for one;
- situating the text in a **broader context**, briefly and relevantly, without leaving the text behind;
- a **vivid, controlled style** that persuades throughout.
What does not earn it: a sweeping opening about "the human condition," name-dropping terms ("sprung rhythm," "free indirect discourse") without using them, or a clever last line tacked onto an otherwise ordinary essay. The point can only be earned on top of solid Row B work; essays that score 2 or lower in Row B rarely earn Row C.

## The three essays

**Q1 · Poetry analysis.** A poem (sometimes two, or a long excerpt) is printed with a prompt that names a focus: the speaker's complex attitude toward something, a relationship, an experience. Read the poem twice before you plan: once for the situation, once for shifts. Your thesis answers the prompt's specific focus (say, "the speaker's complex attitude toward the passing of time"), not just "the poem's meaning." Build your body paragraphs on ideas, not on devices: "the second stanza turns the speaker's grief from the world to the self" is a paragraph; "imagery" is not. Use short quotations, integrated into your sentences, with line numbers if you like. The best essays spend at least one paragraph on the turn or the ending.

**Q2 · Prose fiction analysis.** A passage from a novel or story, usually 500-800 words, with a headnote and a prompt naming a focus, often a character's complex relationship, perception or situation. Watch point of view: who sees and who tells (in "Araby" the grown narrator understands what the boy could not, and the gap between them is the story). Track how the passage moves, from dialogue to description to narratorial generalization, and treat that movement as evidence. Speech and action characterize; so do the narrator's word choices.

**Q3 · Literary argument.** A prompt names a literary concept (a character's divided loyalties, a setting that shapes a character, an act of deception, a role a character must play) and asks you to choose a work of fiction or drama and analyze how that concept contributes to an interpretation of the work as a whole. The prompt lists suggested titles, but you may use any work of comparable literary merit. The trap is plot summary: retelling the story in order, however accurately, caps you at 2 in Row B. Choose a work you know in detail, state a thesis that links the concept to the work's meaning, and organise by stages of your argument, using specific scenes, actions and remembered phrases as evidence.

**Timing each essay.** Plan for six to eight minutes (thesis, three body points, the evidence for each), write for about thirty, and keep two or three for rereading the thesis and fixing errors that obscure meaning. If time runs short on the last essay, write the thesis and one fully developed paragraph rather than three thin ones.

Go into the exam with two or three works you could write on for forty minutes without notes, each with five or six specific moments (scenes, actions, short phrases you remember) that you can bend toward almost any concept. A short story can carry a Q3 essay, but a longer work gives you more evidence to choose from. Whatever you choose, practise writing the thesis sentence for three or four different concepts with the same work; the exam rewards students who can adapt a well-known work to a new question.

## Test day

- **Before.** Bluebook installed and the device charged; the exam app checked in advance as the College Board instructs. Eat breakfast. Do not learn anything new.
- **Section I.** About twelve minutes a set. Headnote, then gist reading, then speaker and attitude, then questions. Skip and mark; return. With two minutes left, answer every blank question: there is no penalty for a wrong answer.
- **Section II.** Read all three prompts first (two minutes). Start with the essay you feel surest of. Plan each before typing. Put the thesis in the first paragraph. Quote short, explain long. Watch the clock at 40 and 80 minutes.
- **If a passage baffles you.** Start with the questions that cite specific lines: they make you reread small pieces, and the passage usually comes into focus. For an essay, a modest, defensible thesis beats a grand one you cannot support.

## Traps that cost points

- Retelling instead of analyzing, in Q2 and especially Q3.
- A thesis that repeats the prompt's words without a claim.
- Device-spotting: naming a metaphor without saying what it does.
- Reading figurative language literally, or literal language as symbol, in Section I.
- Choosing an answer because it is true of the passage rather than because it answers the stem.
- Spending fifteen minutes on one Section I set and rushing the last one.
