# Unit 5 Summary · Poetry II

**Exam weight:** poetry is 36–45% of the multiple-choice section, and Q1 (poetry analysis) is one of the three essays · **Weeks:** 14–16 · **Unit skills:** 3.C, 5.A, 5.B, 5.D, 6.B, 6.C, 6.D, 7.B, 7.C, 7.D, 7.E

## The skills, as this unit uses them

| Skill | What it means in Poetry II |
|---|---|
| 3.C Structure | Sonnet form: octave and sestet, quatrains and couplet, and where the volta falls; stanza shape (Herbert's narrowing stanzas, Keats's eleven-line stanzas). |
| 5.A Literal and figurative | Read the words literally first, then figuratively, and keep both: "mocked", "light", "Talent", "rest", "the silent land". |
| 5.B Word function | A word's job in context: "fondly" (foolishly), "sordid boon", "surmised", "cultured hell". |
| 5.D Imagery | What a pattern of images does: ripeness that tips into ending, a sleeping city, a lifetime seen from a carriage. |
| 6.B Metaphor | Extended metaphor and conceit: Donne's compass and gold, Frost's wall, Herbert's pulley. Map the details. |
| 6.C Personification | The role and temperament a thing is given: Autumn the drowsy harvester, Death the courteous driver, London asleep. |
| 6.D Allusion | What the reference imports and how the poem bends it: Ozymandias, Proteus and Triton, the parable of the talents. |
| 7.B-7.E The essay | A defensible Q1 thesis with a line of reasoning; commentary that explains; evidence from across the poem; clear control. |

## Week 14 · The sonnet and allusion

**Meter.** Iambic pentameter is five iambs (da-DUM) per line: "I MET | a TRAV | 'ller FROM | an AN | tique LAND." Variations spotlight words: Wordsworth's "GET-ting and SPEND-ing" opens on a trochee.

**Two patterns.** Petrarchan: octave ABBAABBA + sestet (CDECDE, CDCDCD), problem then response, turn at line 9. Shakespearean: ABAB CDCD EFEF GG, three developments and a couplet that resolves or twists.

**The texts and their key moves.**

- **Shelley, "Ozymandias" (1818).** A hybrid rhyme scheme (ABABACDCEDEFEF). A chain of voices (speaker, traveller, sculptor, king) puts the king's boast at several removes. "King of kings" claims divine supremacy; "Look on my works, ye Mighty, and despair!" now means the opposite of what the king intended; "Nothing beside remains" answers him in three words.
- **Wordsworth, "The world is too much with us" (1807).** Petrarchan. Commerce has cost us our "powers" of feeling ("a sordid boon"). The volta comes mid-line 9 ("It moves us not. Great God! I'd rather be"); the allusions to Proteus and Triton measure what we've lost: a sea alive with meaning.
- **Milton, "When I consider how my light is spent" (1673).** Petrarchan. "Light" is sight, life and inspiration; the parable of the talents (Matthew 25) turns blindness into fear of judgement. Patience interrupts in line 8, before the octave ends: "They also serve who only stand and wait."
- **McKay, "America" (1921).** Shakespearean. "Bread of bitterness", "tiger's tooth" and "cultured hell" against "vigor" and "strength erect"; the "Yet" arrives a line early, and the rebel simile makes love and defiance one posture; the couplet's treasures "sinking in the sand" recall Shelley's desert.

## Week 15 · Imagery and personification

- **Keats, "To Autumn" (1820).** Stanza 1: touch and taste, swelling ripeness, bees who wrongly "think warm days will never cease". Stanza 2: Autumn as a person in harvest roles (on the granary floor, asleep in a "half-reap'd furrow", gleaner, cider-maker), all at rest, time slowed to "the last oozings hours by hours". Stanza 3: sound, the *ubi sunt* question set aside ("thou hast thy music too"), gnats that "mourn", clouds that "bloom the soft-dying day", swallows gathering to leave.
- **Dickinson, "Because I could not stop for Death" (1890 text).** Death as a courteous gentleman caller; Immortality as fellow passenger; the school, the "gazing grain" and the setting sun as the stages of a life passed like scenery; the grave as "A swelling of the ground"; a speaker narrating centuries later who only "surmised" the destination. Common (hymn) meter keeps it calm. The 1890 editors titled it "The Chariot", regularised its punctuation and omitted one stanza.
- **Wordsworth, "Composed upon Westminster Bridge" (1807).** Petrarchan. The city wears the morning "like a garment" (borrowed, temporary); buildings "Open unto the fields" in "smokeless air"; the volta ranks London above "valley, rock, or hill"; the city ends as one sleeping body, its "mighty heart... lying still".

**Personification vs apostrophe.** Personification gives a human trait; apostrophe speaks to the absent or non-human. Keats does both ("thou"); Dickinson personifies only ("He"); Donne's "Death, be not proud" does both.

## Week 16 · Extended metaphor and the poetry essay

- **Donne, "A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning" (1633).** Part quietly, like good men dying; no "tear-floods" or "sigh-tempests"; the lovers as priests before the "laity"; their parting as the harmless "trepidation of the spheres" against "sublunary" love that needs bodies. Two figures for one separation: gold beaten "to airy thinness" (stretched, not broken) and the compass (joined legs; the fixed foot leans after the moving one; "Thy firmness makes my circle just, / And makes me end where I begun").
- **Frost, "Mending Wall" (1914).** Blank verse. The wall becomes any barrier kept by custom: "What I was walling in or walling out". The neighbour, "like an old-stone savage armed", "moves in darkness" and repeats his father's proverb; but the speaker starts the mending each spring, and the neighbour gets the last word.
- **Herbert, "The Pulley" (1633).** God pours blessings from a glass and keeps back "Rest". The pun (repose / remainder) carries the paradox: we keep "the rest" with "repining restlessness". The pulley, named only in the title, is weariness pulling us down so we may be tossed "to my breast". Stanzas narrow at each close ("Contract into a span").

## The essay moves practised

1. **Read the prompt's key phrase** and answer it ("a complex understanding of human restlessness", "the speaker's complex attitude toward America").
2. **Find the movement**: the volta, a shift in address or tone, how the ending differs from the opening.
3. **Thesis = claim + how**: *Through [technique], [technique] and [technique], [poet] presents [subject] as [complex idea].* Order the techniques as your paragraphs.
4. **Embed short quotations** and spend more words explaining than quoting. Explain function: what the image, figure or turn *does* for the claim.
5. **Reach for Row C** through a real tension: love and defiance in McKay, beauty and fragility in Wordsworth, mercy and affliction in Herbert, a boast that is itself a monument in Sonnet 55.

## Common traps

- Naming a sonnet type without lettering the rhymes; assuming the volta is always at line 9.
- Reading old words in modern senses ("fondly", "post", "fronts", "sublunary").
- Treating an allusion or image as decoration ("Milton alludes to the Bible") instead of explaining its function.
- Calling every vivid description personification; check for a human trait.
- Merging two metaphors (Donne's gold and compass) into one.
- Choosing a side the poem refuses to choose (the speaker in "Mending Wall"; love or hate in "America").
- A thesis that lists devices: "uses imagery, metaphor and diction to convey…" earns nothing.

## What the unit exam looks like

Forty-four questions in about 75 minutes. Five passage sets: three on **unseen** poems (Keats, "On First Looking into Chapman's Homer"; Donne, "Death, be not proud"; Christina Rossetti, "Remember") and two on unit poems ("To Autumn" stanza 3; the first four stanzas of "A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning"). Then terminology and form items (the volta, scansion, Petrarchan or Shakespearean), two short answers (Frost's wall, Dickinson's house) and a full Q1 essay on an unseen Shakespeare sonnet, scored on the 6-point rubric (thesis 0-1, evidence and commentary 0-4, sophistication 0-1). Expect to scan a line, find a turn, explain an allusion's function, and sort literal from figurative language.
