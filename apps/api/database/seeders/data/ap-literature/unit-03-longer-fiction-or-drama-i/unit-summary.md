# Unit 3 Summary · Longer Fiction or Drama I

**Exam weight:** longer fiction or drama is 15-18% of the multiple-choice section, and it is the natural home of the Q3 literary argument essay · **Text:** Mary Shelley, *Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus* (1818 first edition, cited by volume and chapter; 1831 chapter numbers in brackets)
**Skills in focus:** 3.F conflict · 7.B thesis · 1.B character change · 3.E significant events · 7.C commentary · 7.D evidence · 7.E composition; with 1.A, 2.A and 7.A in support

## The skills, as this unit uses them

- **3.F Function of conflict.** Name the conflict, then say what it does. Walton against the ice and against his own loneliness; Victor's ambition against his health, family and conscience; creator against creation; Victor's promise against his duty to humankind in the Orkneys.
- **1.B Change or sameness.** Victor's feelings intensify (horror, remorse, despair) while his conduct (flight, silence) stays the same, until the one change of Volume III: he weighs consequences before he acts. The creature claims a change ("I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend") and ends in self-condemnation.
- **3.E Significant events.** Test each event by what it sets in motion: the abandonment, William's murder, Justine's trial, the meeting at Montanvert, the cottage rejection, the destruction of the female, the wedding night, Walton's turning back.
- **7.B Thesis.** Defensible, interpretive, arguable, and previewing a line of reasoning. Not a plot fact, not a topic, not a matter of taste.
- **7.C and 7.D Commentary and evidence.** Evidence must be relevant to the claim, not merely to the topic, and sufficient across the text. Commentary explains how specific words and details support the claim and how the claim advances the thesis.
- **7.E Composition.** Arguing topic sentences, transitions that state logic (escalation, concession, cause), active sentences with clear subjects, precise verbs, and a conclusion that widens.

## The novel, week by week

**Week 7 · Letters I-IV; Vol. I, Ch. I-IV [1-5].** Walton, sailing north, confesses "I have no friend, Margaret." The ship is trapped in ice; a giant figure crosses on a dog-sledge; Victor is rescued and begins his story as a warning. His childhood with Elizabeth (in 1818 his cousin) and Clerval; Agrippa, which his father calls "sad trash"; the lightning-blasted oak; his mother's death just before Ingolstadt; Krempe's scorn and Waldman's lecture on the moderns who "have indeed performed miracles"; nearly two years of secret labour; the "dreary night of November"; "Beautiful!—Great God!"; flight, Clerval's arrival, fever. *Key moves:* the frame as warning and mirror; conflict located first inside the creator; the night of animation as judgment before action.

**Week 8 · Vol. I, Ch. V-VII; Vol. II, Ch. I-II [6-10].** William strangled, the miniature missing; the storm near Geneva and the lightning that "discovered" the creature; Victor's silence; Justine condemned on the evidence of the miniature, her false confession, her execution; "The tortures of the accused did not equal mine"; despair at Belrive; Chamounix and Montanvert; the creature's speech: "I ought to be thy Adam; but I am rather the fallen angel." *Key moves:* dramatic irony at the trial; Victor's sameness as cause; the meeting as turning point, when the creature speaks for himself.

**Week 9 · Vol. II, Ch. III-IX [11-17].** The creature's first sensations, fire, the shepherd and village; the hovel and the De Laceys (the blind father, Felix, Agatha, then Safie); learning language through Safie's lessons from Volney; the history of the family's exile; the three books (*Paradise Lost*, Plutarch, *Werter*); Victor's journal; the interview with old De Lacey and Felix's attack; "Cursed, cursed creator!"; the cottage burned; the girl saved and the gunshot; William strangled and the miniature planted on Justine; "I am malicious because I am miserable"; the demand for a companion. *Key moves:* relevant versus on-topic evidence; commentary that explains; holding sympathy and judgment together.

**Week 10 · Vol. III, Ch. I-VII [18-24].** England with Clerval; the Orkney hut; Victor's reasoning ("ten thousand times more malignant," "a race of devils"); the female torn apart; "I shall be with you on your wedding-night"; Clerval strangled on the Irish shore, Mr. Kirwin, fever and acquittal; Elizabeth's letter, the wedding, the inn at Evian, Elizabeth strangled; the father's death; the pursuit north; Walton's crew; Victor's death and divided advice ("Seek happiness in tranquillity, and avoid ambition"); the creature's farewell and the ice-raft, "lost in darkness and distance." *Key moves:* the full Q3 essay, planned and controlled.

## Vocabulary to own

Frame narrative · epistolary · nested narrative · retrospective narrator · internal and external conflict · foil · static and dynamic character · turning point · dramatic irony · the sublime · allusion (Prometheus, *Paradise Lost*) · defensible claim · line of reasoning · relevant and sufficient evidence · commentary · concession · topic sentence · transition · sophistication.

## Essay moves practised

1. **Thesis (Row A).** Weak: "Victor creates a creature and regrets it." Strong: "Shelley locates the horror of creation not in the act of making but in the refusal of responsibility for what is made." Make it arguable and let it name the steps.
2. **Claim, evidence, commentary (Row B).** One claim per paragraph; short, embedded evidence; commentary longer than quotation, attending to words ("catastrophe," "wantonly," "thou," "emblem") and linking to the thesis.
3. **Change and function.** State the change precisely, show both ends, explain what it reveals or causes.
4. **Events and function.** Ask what the novel would lose without the event.
5. **Prose analysis (Q2).** Practised on the night of animation (week 7) and the creature's reading of Milton (week 9).
6. **Literary argument (Q3).** Practised on ambition and isolation (week 10) and on response to rejection (unit exam). Plan for ten minutes; write arguing topic sentences; use transitions of logic; conclude by widening.
7. **Sophistication (Row C).** Sustained complexity: the maker and creature as doubles, rivals in suffering; sympathy without excuse; the frame's answer in Walton's choice.

## Common traps

- Calling the creature "Frankenstein," or importing the film (castle, lightning machine, a named assistant).
- Treating the novel as opening with Victor rather than with Walton, or forgetting that every part is first-person.
- Mistaking intensified feeling for change in Victor.
- Settling the creature too early as innocent or as monstrous; the novel keeps both readings alive.
- Quoting a character's claim ("misery made me a fiend") as if it were proof of that claim.
- Summarizing plot in a Q3 essay; graders know the book.
- Edition confusion: in 1818 Elizabeth is Victor's cousin, and chapter numbers restart each volume; 1831 numbers 1-24 and differs in wording here and there.

## What the unit exam looks like

Forty-five questions in about 75 minutes. Four passage sets in AP style: Walton's first two letters; Waldman's lecture; Victor in the Orkneys; and an **unseen passage**, the opening of Nathaniel Hawthorne's "The Birthmark" (1843), about another man of science whose love of science rivals his love for his wife. Expect stems such as "The narrator's attitude toward... is best described as," "In context, the phrase... primarily serves to," and "The conflict in the passage is best described as." Then questions across the whole novel (structure, sequence, character, allusion, setting, commentary and composition), two short answers (Victor's change, the frame) and one Q3 essay on a character's response to rejection, scored on the 6-point rubric: thesis 0-1, evidence and commentary 0-4, sophistication 0-1.
