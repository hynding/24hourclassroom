# Unit 7 Summary · Short Fiction III

**Exam weight:** short fiction is 42–49% of the multiple-choice section · **Weeks:** 22–24 · **CED skills in focus:** 4.D, 5.D, 1.B, 1.D, 5.C, 2.C, with 2.B, 3.A, 3.B, 6.A, 6.C, 7.B, 7.C and 7.D in support
**Read:** Poe, "The Tell-Tale Heart" (1843) · Melville, "Bartleby, the Scrivener" (1853) · Chesnutt, "The Wife of His Youth" (1898)

## The big idea

Unit 7 is the sophistication unit. All three stories ask you to hold two readings at once: a narrator who argues one thing and proves another (Poe); a narrator who feels deeply and acts weakly (Melville); a man whose prejudices and integrity are equally real (Chesnutt). Row C of the essay rubric rewards exactly this: complexity, tension, alternative interpretations, and context handled accurately.

## The skills, as this unit uses them

| Skill | What it means here |
|---|---|
| 4.D Reliability | Say *which* layer you doubt (facts, interpretation, judgment), show the textual cause, and explain the effect. Poe's narrator reports accurately and interprets disastrously; Melville's narrator builds a consoling meaning on a "vague report." |
| 5.D Imagery | Images are where a narrator gives himself away: the vulture eye, the single ray, the muffled heartbeat; Bartleby's "shivering winding sheet." |
| 1.B Change and constancy | Bartleby never changes, so he measures the narrator's changes. Ryder changes completely, in public. Ask what the change, or its absence, *does*. |
| 1.D Relationships | Layers, not labels: employer, benefactor, jailer, brother "of Adam," mourner. Husband, stranger, "wife of my youth." |
| 5.C Symbol | Trace a symbol's origin, development and turn: "blue veins" from gibe to badge to emptied test; the daguerreotype and the mirror. |
| 2.C Character and setting | What the setting gives, what it demands, and how the character answers when it is challenged: Wall Street's walls; Groveland and the remembered plantation. |

## Week 22 · "The Tell-Tale Heart"

- **Frame:** a first-person speaker, after the events, defending his sanity to a "you" who has already judged him ("why *will* you say that I am mad?").
- **The argument that defeats itself:** "I heard many things in hell. How, then, am I mad?" His evidence is the symptom.
- **Motive removed:** "Object there was none. Passion there was none." Only the eye, "a pale blue eye, with a film over it," like a vulture's.
- **Structure:** seven midnights with the eye closed; the eighth night it opens; the murder; the body "between the scantlings"; four o'clock, three officers; the chair over the spot; confession.
- **Key images and figures:** the single ray on "the damned spot" (allusion to *Macbeth*); the heartbeat "such as a watch makes when enveloped in cotton" (repeated); the drum and the soldier; Death personified as stalking.
- **The ending's ambiguity:** the officers "chatted pleasantly, and smiled." Guilt, his own pulse, or the supernatural? Weigh the readings; do not choose one as fact.

## Week 23 · "Bartleby, the Scrivener"

- **Narrator:** a "rather elderly" Wall Street lawyer who believes "the easiest way of life is the best." Office: Turkey, Nippers, Ginger Nut, and a green folding screen.
- **The formula:** "I would prefer not to," delivered "in a singularly mild, firm voice." A refusal with no handle: no defiance to punish, no reason to answer.
- **The narrator's arc:** consternation; the Sunday discovery ("For the first time in my life a feeling of overpowering stinging melancholy"; "sons of Adam"); Providence and resolve; embarrassment before colleagues; moving offices; the offer of his home; the Tombs; "With kings and counselors."
- **Walls:** the subtitle "A Story of Wall-Street"; "dead-wall reveries"; the screen; death at the foot of a prison wall.
- **The last explanation:** the Dead Letter Office rumour, "Dead letters! does it not sound like dead men?" and "Ah Bartleby! Ah humanity!" Recognition, or retreat into abstraction? Both have evidence.

## Week 24 · "The Wife of His Youth"

- **Setting:** Groveland, a Northern city; the Blue Vein Society, light-skinned and exclusive, named by "some envious outsider."
- **Ryder's creed:** "I have no race prejudice ... but"; "ground between the upper and the nether millstone"; Lincoln's "With malice towards none" turned to exclusion; "Self-preservation is the first law of nature."
- **The contrast:** Tennyson's "divinely tall" and "divinely fair" ladies against Liza Jane, "not five feet tall," old, very dark, "a bit of the old plantation life."
- **Symbols:** blue veins (and the echo in her "blue" gums), the book of Tennyson, the daguerreotype of Sam Taylor, the mirror.
- **History to state accurately:** colorism within the Black community after emancipation; slave marriages without legal standing; decades-long searches for separated family.
- **Turn:** the hypothetical at the ball; Mrs. Dixon's "He should have acknowledged her"; "Permit me to introduce to you the wife of my youth."

## Vocabulary to own

Unreliable (fallible vs untrustworthy) narrator · frame of narration · dramatic irony · Gothic · unity of effect · static and dynamic character · foil · passive resistance · enigma · symbol · juxtaposition · colorism · the color line · dialect writing · allusion (*Macbeth*, Job, Petra, Marius, Lincoln, Exodus, Tennyson, *Hamlet*, Malachi).

## Essay moves practised

1. **Report vs inference.** Separate what a narrator tells you happened from what he concludes ("chatted pleasantly" vs "they knew!").
2. **Function over label.** Not "Bartleby is static" but what his stasis measures.
3. **Trace a symbol through time.** Origin, development, turn, effect.
4. **Two readings, weighed.** State both, cite evidence for each, explain what the ambiguity accomplishes.
5. **Context with precision.** One accurate historical sentence beats three vague ones; never invent facts the text does not give (Poe's narrator's relation to the old man; Bartleby's diagnosis).

## Common traps

- Calling a narrator "unreliable" without saying what you doubt and why.
- Treating the Dead Letter Office rumour or the officers' "mockery" as fact.
- Diagnosing characters instead of analysing language.
- Reading the Blue Veins as white, or Ryder as a simple hypocrite.
- Mocking or imitating Liza Jane's dialect; quote it exactly or paraphrase respectfully.
- Plot summary in place of commentary.

## The unit exam

44 items, about 80 minutes. Four passage sets: an **unseen** passage from Chesnutt's "The Sheriff's Children" (1889) on setting and narrative attitude; the eighth night in "The Tell-Tale Heart"; the Sunday discovery in "Bartleby"; Liza Jane's arrival in "The Wife of His Youth." Then items across the unit, two short answers (one on a passage, one on ambiguity), and a literary-argument essay on a character confronting a past or self they would rather not acknowledge, scored on the 6-point rubric (thesis 0-1, evidence and commentary 0-4, sophistication 0-1).
