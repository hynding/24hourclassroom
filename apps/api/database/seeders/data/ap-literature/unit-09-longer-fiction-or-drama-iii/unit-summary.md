# Unit 9 Summary · Longer Fiction or Drama III

**Weeks 28-32** · **Texts:** F. Scott Fitzgerald, *The Great Gatsby* (1925); Henrik Ibsen, *A Doll's House* (1879), translated by William Archer · **Exam weight:** longer fiction or drama is 15-18% of Section I, and these two works are among your strongest choices for Q3.

## The skills, as this unit uses them

| Skill | What it meant in this unit |
|---|---|
| **4.C** Details, diction and syntax reveal a narrator's perspective | Nick's self-corrections ("after boasting this way of my tolerance ... it has a limit"), his divided verdicts ("unaffected scorn" / "something gorgeous"), his modal verbs ("might have," "certainly") and his final "we." |
| **1.E** Choices and speech reveal complexity | Daisy's "beautiful little fool," Gatsby's "San Francisco," Nora's "something in reserve," her refusal to use Dr. Rank's love, her withdrawal from her children. |
| **3.E** The function of significant events | Gatsby's own legend (chapter 4), Jordan's Louisville story, the reunion (chapter 5), the history of James Gatz moved forward to chapter 6. |
| **3.F** The function of conflict | Tom against Gatsby at the Plaza; Daisy against herself; old money against new; Nora against Krogstad, against the law, against herself, and finally against Helmer's idea of what a wife is. |
| **1.B** The function of change or no change | Nick and Nora change; Gatsby, Tom, Daisy and Helmer do not. Krogstad, a minor character, does. |
| **7.B / 7.D** Thesis and evidence for the literary argument | A thesis that is defensible, specific and reaches the work as a whole; evidence that is relevant and sufficient, drawn from beginning, middle and end. |

## Week by week

**Week 28 · Gatsby chapters 1-3.** Nick introduces himself as tolerant and admits in the next breath that his tolerance "has a limit." He exempts Gatsby from his scorn and calls him "an extraordinary gift for hope." Daisy performs her disillusion ("Sophisticated—God, I'm sophisticated!") while revealing real grievance; Tom shows himself in his acts (the racist book, the broken nose); Jordan lies about a borrowed car. The valley of ashes and Eckleburg's eyes set a moral landscape. Gatsby's smile is a mirror, then "an elegant young roughneck."

**Week 29 · Gatsby chapters 4-6.** Gatsby's "God's truth" collapses on "San Francisco," then is half rescued by a medal and a photograph. Jordan reveals 1917, the pearls, the letter, and the house bought to be across the bay. At the reunion the green light becomes "again a green light on a dock," and Daisy "tumbled short of his dreams ... because of the colossal vitality of his illusion." Nick moves James Gatz's history forward: the "Platonic conception of himself," faithful "to the end." Gatsby: "Can't repeat the past? Why of course you can!"

**Week 30 · Gatsby chapters 7-9.** The hottest day; "Her voice is full of money"; the Plaza, where Daisy says "I did love him once—but I loved you too" and Tom exposes the drugstores; Myrtle's death under the car Daisy was driving; Gatsby's vigil; Wilson's revenge; the empty funeral; Jordan's "bad driver"; "careless people"; "So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past."

**Week 31 · A Doll's House Acts I-II.** The "lark" and the macaroons; Nora's secret loan, told to Mrs. Linden with pride and calculation ("something in reserve"); Krogstad's threat and the forged date; Helmer's speech on lying mothers, which Nora hears as a verdict on herself; the stripped Christmas tree; Nora asking Anna whether children forget a mother who "went quite away"; Rank's confession and Nora's refusal; the letter in the box; the tarantella; the hope of "the miracle."

**Week 32 · Act III and Q3.** Mrs. Linden lets the letter stay. Helmer rages, then forgives, imagining a wife "at once his wife and his child." Nora changes her dress, sits him down and names the doll-house: "I lived by performing tricks for you," "doll-wife ... doll-child," "My duties towards myself," "before all else I am a human being ... or at least that I should try to become one." She stopped loving him "when the miracle did not happen." She returns the ring and goes; Helmer hopes for "the miracle of miracles." Then the five-step Q3 plan and an evidence map of the year's works.

## Vocabulary to own

Retrospective narration · participant narrator · qualification · perspective · performance · old and new money · significant event · revelation · turning point · delayed exposition · achronological order · foil · mock-heroic · allusion · external, internal and ideological conflict · climax · dénouement · static and dynamic character · stage direction · dramatic irony · confidante · well-made play · problem play · defensible claim · line of reasoning · relevant and sufficient evidence · commentary · the work as a whole.

## Essay moves practised

- **Prose analysis (Q2) on a narrator's opening.** Track self-correction and qualification as evidence of an attitude (week 28's Nick passage).
- **Analytical paragraph on an event's function.** Claim about what the event changes, two pieces of evidence, commentary on the specific language, a link to a later event (week 29's reunion).
- **Literary-argument paragraph on change and constancy.** Gatsby's vigil as fidelity and doom (week 30).
- **Drama analysis.** Use stage directions and broken-off lines as evidence (week 31).
- **Q3 from prompt to plan.** Choose the work, answer in one sentence about meaning, three steps, two moments per step, an ending that reaches the whole work (week 32).

## Common traps

- Treating Nick as objective because he says he reserves judgment.
- Calling Daisy simply shallow, Helmer simply cruel, or Gatsby simply a liar. Every major character here is a mixture, and the AP exam rewards seeing the mixture.
- Retelling events instead of explaining what they do (3.E) or what a conflict produces (3.F).
- Saying a character "doesn't change" without saying why that matters.
- Ignoring stage directions when writing about a play.
- In Q3: picking a half-remembered work, writing a thesis about plot, and summarising in order.

## What the unit exam looks like

43 questions, about 90 minutes. Four passage sets in Section I style: the valley of ashes (*Gatsby*, chapter 2); Helmer on lying mothers (*A Doll's House*, Act I); an **unseen** passage from the opening of Edith Wharton's *The House of Mirth* (1905), in which Selden appraises Lily Bart; and Gatsby's Louisville courtship (chapter 8). Then standalone questions on both works and on Q3 theses, evidence and commentary; two short answers; a Q2-style essay on the Wharton passage; and a Q3 essay on money and property, with suggested works from the course. For the unseen passage, apply the unit's habits: watch the point of view (third-person limited), the diction of value ("cost a great deal to make"), the metaphor that turns on itself (glaze and clay), and the closing question.
