# Unit 2 Summary · Poetry I

**Weeks:** 4-6 · **Exam weight:** poetry is 36-45% of the multiple-choice section, and Q1 of the free-response section is always a poetry-analysis essay · **Big Ideas:** Character, Structure, Figurative language, Literary argumentation

## The skills, as this unit used them

| Skill | What it means in a poem |
|---|---|
| **1.A** Character | Who is the speaker (or the person portrayed), what is their perspective, and what do they want? Prove it with specific words. |
| **5.B** Words and phrases | Explain why this word: denotation, connotation, and what it does for the poem ("debt", "charter'd", "Bare."). |
| **3.C** Structure | Explain how the arrangement (stanzas, refrains, questions and answers, quatrains and couplet, the order of images) makes meaning. |
| **3.D** Contrast | Name both sides of an opposition (Lamb and Tyger, mask and heart, summer and the beloved) and say what holding them together reveals. |
| **6.A** Simile | An explicit comparison (like, as, than): name tenor and vehicle, the qualities carried over, and the function. |
| **6.B** Metaphor | An identification (stated, implied or extended): the same three steps, plus how an extended metaphor develops. |
| **7.A** Claim and evidence | A paragraph with an arguable claim, short embedded quotations, and commentary that explains how each quotation proves the claim. |

Review skills from Unit 1 that kept returning: **4.A** (identify the speaker), **4.B** (point of view: a collective "we" or "I"), **3.B** (why an event is placed where it is, as in the last line of "Richard Cory").

## Week by week

**Week 4 · Speakers and masks.** Dunbar's "We Wear the Mask" (1895): a collective "we" pays a "debt... to human guile" with a mask that "grins and lies"; stanza 3 turns from the world to Christ, so we overhear what the mask hides. Robinson's "Richard Cory" (1897): the townspeople ("We people on the pavement") crown a neighbor in royal diction ("imperially slim", "richer than a king"); fifteen lines of surface and one line of suicide expose the limits of what they saw. Dickinson's "I'm nobody! Who are you?" (1891): a playful conspirator who mocks fame as croaking "To an admiring bog!" Hughes's "Mother to Son" (1922): a mother's vernacular speech, built on the metaphor of a stair with "tacks", "splinters" and places "Bare.", whose persistence ("I'se still climbin'") authorizes her advice.

**Week 5 · Structure and contrast.** Blake's "The Lamb" (1789) asks and answers; "The Tyger" (1794) asks and never answers, and its returning stanza changes "Could frame" to "Dare frame". "London" (1794) builds from "charter'd" streets through the anaphora of "In every" to a climax ("But most") ending in the oxymoron "Marriage hearse". Frost's "Nothing Gold Can Stay" (1923) widens from a leaf to Eden to dawn to a law, with every verb going down. Shakespeare's Sonnet 73 (1609) gives three quatrain images of decline, each shorter in time (autumn, twilight, embers), and a couplet that turns to the listener: "This thou perceiv'st, which makes thy love more strong".

**Week 6 · Simile and metaphor.** Sonnet 18 proposes a comparison to summer, finds summer's "lease" too short, turns at "But thy eternal summer shall not fade", and claims that the poem itself "gives life to thee". Sonnet 130 refuses every conventional comparison ("nothing like the sun", "If hairs be wires") and calls them "false compare". Burns's "A Red, Red Rose" (1794) joins fresh similes (a rose "newly sprung in June") to hyperbolic vows ("Till a' the seas gang dry"). Dickinson's "Hope" (1891) develops one extended metaphor, a bird that sings "the tune without the words" and never "asked a crumb". Hughes's "The Negro Speaks of Rivers" (1921) uses the simile "My soul has grown deep like the rivers" to give a collective speaker the depth of history.

## The essay moves you practised

1. **Name the speaker accurately** before you interpret anything: "a mother addressing her son", "the town's poor, speaking as 'we'".
2. **Explain, don't identify.** "This is a metaphor" earns nothing; tenor, vehicle, ground and function earn the points.
3. **Read structure as argument.** Say what each stanza does and where the poem turns; connect the shape to the meaning in the same sentence.
4. **Use contrast as a thesis engine.** "By setting X against Y, the poet reveals Z" is a reliable thesis frame for Q1.
5. **Quote short.** Three or four words embedded in your sentence ("guile", "charter'd", "false compare") beat a quoted stanza.
6. **Sophistication** in this unit usually means seeing a tension: the mask that hides pain yet the poem that reveals it; grief and acceptance in Frost; a love proved by refusing praise in Sonnet 130.

## Common traps

- Treating the speaker as the poet (Hughes is not a mother).
- Reading line by line instead of sentence by sentence.
- Answering a question the poem leaves open ("The Tyger").
- Mistaking Sonnet 130 for an insult, or "this" in Sonnet 18 for the summer's day.
- Importing qualities the poem does not use (the rose's thorns).
- Summary instead of commentary: retelling "Richard Cory" without explaining a single word.

## What the unit exam looks like

Forty-four items in about 75 minutes. Four passage sets: two **unseen** poems (Christina Rossetti's "Up-Hill", a question-and-answer allegory of life as a journey, and Dunbar's "Sympathy", the caged bird whose song is "a prayer"), and two studied poems (Sonnet 18 and "Mother to Son"). Then cross-unit items on Robinson, Blake, Frost and Dickinson; fill-in-the-blank terms (vehicle, oxymoron); select-all items on similes and contrasts; two short answers (a claim about "Up-Hill"; the caged bird's song compared with Dunbar's mask); and a 6-point Q1-style essay on Rossetti's sonnet "Remember", whose speaker first asks to be remembered and then, at the turn, tells her beloved to "forget and smile". For the unseen poems, use the unit's method: name the speaker and addressee, mark the turn, find the central comparison, and ask what is set against what.
