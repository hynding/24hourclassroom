# Unit 8 Summary · Poetry III

**Exam weight:** poetry is 36-45% of the multiple-choice section, and Q1 (poetry analysis) is one of the three essays · **CED skills:** 3.C, 3.D, 5.B, 5.C, 6.B, 6.D, 7.B, 7.C, 7.D, 7.E · **Weeks:** 25-27

## The skills in this unit

| Skill | What it means here |
|---|---|
| 3.C Structure | Find where a poem turns (stanza break, "At once", "Surely", "O kinsmen!", a change of tense or addressee) and explain what the turn does. |
| 3.D Contrast | Name the juxtapositions (living Duchess vs portrait, ship vs iceberg, inn vs battlefield) and say what placing them together reveals. |
| 5.B Words | Explain the work done by one word: "stoop", "fatuous", "guttering", "quaint". |
| 5.C Symbol | A real thing that suggests more: roads, falcon, thrush, Goldengrove, the sun in "Futility". Explain with details, never with a one-word code. |
| 6.B Metaphor | Owen's drowning, McKay's hogs and dogs, Millay's tree, Hardy's wedding of ship and ice. Metaphor is how these poets argue. |
| 6.D Allusion | Homer and Dante behind "Ulysses", Neptune in Browning, Bethlehem in Yeats, Horace in Owen, the Fall in Hopkins. Say what the borrowed story adds or reverses. |
| 7.B-7.E Writing | A defensible thesis, commentary that explains, sufficient evidence, and controlled sentences, in single-poem and paired-poem Q1 essays. |

## Week 25 · Dramatic monologue and irony

**Browning, "My Last Duchess" (1842).** The Duke of Ferrara shows a visiting envoy a portrait of his late wife. Three marks of the dramatic monologue: a speaker who is not the poet, a silent listener, a revealing situation (a marriage negotiation). Key moves: the curtain only he may draw (control); the "spot / Of joy" he resents others causing (jealousy); "I choose / Never to stoop" (pride as self-indictment); "I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together" (understated murder); the dowry and the Neptune bronze "cast ... for me" (marriage as possession). The poem runs on dramatic irony: every boast is a confession.

**Tennyson, "Ulysses" (1842).** The aged king leaves Ithaca to Telemachus and rallies his mariners for a last voyage. Contrasts: hearth and sea, son and father ("He works his work, I mine"), age and will ("Made weak by time and fate, but strong in will"). Allusions: Homer's Odysseus, Dante's drowned Ulysses, the Happy Isles and Achilles. Two defensible readings, heroic and ironic; the best essays hold both.

## Week 26 · Symbol and shift

**Frost, "The Road Not Taken" (1916).** The roads were "really about the same"; the claim "I took the one less traveled by" belongs to the imagined future retelling. Symbol of choice, and of the stories we tell about choice.

**Yeats, "The Second Coming" (1920).** Stanza 1 diagnoses collapse (the falcon beyond the falconer, "Things fall apart"); stanza 2 turns to a vision summoned by the hope of the Second Coming, a sphinx-like "rough beast" slouching "towards Bethlehem to be born". Symbol and allusion reverse redemption into dread. The 1920 text reads "a waste of desert sand" and "Wind shadows".

**Hardy, "The Darkling Thrush" (December 1900).** Two stanzas of a dead century ("The Century's corpse outleant"), a turn at "At once", and an "aged thrush, frail, gaunt, and small" singing a "Hope, whereof he knew / And I was unaware". Hope against the evidence, heard but not shared.

**Hopkins, "Spring and Fall" (1918).** The child grieving for "Goldengrove unleaving" is mourning mortality: "It is Margaret you mourn for." Shifts from present grief to colder age to knowing grief; "Fall" as autumn and Eden.

## Week 27 · War, witness and the poetry essay

**Owen, "Dulce et Decorum Est" (1920).** The gas attack, the man "drowning" under "a green sea", the present-tense dream stanza ("guttering, choking, drowning"), and the bitter address to "My friend" that brands Horace's line "The old Lie".

**McKay, "If We Must Die" (1919).** A Shakespearean sonnet of resistance. The animal metaphor reverses: the speakers refuse to die "like hogs", the mob becomes "the murderous, cowardly pack", and the kinsmen die "Like men". Turn at line 9: "O kinsmen!"

**Millay, "What lips my lips have kissed" (1923).** Petrarchan sonnet: the octave's rain "full of ghosts", the sestet's "lonely tree" that cannot name the vanished birds but knows its boughs are silent. Forgetting and grief at once.

**Hardy, "The Convergence of the Twain" (1912).** Stanzas I-V show the Titanic's luxury on the sea floor (mirrors reflecting the sea-worm, "lightless" jewels, the fishes' question); stanzas VI-XI form one sentence in which the Immanent Will prepares the ship's "sinister mate" until "consummation comes". Marriage metaphor turns accident into fate; satire of "the Pride of Life".

## Vocabulary to own

Dramatic monologue · silent auditor · dramatic, verbal and situational irony · juxtaposition · euphemism · allusion (direct and indirect) · symbol (conventional and contextual) · shift / turn / volta · anaphora · chiasmus · paradox · metaphor, extended metaphor, implied metaphor · vehicle and tenor · Petrarchan and Shakespearean sonnet · blank verse · heroic couplet · embedded quotation · literary present tense · point-by-point comparison.

## Essay moves practised

1. **Read for the prompt's question.** "Complex attitude", "complex perspective", "complex response": the answer always holds two things in tension (charm and menace; hope and unbelief; tenderness and despair).
2. **Build the line of reasoning from techniques tied to meaning.** Not "imagery, then metaphor, then tone", but "the curtain shows control; the repeated blush shows jealousy; the bronze shows mastery".
3. **Use shifts as a spine.** A poem's turn is often the natural order of an essay: before, the turn, after.
4. **Explain, then link.** Every quotation earns a sentence on *how* it works and a clause on *why it matters* to the thesis.
5. **Compare in every paragraph** of a paired essay, with transitions that argue (*whereas, while, both ... but*).
6. **Control the sentences.** Short embedded quotations, slashes for line breaks, present tense, precise verbs.

## Common traps

- Believing the speaker (the Duke's modesty, Frost's "less traveled" road, Hardy's "quaint and curious").
- Naming a device without its function ("this is an allusion").
- Decoding symbols one-to-one instead of showing what details open them.
- Calling Hardy's thrush-listener hopeful, or Millay's speaker heartless.
- Writing two separate essays for a paired prompt.
- Quoting from a later edition than the one in front of you: Owen's 1920 text and Yeats's 1920 text differ from many modern printings.

## The unit exam

45 items in about 90 minutes. Five passage sets in Section I style: Owen's "Futility" and Hardy's "The Man He Killed" (both unseen), the first half of "The Convergence of the Twain", the opening of "My Last Duchess" and "Spring and Fall". Then items across the unit on allusion, metaphor, structure, evidence and composition, two short answers (the sun in "Futility"; a thesis on "The Man He Killed"), and a paired-poem Q1 essay comparing the two unseen poems, scored on the 6-point rubric (thesis 0-1, evidence and commentary 0-4, sophistication 0-1). Read each poem twice before the questions, mark the turn, and identify the speaker and listener first.
