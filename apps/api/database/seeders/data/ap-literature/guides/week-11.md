# Week 11 · Setting as Moral Landscape in "Young Goodman Brown"

**CED skills:** 2.B Explain the function of setting in a narrative · 2.C Describe the relationship between a character and a setting
**Big Ideas:** Setting (SET), Character (CHR), Narration (NAR)
**Read:** Nathaniel Hawthorne, "Young Goodman Brown" (1835), in *Mosses from an Old Manse*: [Project Gutenberg #512](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/512)
**Spiral:** Week 1's narrator and point of view (4.A, 4.B) return in Hawthorne's hedging narrator; week 2's setting details (2.A) become this week's setting *functions*; week 5's contrast (3.D) organizes the whole story; the thesis and commentary work of weeks 7 and 9 (7.B, 7.C) goes into this unit's Q2 essays.

## What you need to be able to do

- **2.B** Explain what a setting *does* in a narrative: how it creates a conflict, tests a character, carries an idea, or shapes the reader's response. Describing a place is 2.A; saying why the place matters is 2.B.
- **2.C** Describe how a character relates to a setting (at home in it, at odds with it, changed by it) and what that relationship reveals.

This unit returns to short fiction, the genre behind nearly half of the multiple-choice section, and it centres on the **Q2 prose fiction analysis essay**: a passage you have never seen, forty minutes, and a prompt that asks how the author "uses literary elements and techniques" to convey something about a character or relationship. Setting is one of the elements you will reach for most often, and it is the one students most often only describe. This week trains the move from description to function.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Setting | Time, place and social world of a narrative, including the conditions (light, weather, season) that colour a scene. |
| Function of setting | What the setting accomplishes: establishing conflict, mirroring or testing a character, carrying symbolic meaning, controlling pace or mood. |
| Moral landscape | A place whose physical features stand for moral conditions, as Hawthorne's forest stands for the hidden life of the heart. |
| Allegory | A narrative in which people, places and objects also stand for abstract ideas, often signalled by names (Faith, Goodman). |
| Threshold | A boundary place (a door, a path's entrance, a corner) where a character passes from one world to another. |
| Frame | The scenes that open and close a story; here, Salem village before and after the night. |
| Ambiguity | Deliberate openness to more than one reading; Hawthorne refuses to say whether the meeting was real. |
| Hedging | Qualifying a statement so that the narrator does not vouch for it ("Some affirm," "must have been"). |

## The text in context

Nathaniel Hawthorne (1804-1864) was born in Salem, Massachusetts, the descendant of John Hathorne, one of the magistrates of the 1692 witch trials; he added the *w* to his name, many readers think, to put distance between himself and that ancestor. "Young Goodman Brown" appeared in the *New-England Magazine* in 1835 and was collected in *Mosses from an Old Manse* in 1846. It is set in the late seventeenth century: Brown's father fought in King Philip's War (1675-1676), and several of the women at the witch-meeting carry names of people accused in 1692.

Two features of Hawthorne's method matter for this week. First, he writes **romance** in the nineteenth-century sense: fiction that allows the marvellous into a recognizable world, so that a man can walk from his own front door into a scene that might be supernatural. Second, he habitually **qualifies** the marvellous. Things "seemed" or "must have been"; witnesses "affirm." The result is a story that reads as allegory and as psychological case study at once, and AP prompts reward students who can hold both.

## Close reading

### 1. The threshold: a door, a corner and a head in ribbons

> So they parted; and the young man pursued his way until, being about to turn the corner by the meeting-house, he looked back and saw the head of Faith still peeping after him with a melancholy air, in spite of her pink ribbons.

**Observe.** The place names are civic and religious (the street, the meeting-house); the person left behind is named Faith; the colour detail is pink, domestic, youthful; the mood is "melancholy" despite it.

**Interpret.** Brown is leaving more than his wife. The corner by the meeting-house is the last point at which the village's church is in view, and the narrator lets us see it through Brown's backward look. The "in spite of" clause matters: the cheerful ribbons cannot cover Faith's worry.

**Function (2.B).** The threshold sets up the story's spatial argument: the village is the place of faith and watching love; whatever lies beyond the corner is defined as its opposite. Because the story returns to this same corner at the end, the opening detail becomes a measuring stick for how much Brown has changed.

### 2. The road that closes behind

> He had taken a dreary road, darkened by all the gloomiest trees of the forest, which barely stood aside to let the narrow path creep through, and closed immediately behind.

**Observe.** Superlatives ("all the gloomiest"), a path that "creep[s]" (a word with a serpent in it), trees that act ("stood aside," "closed").

**Interpret.** The forest is personified as a gatekeeper that admits Brown grudgingly and then shuts the gate. A path that closes behind you is a path you cannot simply retrace.

**Function (2.B, 2.C).** The setting tells us what Brown will not admit: his "one night" is not reversible. His relationship to the place is already one of willing entrapment. The sentence comes immediately after the narrator's ironic praise of his "excellent resolve," so the landscape answers the resolve: you think you can come back; the trees think otherwise.

### 3. An empty forest full of people

> It was all as lonely as could be; and there is this peculiarity in such a solitude, that the traveller knows not who may be concealed by the innumerable trunks and the thick boughs overhead; so that with lonely footsteps he may yet be passing through an unseen multitude.

**Observe.** The narrator steps out of the story into a general present tense ("there is this peculiarity"); the paradox of lonely footsteps among a multitude.

**Interpret.** Solitude is not emptiness; it is not knowing who is there. That is a description of a forest and of a moral condition: we cannot see into our neighbours' hearts.

**Function.** This is foreshadowing by setting. Every later revelation (riders Brown hears but cannot see, voices in a cloud, the congregation that grows "out of the darkness") makes the "unseen multitude" literal. It also shows how Hawthorne turns a place into an idea without leaving the realistic surface.

### 4. Back in the village

> The good old minister was taking a walk along the graveyard to get an appetite for breakfast and meditate his sermon, and bestowed a blessing, as he passed, on Goodman Brown. He shrank from the venerable saint as if to avoid an anathema.

**Observe.** Nothing in the morning village is sinister: a walk, breakfast, a sermon, a blessing. The only violent verb is Brown's ("shrank").

**Interpret.** The setting is unchanged; Brown's relationship to it is reversed. A blessing feels to him like a curse ("anathema").

**Function (2.C).** The return to the village isolates the change in the character. This is why the narrator can say "Be it so if you will" about the dream question: what matters is that Brown can no longer live in the place he came from.

## From reading to writing

Q2 prompts never say "discuss the setting." They ask how the author uses literary elements and techniques to convey, for example, "the complex relationship between a character and a place" or "a character's shifting perspective." Setting becomes evidence only when you explain what it does.

**Model paragraph (on the departure passage):**

> Hawthorne makes the forest answer Brown's self-deception before Brown can act on it. Having promised himself that "after this one night" he will "cling to her skirts and follow her to heaven," Brown takes a road that the trees "barely stood aside" to admit and that "closed immediately behind." The personified trees contradict the promise: a man who believes he can step out of goodness for one night and step back finds the way back already shut. The forest's crowd of possible watchers, "an unseen multitude," completes the irony, since Brown imagines he is slipping away alone when the story will reveal that everyone he knows has walked this path. The setting therefore does more than frighten him; it exposes the bargain at the centre of his character, the belief that sin can be visited without being lived in.

*Why it works:* the claim is about Brown, not about the forest; each quotation is embedded and followed by what it shows; the commentary links two techniques (personification, paradox) to one idea; the last sentence returns to the claim and pushes it further.

**Weak version:**

> The forest in this passage is very dark and gloomy. Hawthorne says it is "a dreary road, darkened by all the gloomiest trees of the forest." This shows the setting is scary and that something bad will happen.

*Why it scores low:* it describes rather than explains; "something bad will happen" could be said of any spooky setting; there is no claim about Brown, so the evidence supports nothing in particular.

## Common misreadings

- **"It was all a dream, so it doesn't matter."** The narrator never settles the question, and says that either way it was "a dream of evil omen." Arguing that the story is pointless if dreamed ignores the ending's whole emphasis on consequence.
- **"The story proves the Puritans were all hypocrites."** The evidence is staged by the devil, in firelight, through sounds and rumours. A stronger reading notices that Brown's certainty about everyone's guilt is itself a kind of sin, the one that ruins his life.
- **"Faith definitely went to the meeting."** The ribbon falls, but she is wearing ribbons in the morning, and the narrator says "Whether Faith obeyed he knew not." Keep the ambiguity in your claim.
- **Treating setting as background.** In Hawthorne, every place is chosen: the meeting-house corner, the closing path, the rock like a pulpit. If you mention a place, say what it does.
- **Missing the narrator's irony.** "Excellent resolve" and "pleasant and praiseworthy meditations" are not compliments. The narrator knows more than Brown and lets us see it.
