# Week 10 · Endings and the Literary Argument: Volume III and Walton's Close

**CED skills:** 7.E Demonstrate control over the elements of composition to communicate clearly
**Big Ideas:** Literary Argumentation (LAN); with Character (CHR), Structure (STR) and Narration (NAR)
**Read:** Mary Shelley, *Frankenstein* (1818), Volume III, Chapters I-VII, including Walton's concluding letters (1831 edition: Chapters 18-24). Text: [Project Gutenberg #41445](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/41445).
**Spiral:** everything in the unit converges: conflict (3.F) and thesis (7.B) from week 7, change and significant events (1.B, 3.E) from week 8, commentary and evidence (7.C, 7.D) from week 9, and the frame narrative from the first letter.

## What you need to be able to do

- **7.E** Control the elements of composition so a reader can follow your argument: an introduction that states the thesis, body paragraphs that each advance one step of the line of reasoning, transitions that show the relation between steps, precise word choice, varied and controlled sentences, and a conclusion that does more than repeat.
- Put the whole unit together in a **complete literary argument essay** (AP Question 3) on a novel you now know from end to end.

In plain language: last week you built strong paragraphs. This week you build an essay out of them that reads as one argument rather than a stack of observations.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Literary argument (Q3) | The AP essay that gives a literary concept and lets you choose a work; you argue how the concept contributes to the meaning of the work as a whole. |
| Meaning of the work as a whole | What the entire novel suggests about human experience, not one scene's effect. Q3 asks you to reach it. |
| Topic sentence | The first sentence of a body paragraph: a claim that advances the line of reasoning, not a plot point. |
| Transition | A word, phrase or sentence that shows the logical relation between ideas: contrast, cause, concession, escalation. |
| Concession | Acknowledging a point against your thesis, then showing why your reading still holds. A sign of sophistication when it is earned. |
| Syntax control | Varying sentence length and structure for emphasis and clarity, and keeping each sentence's subject the thing the sentence is about. |
| Dénouement | The resolution after the climax; here Walton's choice to turn back, Victor's death and the creature's farewell. |

## The text in context: what happens

**Ch. I.** Victor delays both the female creature and his marriage to Elizabeth. He travels to England with Clerval, meeting him at Strasburgh, by way of the Rhine.

**Ch. II.** London, then Oxford, Matlock and the Lakes, then Edinburgh and toward Perth. Victor leaves Clerval and sets up a laboratory in a hut on a remote island in the Orkneys.

**Ch. III.** Working at night, Victor reasons that a female might be "ten thousand times more malignant than her mate" and that the two might breed "a race of devils." Seeing the creature's grin at the window, he tears the half-made female to pieces. The creature threatens him: "I shall be with you on your wedding-night." Victor sinks the remains at sea, drifts in his boat, and lands on the Irish coast.

**Ch. IV.** Accused of murdering a young man found strangled on the shore, he is brought before the magistrate, Mr. Kirwin, and sees the body: Clerval. He lies in fever for about two months in prison; his father comes; he is cleared when it is proved he was in the Orkneys when the murder occurred.

**Ch. V.** Elizabeth writes, offering to release him if he loves another. He promises to marry her and to reveal a secret after the wedding. They marry and cross the lake toward Evian.

**Ch. VI.** At the inn, Victor, armed, searches the house for the creature, believing himself the target. He hears a scream: Elizabeth has been strangled. The creature appears at the window, grinning, and escapes into the lake. Victor's father dies soon after. Victor tells a magistrate in Geneva the truth, is not effectively helped, and swears vengeance over the graves of his family.

**Ch. VII.** The pursuit across Europe and into Russia and the Arctic, the creature leaving taunting messages and sometimes food. Then **Walton's continuation**: the ship is beset by ice; the crew demands to turn south; Victor rouses them to go on; Walton yields to his men and turns back. Victor dies. Walton finds the creature over the body, hears his last speech, and watches him leap onto an ice-raft and vanish "in darkness and distance."

## Close reading

**1. Victor's reasoning (Ch. III).**

> I was now about to form another being, of whose dispositions I was alike ignorant; she might become ten thousand times more malignant than her mate, and delight, for its own sake, in murder and wretchedness.

*Observe:* careful conditional reasoning ("might," "ignorant"), a contrast with the night of animation, when he never paused. *Interpret:* for once Victor considers consequences before acting. *Function:* this is the only clear change in Victor (1.B), and it causes the novel's catastrophe: by refusing the creature's demand on behalf of "the species of man," he provokes the murders of Clerval and Elizabeth. Whether the decision is wise or selfish (he never asks the female's consent, any more than he asked the creature's) is a strong thesis question.

**2. The threat (Ch. III).** "I shall be with you on your wedding-night." Victor reads the line as a threat against his own life, and the reader, who has seen the creature strike at Victor through William and Justine, has reason to fear otherwise. *Function:* dramatic irony drives the last third of the novel. Victor's self-centredness (week 8) persists to the end: he arms himself and searches the inn while Elizabeth waits alone.

**3. The farewell (Ch. VII).** Over Victor's body, the creature speaks to the dead man:

> Farewell, Frankenstein! If thou wert yet alive, and yet cherished a desire of revenge against me, it would be better satiated in my life than in my destruction. But it was not so; thou didst seek my extinction, that I might not cause greater wretchedness; and if yet, in some mode unknown to me, thou hast not yet ceased to think and feel, thou desirest not my life for my own misery. Blasted as thou wert, my agony was still superior to thine; for the bitter sting of remorse may not cease to rankle in my wounds until death shall close them for ever.

*Observe:* the "thou" of Montanvert returns; the creature claims a greater "agony" than Victor's. *Interpret:* he now understands Victor's motive ("that I might not cause greater wretchedness") more fairly than Victor ever understood his. *Function:* the novel ends with the made being judging the maker, and with the judge condemning himself as well; remorse becomes the bond between them. Compare Victor at Justine's trial: "The tortures of the accused did not equal mine." Both claim the greater suffering, the rivalry that has bound them throughout.

**4. Walton's choice.** The frame closes. Walton, like Victor, wanted to go on; his crew demands to turn back, and he does. Victor scorns the crew and dies unrepentant about the value of the attempt, though he warns Walton against ambition. *Function:* the frame that opened with a man choosing ambition over companionship ends with a man choosing his companions' lives over glory. Whether Walton has learned or merely yielded is open, and that openness is one of the novel's endings.

## From reading to writing: a complete literary argument (Q3)

**Sample prompt.** *Many works of literature feature a character who creates something, a work, a family, a cause, and must then answer for what that creation becomes. Either from your own reading or from the list below, choose a work of fiction in which a character must answer for a creation. Then, in a well-written essay, analyze how the character's response to that creation contributes to an interpretation of the work as a whole.*

**1. Plan the line of reasoning before writing.** Ten minutes of planning saves the essay. Write the thesis, then one topic-sentence claim per body paragraph, then the two or three pieces of evidence for each.

> *Thesis:* In *Frankenstein*, Victor's repeated flight from what he has made, at the moment of animation, at Justine's trial and on his wedding night, shows that Shelley locates the horror of creation not in the act of making but in the refusal of responsibility for what is made.
>
> *Body 1 (abandonment):* The night of animation: he judges and flees before the creature acts.
> *Body 2 (silence):* Justine's trial: feeling replaces action; "the fangs of remorse."
> *Body 3 (the one decision):* Destroying the female: the only time he weighs consequences, and he still refuses to meet the creature as a being with claims.
> *Body 4 or conclusion (the frame):* Walton turns back; responsibility to others chosen over glory.

**2. Write topic sentences that argue.** Compare "In Volume III, Victor goes to the Orkneys" (plot) with "Even Victor's one act of foresight in the Orkneys repeats his old refusal, because he decides the creature's fate without hearing him again" (claim). Each topic sentence should be provable and should move the thesis forward.

**3. Use transitions that show logic, not sequence.** "Then," "next" and "also" only list. Prefer transitions that state a relation:

- *Escalation:* "If his flight on the night of animation might be excused as shock, his silence at Justine's trial cannot be."
- *Concession:* "Victor does, at last, consider consequences in the Orkneys; yet the decision that results..."
- *Cause:* "Because Victor never answers the creature's claim, the creature answers it for him on the wedding night."

**4. Control the sentence.** Keep the grammatical subject of a sentence the thing you are making a claim about. "It is shown by Shelley that Victor's horror..." buries the subject; "Shelley shows that Victor's horror..." is direct. Vary length for emphasis: after a long sentence of evidence, a short claim lands. "He does not stay."

**5. Choose precise words.** Avoid "shows how" without saying how; avoid "very," "really," "a lot." Prefer verbs that name the move: *exposes, undercuts, complicates, mirrors, anticipates, indicts.*

**6. Conclude by widening, not repeating.** A conclusion that restates the thesis earns nothing. One that extends it (to Walton's choice, to the creature's self-condemnation, to what the novel suggests about any maker's duties) can help earn the sophistication point, if the essay has prepared it.

A model body paragraph showing control:

> If Victor's flight on the night of animation might be excused as shock, his silence at Justine's trial cannot be. He knows that the creature killed William, since lightning "discovered its shape plainly" near the scene, yet in court he describes only his own pain: "the fangs of remorse tore my bosom." The image is violent, but its object is Victor's heart, not the girl who will hang. Shelley thus turns the trial into a second abandonment, this time of an innocent human being, and the pattern becomes legible: whenever Victor's creation demands that he act, he feels instead.

## Common misreadings

- **"The creature kills Elizabeth because she insulted him."** He kills her to repay Victor for destroying the female, as he promised on the island.
- **"Victor learns his lesson and repents."** He warns Walton against ambition but, in nearly the same breath, allows that another may succeed where he failed. His final position is divided, not repentant.
- **"The creature dies at the end."** He announces that he will die and leaps onto the ice-raft; the novel ends with him "lost in darkness and distance." His death is promised, not shown.
- **"A Q3 essay should summarize the plot first."** Graders know the plot. Summary displaces argument and costs Row B points.
- **"Sophistication means big words."** It means a complex argument, sustained: tensions, alternative readings, the work's broader context, a style that is precise and vivid.

## Connections and looking ahead

The unit ends where it began, in the ice with Walton, and the frame turns into an argument about choice. Week 7's conflicts are resolved by death and departure; week 8's question of who changes is answered (Victor once, briefly; the creature, finally, toward self-judgment); week 9's argument of the creature is completed in his farewell.
