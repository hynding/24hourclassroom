# Week 08 · Consequences: William, Justine and the Meeting on the Glacier

**CED skills:** 1.B Explain the function of a character changing or remaining unchanged · 3.E Explain the function of a significant event or related set of significant events in a plot
**Big Ideas:** Character (CHR), Structure (STR)
**Read:** Mary Shelley, *Frankenstein* (1818), Volume I, Chapters V-VII, and Volume II, Chapters I-II (1831 edition: Chapters 6-10). Text: [Project Gutenberg #41445](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/41445).
**Spiral:** last week's conflicts (3.F) become events this week; the thesis work (7.B) continues; setting (week 2), contrast (week 5) and narration (week 1) all do work in these chapters.

## What you need to be able to do

- **1.B** Decide whether a character changes or stays the same across a stretch of the story, find the evidence, and explain what that change (or failure to change) *does*: what it reveals, what it causes, what idea it tests.
- **3.E** Pick out the events that turn a plot, and explain their function: what they set in motion, reveal or make impossible afterwards.

In plain language: ask of every major event, "What would the novel lose if this did not happen?" and ask of every major character, "Who is this person at the start of these chapters and at the end, and why does Shelley need that difference (or that sameness)?"

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Dynamic character | One who changes in a significant way (belief, values, understanding). |
| Static character | One who stays essentially the same; not a flaw, often a function. |
| Turning point | An event after which the direction of the plot changes. |
| Dramatic irony | The reader knows something a character does not. We know who killed William; the court does not. |
| Guilt vs responsibility | Guilt is a feeling; responsibility is a fact about cause and duty. Victor feels the first and avoids acting on the second. |
| Sublime | In Romantic writing, the awe mixed with terror that vast nature (Alps, storms, glaciers) produces. |
| Justine Moritz | A girl taken into the Frankenstein household as a servant, loved by Caroline; accused of William's murder. |
| Montanvert | The mountain above the valley of Chamounix (Chamonix), with the glacier called the Mer de Glace, where Victor meets the creature. |

## The text in context: what happens

**Vol. I, Ch. V.** Clerval nurses Victor through the winter; a letter from Elizabeth brings home news, including the history of Justine Moritz. Victor, recovered, cannot bear the sight of his chemical instruments and turns with Clerval to languages and walks in spring.

**Vol. I, Ch. VI.** A letter from his father: William, Victor's youngest brother, has been found strangled, and a miniature of their mother that he wore is missing. Victor travels home. Near Geneva, at night, a storm breaks over the lake and the Alps, and in a flash of lightning he sees the creature. He becomes convinced it killed William, and decides to say nothing, because no one would believe him. At home he learns that Justine has been accused: the miniature was found in her pocket.

**Vol. I, Ch. VII.** Justine's trial. Elizabeth speaks for her character; the court condemns her. In prison she tells Elizabeth and Victor that she confessed falsely, pressed by her confessor, to obtain absolution. She is executed. Victor, who knows she is innocent, says nothing.

**Vol. II, Ch. I.** Victor's remorse and despair; the family moves to Belrive; Elizabeth mourns the injustice of the world. In August the family travels to Chamounix.

**Vol. II, Ch. II.** Victor climbs alone to Montanvert. The creature approaches across the ice. Victor curses him; the creature answers with a speech and asks to be heard. Victor follows him to a hut, and the creature's narrative begins.

## Close reading

**1. The lightning (Vol. I, Ch. VI).**

> A flash of lightning illuminated the object, and discovered its shape plainly to me; its gigantic stature, and the deformity of its aspect, more hideous than belongs to humanity, instantly informed me that it was the wretch, the filthy dæmon to whom I had given life. What did he there? Could he be (I shuddered at the conception) the murderer of my brother?

*Observe:* the lightning "discovered" the shape: it reveals, as lightning revealed the blasted oak to the boy Victor. *Interpret:* the same natural force that seemed to promise knowledge now shows him its consequence. *Function (3.E):* the sighting turns private guilt into a plot. From here Victor knows something no one else knows, and the dramatic irony of Justine's trial depends on it. Notice also the parenthesis "(I shuddered at the conception)": "conception" is a word for an idea and for the beginning of a life.

**2. Justine's trial (Vol. I, Ch. VII).**

> The tortures of the accused did not equal mine; she was sustained by innocence, but the fangs of remorse tore my bosom, and would not forego their hold.

*Observe:* Victor compares his suffering with that of a girl on trial for her life, and decides his is worse. *Interpret:* his remorse is real, but it is turned inward; it becomes another feeling about Victor. *Function (1.B):* this is the clearest evidence that Victor does **not** change in the way the plot asks. He felt horror and fled in Chapter IV; he feels remorse and stays silent in Chapter VII. The event of the trial tests whether he will accept responsibility, and his failure is what makes Justine's death a tragedy rather than an accident. Ask yourself whether Shelley wants us to pity him here, judge him, or both; that question is a thesis.

**3. The dead calm (Vol. II, Ch. I).** The volume opens:

> Nothing is more painful to the human mind, than, after the feelings have been worked up by a quick succession of events, the dead calmness of inaction and certainty which follows, and deprives the soul both of hope and fear.

*Observe:* a general statement, in the present tense, before any action. *Interpret:* Victor stands outside his story to generalize about minds. *Function:* structurally, it marks the pause after a "quick succession of events" (murder, trial, execution) and before the next turn. The sentence shows how the narrative is paced: rapid events, then a long stretch of brooding, then the glacier.

**4. The creature's argument (Vol. II, Ch. II).**

> Remember, that I am thy creature: I ought to be thy Adam; but I am rather the fallen angel, whom thou drivest from joy for no misdeed. Every where I see bliss, from which I alone am irrevocably excluded. I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend. Make me happy, and I shall again be virtuous.

*Observe:* the creature speaks with balance and allusion (Adam and the fallen angel come from *Paradise Lost*), using "thou" as if addressing a god or a father. *Interpret:* he claims to be a **dynamic** character, changed by treatment: "benevolent and good" became "fiend." And he claims the change can be reversed. *Function (1.B, 3.E):* this meeting is the turning point of the novel. Until now the creature has been seen only through Victor's eyes ("wretch," "dæmon"); now he speaks, eloquently, and Victor's judgment of him is put on trial. The claim of change forces the novel's great question: is the creature's violence his nature, or what was done to him?

## From reading to writing: change, event and function

When you write about change, avoid "Victor changes a lot" or "the creature becomes evil." Use the three-part move:

1. **State the change (or sameness) precisely.** From what to what, and where?
2. **Cite evidence at both ends.** One detail early, one late.
3. **Explain the function.** What does the change reveal or cause, and how does it serve the novel's meaning?

*Model paragraph:*

> Victor's refusal to speak at Justine's trial shows that his remorse changes his feelings but not his conduct, and this sameness turns the trial into the novel's first indictment of its narrator. *(claim: precise sameness, function)* On the night of animation he "rushed out of the room" rather than face what he had made; at the trial, knowing Justine innocent, he again withdraws, insisting that "the fangs of remorse tore my bosom." *(evidence at both ends)* The violent image of remorse as a beast with "fangs" makes his suffering vivid, but it also keeps the sentence's attention on "my bosom" rather than on the girl who is about to die. *(commentary on diction)* Because Victor's pattern of feeling intensely while acting not at all repeats, Shelley makes Justine's execution a consequence of character rather than chance, preparing the reader to weigh the creature's later claim that he, too, was made what he is by another's neglect. *(function, link forward)*

A weaker version would say "Victor feels guilty at the trial, which shows he has changed and is sorry," mistaking intensified feeling for change and offering no function.

## Common misreadings

- **"Victor could not have saved Justine."** He believes no one would credit his story, and he may be right; but the novel never shows him trying. Whether silence was prudence or cowardice is arguable, which is exactly why it makes a good essay topic.
- **"The creature is clearly lying at Montanvert."** He is persuasive, and he has killed William. The novel lets both be true. Do not resolve the tension too early.
- **"Static characters are badly written."** Clerval and Elizabeth stay much the same; their steadiness is a measure for Victor's instability.
- **"A significant event is just a big event."** Significance is function. A short scene (the lightning flash) can matter more than a long one.
- **"The storm is just atmosphere."** It is also the scene of revelation, echoing the lightning on the oak in Volume I.

## Self-check

1. What evidence leads to Justine's arrest? What does Victor know that the court does not?
2. Find one moment where Victor's feelings change and his actions do not. Explain the function.
3. Why is the meeting at Montanvert a turning point? Name two things that are different afterwards.
4. The creature says "misery made me a fiend." What would you need from his narrative next week to test this claim?
5. How does the storm in Vol. I, Ch. VI echo an earlier event? What does the echo suggest?
