**Big Ideas:** Structure (STR), Figurative language (FIG)

**Spiral:** Last week you learned to name a speaker (4.A) and explain single words (5.B, 1.A). This week asks a bigger question: why are the parts of a poem arranged as they are? Unit 1's work on the order of events in a story (3.A, 3.B) is the prose version of the same skill: "Richard Cory" saved its death for the last line, just as Chopin saved hers for the last sentence.

## What you need to be able to do

- **3.C** Explain how the arrangement of a poem (its stanzas, repetitions, rhymes, the order of its images, its turns) contributes to its meaning.
- **3.D** Identify contrasts in a poem (between images, poems, speakers, tones, before and after) and explain what they reveal.

In plain language: when you finish a poem, you should be able to say what each stanza does, where the poem turns, and what two things it sets against each other, and then say why that design matters.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Structure | The arrangement of a poem's parts: lines, stanzas, rhyme, repetition, the order of ideas and images. |
| Shift (turn) | A change in speaker, addressee, tone, subject or argument, often marked by "but", "yet", "so" or a stanza break. |
| Contrast | Two things set against each other so that each clarifies the other. |
| Quatrain / couplet | A four-line stanza or unit / a pair of rhyming lines. |
| Shakespearean sonnet | Fourteen lines of iambic pentameter: three quatrains (ABAB CDCD EFEF) and a couplet (GG). |
| Anaphora | Repetition of a word or phrase at the start of successive lines or clauses ("In every... In every"). |
| Refrain | A repeated line or phrase that returns at intervals. |
| Paradox | A statement that seems self-contradictory but makes sense on reflection ("Nature's first green is gold"). |
| Oxymoron | Two opposed words pressed together ("Marriage hearse"). |
| Rhetorical question | A question asked for effect rather than to get an answer; a poem of unanswered questions can be a structure in itself. |

## A method for structure

Structure questions ask you to step back from the words. Four questions do most of the work:

1. **What does each stanza do?** Write a verb for each: asks, answers, lists, intensifies, turns, concludes.
2. **What repeats, and what changes in the repetition?** A refrain or a returning stanza is never quite the same the second time.
3. **Where is the turn?** Look for the place where the poem stops doing one thing and starts doing another.
4. **What is set against what?** Name the two sides of every contrast, then ask what the poem gains by holding them together.

Your answer to "what is the function of the structure?" should then connect the shape to the meaning: "Because each quatrain covers a shorter span of time, the poem makes the speaker's remaining life feel as if it is shrinking."

## The texts in context

**William Blake (1757-1827)** was a London engraver, painter and poet who printed his own books by a method he called illuminated printing, etching words and pictures together on copper plates and colouring each copy by hand. *Songs of Innocence* appeared in 1789; in 1794 he joined it to *Songs of Experience* under a title announcing "the Two Contrary States of the Human Soul". Several poems answer one another across the two books, and "The Lamb" and "The Tyger" are the best known pair. "London" belongs to Experience and reflects a city in the age of the French Revolution, of child chimney-sweeps and of wars with France.

**Robert Frost (1874-1963)** published "Nothing Gold Can Stay" in 1923 and collected it the same year in *New Hampshire*, the book that won his first Pulitzer Prize. It is eight lines of three-beat verse in rhymed couplets.

**William Shakespeare (1564-1616)** wrote 154 sonnets, published together in 1609. Sonnets 1-126 are mostly addressed to a young man, and Sonnet 73 is among them. The punctuation of the 1609 text, followed loosely by the Project Gutenberg edition quoted here, is heavier and more comma-filled than modern editions.

## Close reading

### 1. Question and answer: "The Lamb" against "The Tyger"

> Little Lamb who made thee
> Dost thou know who made thee

> Tyger Tyger, burning bright,
> In the forests of the night;
> What immortal hand or eye,
> Could frame thy fearful symmetry?

**Observe.** Both poems open by asking who made an animal. "The Lamb" asks, lists the lamb's gifts ("clothing of delight", "a tender voice"), and in stanza 2 answers: "Little Lamb I'll tell thee! / He is called by thy name, / For he calls himself a Lamb". "The Tyger" never answers. Its questions grow shorter and more urgent: "What the hammer? what the chain, / In what furnace was thy brain?"

**Interpret.** The structures are the two states of the soul. The child of Innocence can answer because his world is whole: child, lamb and creator share one name. The speaker of Experience faces a creature of fire and can only ask, imagining a blacksmith-god at a forge. The paired poems meet in a single line of "The Tyger": "Did he who made the Lamb make thee?"

**Function.** The contrast between the poems (3.D) is carried by their structures (3.C): an answered question against unanswered ones. The last stanza of "The Tyger" repeats the first with one change: "Could frame thy fearful symmetry?" becomes "Dare frame thy fearful symmetry?" The question has moved from whether such a maker is able to whether any maker would dare. A returning stanza that changes one word is a classic structural detail to explain.

### 2. Accumulation and climax: "London"

> In every cry of every Man,
> In every Infants cry of fear,
> In every voice: in every ban,
> The mind-forg'd manacles I hear

**Observe.** The stanza is a single sentence whose main clause ("I hear") arrives only in the last line, after five repetitions of "every". The first stanza was about seeing ("mark in every face I meet"); from here the speaker hears.

**Interpret.** Anaphora piles up voices until the suffering is universal: men, infants, every voice, every legal prohibition. The "mind-forg'd manacles" are chains made in the mind: habits of fear and obedience that people carry even where no law is visible.

**Function.** The poem's four quatrains build steadily. Stanza 1 shows the "charter'd" city, stanza 2 generalizes the bondage, stanza 3 names the institutions ("Every blackning Church appalls", "the hapless Soldiers sigh / Runs in blood down Palace walls"), and stanza 4, beginning "But most", gives the worst sound last: "the youthful Harlots curse / Blasts the new-born Infants tear / And blights with plagues the Marriage hearse". The structure is a climax, and its final oxymoron fuses the beginnings of life (marriage, birth) with death.

### 3. From a leaf to a law: "Nothing Gold Can Stay"

> Then leaf subsides to leaf.
> So Eden sank to grief,
> So dawn goes down to day.
> Nothing gold can stay.

**Observe.** The poem's first five lines describe spring leaves: "Nature's first green is gold, / Her hardest hue to hold." Then two lines begin with "So", and the last line states a rule. Every verb in the passage moves downward: "subsides", "sank", "goes down".

**Interpret.** "So" means "in the same way". A leaf losing its early gold is like the fall from Eden and like dawn fading into ordinary daylight. Even day, normally the bright part, is a descent from dawn.

**Function.** The structure widens from one leaf to all of nature, human history and time, which is why the last line can sound like a law. The contrast set up in line 1 (green against gold, the ordinary against the precious) is what the widening proves: the most valuable stage is the first and the shortest. The calm, rhymed couplets give the loss the finality of a proverb.

### 4. Three images and a turn: Sonnet 73

> In me thou see'st the twilight of such day
> As after sunset fadeth in the west;
> Which by and by black night doth take away,
> Death's second self, that seals up all in rest.

**Observe.** The second quatrain is one of three images: autumn ("yellow leaves, or none, or few"), twilight, and a fire "That on the ashes of his youth doth lie". Each quatrain begins with what "thou" can see in "me". The couplet changes subject: "This thou perceiv'st, which makes thy love more strong, / To love that well, which thou must leave ere long."

**Interpret.** Each image covers less time: a season, a sunset, the last glow of embers. Night is "Death's second self" because it closes everything in sleep. The fire is "Consum'd with that which it was nourish'd by", smothered by the ash of the wood that once fed it.

**Function.** The Shakespearean form fits the argument. Three quatrains present three increasingly urgent pieces of evidence that the speaker is near death; the couplet turns from the speaker to the listener and draws the conclusion. The contrast between twelve lines of decline and two lines of strengthened love is the sonnet's point: mortality is the reason love grows "more strong".

## From reading to writing

When you write about structure, connect the shape to the meaning in every sentence. Do not write "The poem has three quatrains and a couplet" and stop.

**Model paragraph (on Sonnet 73)**

> Shakespeare arranges his three images of decline so that the time left to the speaker seems to shrink before our eyes. **[claim about structure]** The first quatrain gives a season, autumn's "yellow leaves, or none, or few"; the second only the "twilight of such day" that "black night doth take away"; the third the last "glowing" of a fire on its "death-bed". **[evidence in order]** Moving from months to hours to the final minutes of a fire, the quatrains make the speaker's life contract with each image, so that by line 12 death seems only moments away. **[commentary on function]** That urgency is what gives the couplet its force: the listener's love grows "more strong" precisely because the poem has shown how little time is left. **[link to the turn]**

**A weak version:** "Sonnet 73 has three quatrains and a couplet. The quatrains are about getting old and the couplet is about love. This shows the speaker is old." It names the form but never explains what the arrangement does, and its last sentence restates the obvious.

## Common misreadings

- **"Structure" means "rhyme scheme".** Rhyme is one part. Order of images, repetitions, questions and turns usually matter more.
- **Answering "The Tyger".** The poem does not say whether the same God made both animals; an essay that claims it does is not defensible. Its point is the question.
- **Reading "London" as a tourist poem.** "Charter'd" is a criticism, not a boast.
- **Missing the turn in Sonnet 73.** The couplet is about the listener's love, not about the speaker's death. Many readers stop at "the poem is about getting old".
- **Treating "Nothing Gold Can Stay" as simply sad.** The poem calls loss "grief" but presents it as natural law, in the measured voice of a proverb; the tension between those is worth writing about.
