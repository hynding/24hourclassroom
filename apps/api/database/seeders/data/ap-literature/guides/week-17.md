**Spiral:** Unit 3's *Frankenstein* gave you a long work read in stages and the literary argument essay; Unit 4 gave you characters' relationships (1.C, 1.D) and setting (2.B); Units 2 and 5 gave you metaphor, allusion and structure in verse. A Shakespeare play draws on all of them, because a play is verse, character and plot at once.

## What you need to be able to do

- **1.E** Read a character through what he chooses, does and says, and explain the complexity this reveals: two of his own statements that pull against each other, or a choice that contradicts a stated value. Then say what the complexity does for the play.
- **3.D** Find the contrasts a text is built on (between characters, speeches, scenes, images, or appearance and reality) and explain what each one makes the audience understand or feel.
- Know the conventions of Shakespeare's stage well enough to use them as evidence: soliloquy, aside, blank verse and prose, the rhymed couplet at a scene's end.

In plain terms, a play has no narrator to tell you what a character thinks. Everything you know comes from what characters say to each other, what they say alone, and what they do. This week is about learning to read that evidence.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Soliloquy | A speech by a character alone on stage (or believing himself unheard), thinking aloud. By convention it gives us his private mind, though he can still misjudge or deceive himself. |
| Aside | A brief remark heard by the audience but not, by convention, by the other characters present. |
| Blank verse | Unrhymed iambic pentameter: five stressed beats in a ten-syllable line. Shakespeare's normal measure for serious and public speech. |
| Prose | Unmetred speech. In *Hamlet* it marks informal talk, servants and gravediggers, letters, and Hamlet's "antic" behaviour. |
| Couplet | Two rhymed lines; at the end of a scene it works like an audible full stop. |
| Foil | A character whose likeness to another throws their differences into relief (Laertes and Fortinbras will be Hamlet's). |
| Antic disposition | Hamlet's phrase (1.5) for the strange, mad-seeming manner he says he may choose to adopt. |
| Quarto, Folio | The early printings: First Quarto 1603, Second Quarto 1604-05, First Folio 1623. Modern editions combine them. |

**A note on line numbers.** Project Gutenberg's text (the one quoted in this course) has no line numbers. When this course cites act.scene.line, for example 1.2.133, the numbers are those of the Folger edition. Other editions number differently, especially in prose scenes, so in an essay always cite the act and scene and quote the words; the numbers are a convenience.

## The text in context

*Hamlet* was probably written around 1599-1601, for the Lord Chamberlain's Men at the Globe. It survives in three early versions: a short, garbled First Quarto (1603), a long Second Quarto (1604-05) whose title page advertised it as enlarged to almost twice the length of the first and the First Folio (1623) printed by Shakespeare's fellow actors after his death. Each contains lines the others lack. Almost every modern text, including Gutenberg's and the Folger, is a conflation, which is why your copy may differ slightly from the quotations here.

The play belongs to a popular Elizabethan genre, the revenge tragedy: a ghost, a secret murder, a son charged with vengeance, feigned madness, a play within the play and a stage full of corpses. Shakespeare keeps the machinery and turns it inward, so the question of the play is not only how Hamlet will take revenge but what kind of man is being asked to do it, and whether he should.

Act I does three jobs. Scene 1 creates unease: sentries at midnight, a ghost in the dead king's armour, the threat of young Fortinbras of Norway. Scene 2 shows the new order (Claudius, now king and married to his brother's widow, conducting smooth public business) against the one figure in black who will not join in. Scenes 3 to 5 set Polonius's family beside Hamlet's and then deliver the Ghost's revelation: Claudius poisoned his brother, and Hamlet must "Revenge his foul and most unnatural murder."

## Close reading

### 1. A public speech (1.2.1-14)

Claudius opens his first court with the marriage:

> Have we, as ’twere with a defeated joy,—
> With an auspicious and one dropping eye,
> With mirth in funeral, and with dirge in marriage,
> In equal scale weighing delight and dole,—
> Taken to wife

**Observe.** Every line balances one thing against its opposite: joy and defeat, an eye that smiles and an eye that weeps, mirth and funeral, dirge and marriage, delight and dole. The syntax holds back the main verb, "Taken to wife," until the end of a long build-up.

**Interpret.** The balance is the argument. If grief and joy weigh exactly the same, then a wedding a few weeks after a funeral is not indecent but reasonable. The delayed verb lets the awkward fact arrive cushioned in rhetoric.

**Function (3.D).** On its own the speech is masterly. Its function becomes clear only when the court leaves and Hamlet's soliloquy contradicts it, as the next example shows.

### 2. A private speech (1.2.133-153)

Alone, Hamlet wishes "that this too too solid flesh would melt," calls the world "an unweeded garden, / That grows to seed," and measures the two kings:

> So excellent a king; that was, to this,
> Hyperion to a satyr

**Observe.** Where Claudius balances, Hamlet ranks: sun god against goat-man. Where Claudius delays his verb smoothly, Hamlet's sentences break: "But two months dead!—nay, not so much, not two:" The time shrinks every time he repeats it, from two months to a month to "A little month."

**Interpret.** The contrast shows the same events from two sides. What Claudius made orderly, Hamlet finds monstrous. The broken syntax is the sound of a mind circling something it cannot bear.

**Function (1.E and 3.D).** The soliloquy is our first proof that Hamlet's private self differs from his public silence, and it ends with a choice: "But break my heart,—for I must hold my tongue." Before any ghost has asked anything of him, his first decision is to conceal. The contrast between the two speeches also teaches the audience a habit the play rewards: distrust the polished surface.

### 3. "I know not seems" (1.2.79-89)

Earlier in the scene Gertrude asks why his grief "seems" so particular. He answers:

> Seems, madam! Nay, it is; I know not seems.
> [...] these, indeed, seem;
> For they are actions that a man might play;
> But I have that within which passeth show;
> These but the trappings and the suits of woe.

**Observe.** He separates outward signs (his "inky cloak," sighs, tears, which anyone could act) from an inner reality that "passeth show," that cannot be displayed.

**Interpret.** Hamlet sets himself up as the enemy of performance in a court that runs on it.

**Function (1.E).** Hold that thought until 1.5, where he tells Horatio he may choose "To put an antic disposition on," to act strangely, even madly, on purpose. The man who knows "not seems" will spend the next acts seeming. That is a complexity created entirely by his own speech and choice, and its function is large: from now on, neither the court nor the audience can be sure which of his words are sincere.

### 4. A couplet that closes the act (1.5)

> The time is out of joint:—O cursed spite,
> That ever I was born to set it right!

**Observe.** The rhyme marks the end of a movement. The first image is medical: time is a dislocated limb, and Hamlet is the one who must set the bone.

**Interpret.** One line accepts the task ("born to set it right"); the other curses it ("O cursed spite"). He takes up revenge and resents it in the same breath.

**Function.** The ambivalence the couplet records is what drives Acts II to IV. When you write about Hamlet's delay later, this is your earliest evidence that the reluctance is in him from the start, not merely forced on him by circumstances.

## From reading to writing

**Prompt-style task:** Explain how Shakespeare uses contrast in Act I, scene 2 to reveal Hamlet's character.

**Model paragraph (claim, evidence, commentary):**

> Shakespeare introduces Hamlet as the one honest voice in a court of polished surfaces, and he does it by contrast. Claudius's address to the court is a model of balance: he has married "With mirth in funeral, and with dirge in marriage, / In equal scale weighing delight and dole," as if grief and joy could be weighed out and cancelled. The moment the court leaves, Hamlet's soliloquy undoes that balance. Instead of weighing, he ranks: his father and his uncle are "Hyperion to a satyr," god and beast. Instead of smooth periods, his sentences break and restart ("But two months dead!—nay, not so much, not two"), and the interval keeps shrinking as his outrage grows. The contrast does more than show that Hamlet disagrees with the King; it teaches the audience to hear Claudius's eloquence as concealment and Hamlet's broken speech as truth. Yet the soliloquy ends, "But break my heart,—for I must hold my tongue": the prince who sees through the court's performance chooses, for now, to keep up a performance of his own.

**Why it works.** The claim is arguable (Hamlet as "the one honest voice," with a qualification at the end). Each quotation is chosen for a reason and followed by commentary that says what the technique does: balance, ranking, broken syntax. The last two sentences reach for complexity (1.E): the truth-teller also conceals.

**A weak version:** "In Act I, scene 2, Claudius gives a speech and then Hamlet gives a soliloquy. Hamlet is sad because his father died and his mother remarried. He says 'Frailty, thy name is woman!' This shows he is upset." This summarises, quotes without explaining, and never says what the contrast does.

## Common misreadings

- **"A soliloquy is always the truth."** It is sincere: no one is being deceived. But a speaker can be wrong, or unfair, or working himself into a feeling. "Frailty, thy name is woman!" tells us about Hamlet's state of mind more than about women.
- **"Hamlet suspects murder from the start."** In 1.2 his disgust is at the remarriage. The murder is revealed only in 1.5 (though he exclaims "O my prophetic soul!" when he hears it, as if a suspicion had been waiting).
- **"The Ghost wants revenge on Gertrude too."** It explicitly forbids that: "Leave her to heaven."
- **"Prose means a low character."** In this play prose often marks a mode, not a class: Hamlet himself speaks prose whenever he plays the madman or talks informally.
- **"Polonius's advice is the moral of the play."** "To thine own self be true" is famous out of context. In context it caps a list of prudent rules from a man about to spy on his own children.
- **Citing line numbers as if they were universal.** Cite act and scene, quote the words, and treat the numbers as edition-specific.

## Connections and looking ahead

- **Earlier units.** Like Victor Frankenstein, Hamlet is a gifted, isolated young man given a task he did not choose. Like the characters of Unit 4, he is defined by a relationship (with his mother) that the text reveals through small, telling details.
- **Next week (Act II).** The "antic disposition" goes public, and the play fills with characters reporting on Hamlet: Ophelia, Polonius, Rosencrantz and Guildenstern. You will ask how reliable those reports are (4.D) and how the contrasting characters around Hamlet define him (1.C).
- **The exam.** Longer fiction or drama is 15-18% of the multiple-choice section, and a play is a natural choice for the Q3 literary argument essay. Prompts about a character's internal conflict, a character who must choose between duty and conscience, or appearance and reality all invite *Hamlet*. Act I gives you the evidence for where the conflict begins.
