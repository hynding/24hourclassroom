## What you need to be able to do

- **7.D** Choose evidence that is relevant (it bears on the specific claim) and sufficient (enough of it, from enough of the work, to carry the claim). In a five-act play this means choosing moments, not piling up quotations.
- **7.C** Write commentary that explains how each piece of evidence advances the line of reasoning and supports the thesis. Commentary is where the essay's points are actually made.

In plain terms: you have read the whole play. This week is about turning it into an argument.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Line of reasoning | The sequence of claims that leads from the thesis through the body of an essay. |
| Commentary | Explanation of what the evidence shows and why it matters to the claim; the "so what?" |
| Relevant evidence | Evidence that bears on the specific terms of the claim, not just the topic. |
| Sufficient evidence | Enough evidence, from enough of the work, to support the scope of the claim. |
| Clown (Shakespearean) | A rustic comic character, like the gravediggers; not a circus clown. |
| Memento mori | "Remember you must die": an image, often a skull, meant to make the viewer contemplate death. |
| Providence | The belief that God governs events, down to "the fall of a sparrow." |
| Catastrophe | In tragedy, the final turn that brings about the deaths. |

## The text in context

**5.1, the graveyard.** Two gravediggers joke in prose about whether a drowned woman who may have killed herself should have Christian burial. Hamlet and Horatio, newly returned, watch the First Gravedigger toss up skulls. From his talk we learn that he became sexton the day "young Hamlet was born" and has been at it "thirty years": Hamlet is about thirty. He hands Hamlet Yorick's skull. Then a funeral procession arrives with "maimed rites." It is Ophelia's. Laertes leaps into the grave; Hamlet leaps in after him, declaring himself "Hamlet the Dane," and they grapple.

**5.2, the catastrophe.** Hamlet tells Horatio how, sleepless at sea, he found the King's commission ordering his death and replaced it with one ordering the deaths of its bearers, Rosencrantz and Guildenstern. He has come to believe that "There’s a divinity that shapes our ends, / Rough-hew them how we will." Osric brings the King's wager on a fencing match. Hamlet has misgivings but sets them aside: "the readiness is all." In the duel the Queen drinks from the poisoned cup; Laertes wounds Hamlet with the poisoned blade; in a scuffle they exchange rapiers and Hamlet wounds Laertes. The Queen dies, crying "The drink, the drink!—I am poison’d." Laertes confesses, "the king, the king’s to blame." Hamlet stabs Claudius with the envenomed point and forces the cup on him. Laertes and Hamlet exchange forgiveness. Hamlet stops Horatio from drinking the rest so that he will live to "tell my story," gives his "dying voice" to Fortinbras, and dies: "the rest is silence." Fortinbras arrives, hears that Rosencrantz and Guildenstern are dead, and orders a soldier's funeral.

## Close reading

### 1. The skull (5.1.190-202)

> Alas, poor Yorick!—I knew him, Horatio; a fellow of infinite jest, of most excellent fancy: he hath borne me on his back a thousand times; and now, how abhorred in my imagination it is! my gorge rises at it. [...] Now, get you to my lady’s chamber, and tell her, let her paint an inch thick, to this favour she must come;

**Observe.** The speech moves from memory, to physical disgust, to satire, and then to Alexander and Caesar.

**Interpret.** Death has stopped being an idea. In 3.1 it was "a sleep" and "The undiscover’d country"; here it is a jawless skull, and Alexander "smelt so."

**Function.** The skull is a memento mori and the last of the play's images of disguise: no paint survives "this favour." For an essay, this passage is evidence of change. It answers the abstract death of "To be, or not to be" with a concrete one.

### 2. Providence and readiness (5.2)

> There’s a divinity that shapes our ends,
> Rough-hew them how we will.
> (5.2.11-12)

> there’s a special providence in the fall of a sparrow. If it be now, ’tis not to come; if it be not to come, it will be now; if it be not now, yet it will come: the readiness is all.
> (5.2.233-237)

**Observe.** The first is verse, the second prose; both are calm. The sparrow alludes to the Gospel (Matthew 10:29). The second passage turns on itself three times and arrives at four words.

**Interpret.** Hamlet has stopped planning. He credits his escape to "rashness" and to a power that finishes what men only rough out.

**Function.** These are the two best pieces of evidence for any claim about Hamlet's change (1.B). Be careful with the commentary: whether the change is wisdom, resignation or fatalism is the interpretive question. A strong essay names it and takes a side.

### 3. The rebound (5.2)

> I am justly kill’d with mine own treachery.
> (5.2.337)

**Observe.** Laertes is wounded by the blade he poisoned; the Queen dies of the cup Claudius prepared; Claudius dies of both.

**Interpret.** Every hidden weapon turns on its maker, as Polonius died behind his own arras.

**Function.** This is the play's pattern of justice. No character plans it, and it completes the revenge Hamlet could not plan either.

## From reading to writing: building a Q3 paragraph

**Prompt:** Many works feature a character whose hesitation before a decisive act reveals as much as the act itself. Analyze how the hesitation contributes to an interpretation of the work as a whole.

**Step 1: choose evidence for sufficiency (7.D).** A claim about the whole play needs a beginning, a turning point and an end. For example:

- Act I: "O cursed spite, / That ever I was born to set it right."
- Act III: sparing Claudius at prayer, then killing Polonius on impulse.
- Act V: "the readiness is all."

Three moments, one from each phase, beat ten quotations from one soliloquy.

**Step 2: write commentary that connects (7.C).** Here is one body paragraph built from the Act III evidence:

> The sequence at the centre of the play shows that Hamlet's hesitation is not a failure of will but a refusal to act without understanding, and that the alternative is worse. When he finds Claudius at prayer, he has the proof the Mousetrap gave him and a drawn sword, yet he stops, reasoning that a man killed while praying goes to heaven and that this would be "hire and salary, not revenge." The hesitation is chilling (he wants Claudius damned, not merely dead), but it is also thought at work. Minutes later, with no time to think, he stabs through the arras at a voice and kills Polonius. Set side by side, the scenes show what Hamlet's thinking has been protecting him from: impulsive action finds the wrong victim and makes Hamlet himself the killer of a father, the very crime he set out to avenge. The play's tragedy is that neither path works. Deliberation misses its moment and impulse creates a new revenge plot in Laertes, so Hamlet's hesitation becomes Shakespeare's way of showing that in a corrupt world thought and action cannot be made to agree.

**Annotation.**

- *First sentence:* a claim about function, not a plot point; it advances the thesis.
- *Evidence:* specific and accurate ("hire and salary, not revenge"; the arras; Polonius), chosen because it contains both kinds of action.
- *Commentary:* "The hesitation is chilling ... but it is also thought at work" interprets the evidence; "Set side by side ... show" explains the relationship between two pieces of evidence, which is the heart of 7.C.
- *Last sentence:* returns to the work as a whole, which is what Q3 asks for.

**A weak version:** "Hamlet hesitates a lot in the play. In Act III he could kill Claudius but he doesn't because Claudius is praying. Then he kills Polonius. This shows that Hamlet hesitates. Hesitation is important in the play." The evidence is relevant, but the commentary only restates it, so there is no line of reasoning.

## Habits that sink essays on Hamlet

- **Plot summary in place of commentary.** Graders know the play; tell them what the evidence means.
- **Quotation without explanation.** Every quotation needs at least one sentence that explains how it supports the claim.
- **Evidence from one scene for a claim about the whole play.** Spread it across the acts.
- **"Hamlet is indecisive" as a thesis.** Too general to argue. Say what the indecision means or does.
- **Mixing up who does what.** Laertes poisons the blade; Claudius poisons the cup; Gertrude is not the target. Hamlet never reaches England.
- **Over-quoting famous lines.** "To be, or not to be" is not automatically your best evidence. Choose what fits the claim.
