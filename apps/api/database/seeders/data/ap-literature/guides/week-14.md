## What you need to be able to do

- **5.A** Tell the literal meaning of a word or phrase from its figurative meaning, and notice when a poem keeps both alive at once ("mocked", "light", "Talent").
- **6.D** Recognise an allusion, say what it refers to, and, above all, explain what the reference does in this poem: what story it imports and how the poem uses, bends or reverses that story.
- **Form, as the means of both.** Name a sonnet's type from its rhyme scheme, scan a line of iambic pentameter, and find the volta, then explain why the poem turns where it does.

In plain language: by Friday you should be able to pick up any sonnet, mark its rhymes, find its turn, and write two sentences about how one allusion or one double-meaning word changes what the poem says.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sonnet | A fourteen-line poem, in English usually iambic pentameter, with a set rhyme scheme and a turn. |
| Iamb | A foot of two syllables, unstressed then stressed: a-BOUT, the SAND. |
| Iambic pentameter | Five iambs per line, ten syllables, five rising beats; the standard line of the English sonnet, of Shakespeare's plays and of blank verse. |
| Trochee, spondee | Stressed-unstressed (GET-ting); stressed-stressed (GREAT GOD). Variations that put weight on particular words. |
| Scansion | Marking stressed and unstressed syllables to find a line's meter and its variations. |
| Petrarchan (Italian) sonnet | Octave ABBAABBA + sestet CDECDE or CDCDCD; problem, then response. |
| Shakespearean (English) sonnet | Three quatrains + couplet, ABAB CDCD EFEF GG; development, then summing up or twist. |
| Octave, sestet, quatrain, couplet | Groups of eight, six, four and two lines. |
| Volta | The turn: the place where a sonnet's thought shifts. |
| Caesura, enjambment | A pause within a line; a sentence running on past a line break. |
| Allusion | An unexplained reference to something outside the text: myth, scripture, history, literature. |
| Literal / figurative | What words say on the surface; what they mean by comparison, extension or implication. |

## The sonnet as a machine for thinking

A sonnet is small enough to hold in your head and strict enough that every departure from the rules is visible. That is why the AP exam loves it: a poet who places a turn early, breaks the rhyme scheme, or runs a sentence across the octave and sestet is making a choice you can point to.

**Meter.** English is a stress language, and iambic pentameter is its most natural verse line. Scan by saying the line aloud and marking the syllables you lean on. "I MET | a TRAV | 'ller FROM | an AN | tique LAND" is regular (the poem's "traveller" is spoken as two syllables). Regularity is the background; meaning lives in the variations. Wordsworth's second line begins "GET-ting and SPEND-ing": the first foot is a trochee, and the line lurches into motion the way the commerce it describes does. When you scan, you are not hunting for errors; you are looking for the words the rhythm spotlights.

**Two patterns.** The *Petrarchan* sonnet, named for the fourteenth-century Italian poet Petrarch, divides into an octave rhyming ABBAABBA and a sestet with two or three new rhymes (CDECDE, CDCDCD). Only four or five rhyme sounds in fourteen lines is hard to do in English, which has fewer rhymes than Italian, and the form's two blocks invite a two-part argument: question and answer, problem and resolution, description and reflection. The *Shakespearean* sonnet, which English poets developed in the sixteenth century, uses seven rhyme sounds in three quatrains and a closing couplet. Its rhythm is cumulative: three tries at an idea, then a two-line verdict, which can resolve the poem or undercut it (week 6's Sonnet 130 used the couplet to overturn twelve lines of mock insults).

**The volta.** Whichever pattern a poet uses, look for the turn. Petrarchan sonnets usually turn at line 9; Shakespearean sonnets at line 9, at the couplet, or both. Signals include *but*, *yet*, *then*, a question followed by an answer, a change of speaker, tense, addressee or tone. This week every sonnet does something interesting with its turn: Milton starts his answer early, Wordsworth turns in the middle of a line, McKay's "Yet" arrives a line early, and Shelley's poem hardly fits either pattern at all.

## The texts in context

**Shelley, "Ozymandias" (1818).** Published in the London weekly *The Examiner* in January 1818, written in a friendly contest with the poet Horace Smith, who wrote a sonnet on the same subject. Ozymandias is the Greek form of a throne name of Ramesses II, the thirteenth-century BCE pharaoh, and the ancient historian Diodorus Siculus records a boastful inscription on one of his statues. Shelley, a radical who loathed tyranny, writes a sonnet whose rhyme scheme (ABABACDCEDEFEF) interlocks and will not stay in its blocks.

**Wordsworth, "The world is too much with us" (1807).** From *Poems, in Two Volumes*. A strict Petrarchan sonnet (ABBAABBA CDCDCD) by the poet who, with Coleridge, had argued in *Lyrical Ballads* for poetry rooted in feeling and in nature. Its complaint about "Getting and spending" was written as Britain's commercial and industrial economy expanded.

**Milton, "When I consider how my light is spent" (1673).** Milton was blind by 1652, in his early forties, after years of work for the Commonwealth government; *Paradise Lost* was still ahead of him. The sonnet, usually numbered 19, appeared in his *Poems, &c. upon Several Occasions* (1673). It is a Petrarchan sonnet (ABBAABBA CDECDE) built around a parable from Matthew's Gospel.

**McKay, "America" (1921).** Claude McKay, born in Jamaica, came to the United States in 1912 and became one of the first major voices of the Harlem Renaissance. "America" appeared in *The Liberator* in December 1921 and in *Harlem Shadows* (1922). It is a Shakespearean sonnet: a Black poet taking the most English of forms to speak about a nation that wounded him. (Unit 8 will read his "If We Must Die".)

## Close reading

**1. A command that changes meaning (5.A, irony).**

> "My name is Ozymandias, king of kings:
> Look on my works, ye Mighty, and despair!"
> Nothing beside remains.

*Observe:* the inscription is all title and imperative, ending in an exclamation; the reply is three words and a full stop in the middle of a line. *Interpret:* the words were carved to tell rival kings to despair of ever matching him. Read now, among sand, they tell all the mighty to despair because their own works will end like this. *Function:* nothing in the inscription has changed except its setting; time has turned literal boasting into figurative warning. The allusive title "king of kings", which echoes the Bible's name for God, raises the claim so that the fall is greater.

**2. Allusion as a wished-for way of seeing (6.D).**

> Great God! I'd rather be
> A Pagan suckled in a creed outworn;
> So might I, standing on this pleasant lea,
> Have glimpses that would make me less forlorn;
> Have sight of Proteus rising from the sea;
> Or hear old Triton blow his wreathèd horn.

*Observe:* the sestet is a wish ("I'd rather be... So might I") built on two Greek sea gods: Proteus, the shape-changing prophet of the sea, and Triton, who blows a conch shell to rouse or calm the waves. The speaker calls their religion "a creed outworn". *Interpret:* he does not believe in them; he envies a way of seeing the sea as alive with presence. *Function:* the allusions measure the loss diagnosed in the octave. A modern Christian ("Great God!") would rather be a pagan than numb, which is a shocking thing for an 1807 poet to say, and the shock is the point.

**3. A parable and a pun (5.A and 6.D together).**

> When I consider how my light is spent,
> Ere half my days, in this dark world and wide,
> And that one Talent which is death to hide
> Lodged with me useless

*Observe:* "light" is eyesight and the daylight of a working life; "spent" is the language of money; "Talent" is capitalised. *Interpret:* in the parable of the talents (Matthew 25:14-30) a master entrusts sums of money to servants and punishes the one who buried his. Milton's talent is his gift as a poet, buried by blindness. *Function:* the allusion supplies a master who will return, an account to render and a punishment to fear, so private grief becomes a question of judgement. The sestet then changes the parable's economy: "God doth not need / Either man's work or his own gifts". The master of this poem is a king with "Thousands" of servants; some run, and "They also serve who only stand and wait."

Notice too the form: patience's reply begins "But patience, to prevent / That murmur" in line 8, before the octave has finished. The answer interrupts the complaint, and the sentence runs across the octave-sestet boundary.

**4. A simile that stands up (5.A, with 6.A from week 6).**

> Yet, as a rebel fronts a king in state,
> I stand within her walls with not a shred
> Of terror, malice, not a word of jeer.

*Observe:* McKay's "Yet" comes at line 8, the last line of the second quatrain, not at line 9. America, a republic, is likened to "a king in state", enthroned; the speaker to a rebel who "fronts" (faces) him. *Interpret:* the stance is open and unafraid, and the speaker refuses both fear and spite. *Function:* the simile converts the first quatrain's injuries ("bread of bitterness", "tiger's tooth") into posture: the vigour America pours into him becomes "strength erect against her hate", and he stands upright inside her walls. The couplet ("Like priceless treasures sinking in the sand") then echoes Shelley's desert: a mighty power's "granite wonders" will go the way of Ozymandias.

## From reading to writing

The Q1 poetry essay asks you to analyse how a poet uses literary elements and techniques to convey something specific (an attitude, a complex relationship, a shift). Allusion and double meaning are excellent evidence because they compress so much. Here is a model body paragraph on Milton.

> Milton turns his blindness into a crisis of conscience by borrowing the parable of the talents. Light "spent" and a talent "Lodged with me useless" speak the language of money, and the parable supplies the rest: a master who will return, a "true account" to present, and a servant punished for burying what he was given. The speaker's fear is therefore not only that he cannot write but that he will be judged for it, which is why his question, "Doth God exact day-labour, light denied?", feels both reasonable and, as he admits, "fond", that is, foolish. Patience answers by changing the economy of the parable: a God who "doth not need / Either man's work or his own gifts" is not a master collecting interest but a king served by thousands, and service can consist of readiness alone.

*Annotations.* The claim (sentence 1) is interpretive and arguable. The evidence is short, embedded quotation. The commentary explains what the allusion brings in (master, account, punishment) and how the sestet revises it, which is the 6.D move. The gloss on "fondly" is a 5.A move used in support of the argument, not as a vocabulary note.

A **weak version** says: "Milton uses an allusion to the Bible. He talks about the parable of the talents. This shows he is worried. He also uses imagery of light and dark." Every sentence is true, and none explains anything. Naming a device is identification; the points in Row B come from explaining its function.

## Common misreadings

- **"The sonnet is about Ozymandias."** It is about what happens to power, pride and art over time; Ozymandias is the example. The sculptor's art outlasts the king, and the poem outlasts both.
- **Calling every fourteen-line poem Petrarchan or Shakespearean without checking.** Letter the rhymes first. "Ozymandias" fits neither pattern exactly.
- **Assuming the volta is always at line 9.** Milton turns in line 8, Wordsworth mid-line 9, McKay at line 8.
- **Reading old words in their modern sense.** "Fondly" means foolishly; "post" means travel fast; "lea" is a meadow; "fronts" means faces.
- **Treating an allusion as decoration.** If you can delete the allusion without changing the argument, you haven't explained it yet.
- **Reading Wordsworth as a pagan.** He says he would *rather* be one; the hypothetical expresses the depth of the loss, not a conversion.
- **Reading "America" as a hate poem or a love poem.** It is both, and the essay that explains how they coexist earns the points.
