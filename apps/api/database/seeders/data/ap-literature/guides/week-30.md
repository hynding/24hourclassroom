# Week 30 · Conflict and Change, The Great Gatsby Chapters 7-9

**CED skills:** 3.F Explain the function of conflict in a text · 1.B Explain the function of a character changing or remaining unchanged
**Big Ideas:** Structure (STR), Character (CHR)
**Read:** F. Scott Fitzgerald, *The Great Gatsby* (1925), chapters VII-IX ([Project Gutenberg #64317](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317))
**Spiral:** Nick's perspective (week 28), significant events (week 29), reliability (weeks 18 and 22), setting (week 11), metaphor (weeks 6, 16), commentary and evidence (weeks 9 and 21).

## What you need to be able to do

- **3.F** Name the conflicts in a text, including the ones that are not fights, and explain what each one does: what it reveals about the characters, what it forces them to choose, and what meaning it produces.
- **1.B** Decide whether a character changes, and explain the function of that change or that constancy. A static character is not a weaker character; Gatsby's refusal to change is the novel's subject.

Chapters 7-9 are where every conflict the novel has built comes to a crisis on a single hot afternoon and evening, and then the book spends two chapters on consequences. Which characters are altered by those consequences, and which are not, is how Fitzgerald tells us what he thinks of them.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| External conflict | A struggle between a character and an outside force: another person, a society, nature. |
| Internal conflict | A struggle within a character between desires, loyalties or values. |
| Climax | The point of greatest tension, where the main conflict turns; the Plaza Hotel scene. |
| Dynamic character | One who changes in a significant way (Nick). |
| Static character | One who does not (Tom, Daisy and, in his own way, Gatsby). |
| Foil | A character whose contrast sharpens another's (Tom and Gatsby; Nick and Gatsby). |
| Dénouement | The unwinding after the climax: the funeral, the farewells, the return West. |
| Universalising ending | A close that turns a particular story into a statement about everyone ("So we beat on"). |

## The text in context

Chapter 7 takes place on the hottest day of the summer. Gatsby has stopped his parties and replaced his servants so gossip will not reach Daisy. At the Buchanans' lunch, Daisy's daughter appears, Daisy all but declares her love in front of Tom, and Gatsby says of her, "Her voice is full of money." The group drives into New York, Tom at the wheel of Gatsby's yellow car, with a stop at Wilson's garage, where Myrtle watches from a window. In a suite at the Plaza Hotel the conflict breaks open: Gatsby demands that Daisy say she never loved Tom; she cannot ("I did love him once—but I loved you too"); Tom exposes Gatsby's bootlegging drugstores; Daisy shrinks back toward Tom. On the drive home, with Daisy at the wheel of Gatsby's car, Myrtle runs into the road and is killed. Nick, who has realised it is his thirtieth birthday, leaves Gatsby standing guard outside the Buchanans' house.

In chapter 8 Gatsby tells Nick the true story of 1917; Nick shouts across the lawn that Gatsby is worth "the whole damn bunch put together"; George Wilson, told by Tom whose car it was, shoots Gatsby in his pool and then himself. Chapter 9 is the aftermath: an almost empty funeral, Gatsby's father with a boyhood schedule in a copy of *Hopalong Cassidy*, Nick's break with Jordan, a last meeting with Tom, and the final page on Gatsby's beach.

## Close reading

### 1. The Plaza: an external conflict decided internally

> "Oh, you want too much!" she cried to Gatsby. "I love you now—isn't that enough? I can't help what's past." [...] "I did love him once—but I loved you too."

**Observe.** Daisy's sentences are exclamations and pleas. "Now" and "past" are set against each other. The word "once" concedes what Gatsby needs her to deny.

**Interpret.** Gatsby's demand is not "leave Tom" but "say you never loved him": erase five years. Daisy can give him the present, not the past.

**Function (3.F).** The external conflict (Tom against Gatsby) is decided by an internal one (Daisy's divided loyalty) and by Gatsby's conflict with time. The scene shows that Gatsby's real opponent was never Tom.

### 2. Tom's speech: the conflict is also about class

> "I suppose the latest thing is to sit back and let Mr. Nobody from Nowhere make love to your wife. Well, if that's the idea you can count me out.... Nowadays people begin by sneering at family life and family institutions [...]"

**Observe.** "Nobody from Nowhere" attacks origins, not conduct. Tom shifts to grand claims about "family institutions," then (in the full speech) to racial anxiety.

**Interpret.** A man with a mistress poses as defender of the family. The romantic rivalry is a class war: the old order closing ranks against an intruder.

**Function.** The speech widens the conflict and indicts the winner. Tom wins Daisy, and the novel makes sure we see what kind of victory it is.

### 3. "Careless people": the static characters

> They were careless people, Tom and Daisy—they smashed up things and creatures and then retreated back into their money or their vast carelessness, or whatever it was that kept them together, and let other people clean up the mess they had made [...]

**Observe.** "Retreated back" (a return to where they started), "things and creatures" (victims reduced), "other people clean up" (cost shifted onto others).

**Interpret.** The Buchanans end exactly as they began, shielded by money. Nothing that has happened touches them.

**Function (1.B).** Their unchangedness is an accusation. The novel measures them against Gatsby, who dies for his constancy, and Nick, who is changed.

### 4. The last page: the conflict made universal

> Gatsby believed in the green light, the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us. [...] So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past.

**Observe.** "Gatsby" becomes "us" and "we." The present tense arrives. The last image sets effort ("beat on") against force ("current") and result ("borne back").

**Interpret.** Gatsby's struggle against time is everyone's: we row toward a future that recedes and are carried back toward what we were.

**Function.** The conflict is resolved for Gatsby by death and transformed for everyone else into a condition of life. The ending also places Nick inside what he has judged: "we" includes him.

## From reading to writing

This week's model is a literary-argument paragraph (Q3 style), the kind you will write in week 32.

**Prompt.** Choose a character who refuses to change and explain how that refusal contributes to the meaning of the work.

**Model paragraph.**

> In *The Great Gatsby*, Gatsby's refusal to change after the Plaza Hotel shows that the quality Nick most admires in him, his "gift for hope," is the same quality that destroys him. **[Claim: names the character, the refusal and its double meaning.]** Even after Daisy admits "I did love him once—but I loved you too," and even after she has killed Myrtle with his car, Gatsby spends the night outside the Buchanans' house in case she needs him, while inside she sits with Tom at the kitchen table. **[Evidence: a precise moment after the turning point.]** The vigil repeats, in miniature, the five years Gatsby has spent looking at the green light: he still guards a Daisy who exists mainly in his imagination, and the real Daisy has already chosen. **[Commentary: links the moment to the novel's pattern.]** Nick had said in chapter 6 that Gatsby was faithful to his "Platonic conception of himself" "to the end," and the vigil proves it, but the same fidelity sends him to the pool to wait for a call that never comes, where Wilson finds him. **[Commentary: shows constancy as both virtue and fatal flaw.]** Gatsby's unchangedness therefore lets Fitzgerald honour hope and expose its cost in a single character. **[Link back to the claim and to meaning.]**

**What a weaker version looks like.** "Gatsby never changes. He always loves Daisy. He waits outside her house and then he gets shot. This shows that you should not live in the past." The claim is a moral, not an interpretation; the evidence is summary; there is no explanation of *how* the refusal works or why it matters.

## Common misreadings

- **"Gatsby changes when he learns the truth about Daisy."** He never accepts it: he waits outside her house and by the pool for her call.
- **"Daisy is the villain."** She makes the fatal mistake and the cowardly choice, but Nick's verdict falls on "Tom and Daisy" together and on the world that made them.
- **"Nick ends as neutral as he began."** He judges plainly at the end ("careless people," "rotten crowd") and leaves the East; he is the dynamic character.
- **"The ending is purely hopeful."** The future "recedes," and we are "borne back." Hope and futility are in the same sentence.
- **"Tom feels guilty."** He tells Nick he was justified; his only tears are for Myrtle.

## Connections and looking ahead

- **Earlier in the course.** Victor Frankenstein (week 10) is a character whose refusal to change, to the last pursuit across the ice, both defines and destroys him; Walton, the frame narrator, is the one who changes, turning his ship south. The structure is close to *Gatsby*'s, and the pairing makes an excellent Q3 comparison. Hamlet's change in Act V (week 21), his readiness, offers a contrast: a character who does change, and dies anyway.
- **This unit.** Weeks 31-32 turn to *A Doll's House*, where the central character changes more completely than anyone in *Gatsby*, and her husband does not. The 1.B and 3.F work of this week transfers directly.
- **The exam.** Q3 prompts often ask about conflict ("a character torn between...") or change ("a character's transformation"). *Gatsby* gives you three static characters, one dynamic narrator and a climax built of nested conflicts: enough for many prompts.
