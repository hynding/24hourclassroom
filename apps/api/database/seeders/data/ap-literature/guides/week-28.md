**CED skills:** 4.C Identify and describe details, diction or syntax that reveal a narrator's perspective · 1.E Explain how a character's own choices, actions and speech reveal complexities, and the function of those complexities
**Big Ideas:** Narration (NAR), Character (CHR)

**Spiral:** first-person and retrospective narration (week 1), reliability (week 18, *Hamlet*; week 22, "The Tell-Tale Heart"), setting as moral landscape (week 11), symbol (weeks 19 and 24), and the thesis (week 7) all come back this week.

## What you need to be able to do

- **4.C** Point to specific words, details and sentence shapes in Nick's narration and say what attitude they reveal: not just "Nick admires Gatsby" but which phrase, which qualification, which correction shows it.
- **1.E** Explain what a character's own choices and speech reveal that a summary would miss, and why that complexity matters to the novel. Daisy's confession, Tom's violence, Jordan's lie and Gatsby's distance from his own party are this week's evidence.

In plain terms: this is the week to stop treating Nick as a camera. He is a person with a history, a temperament and a stake in the story, and the first three chapters are as much about how he sees as about what he sees.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Perspective | A narrator's or character's way of seeing and valuing: attitudes, assumptions, sympathies. Built from diction, syntax and selection. |
| Retrospective narration | Telling a story after it has ended. The narrating "I" knows more than the "I" who lived the events. |
| Participant narrator | A first-person narrator who takes part in the action (Nick) rather than only observing it. |
| Qualification | A clause or sentence that limits or corrects a claim ("it has a limit"). Nick's signature move. |
| Diction | Word choice, including connotation and register ("unaffected scorn," "foul dust"). |
| Syntax | Sentence structure: length, order, interruption, parallelism, dashes. |
| Characterisation through action | Revealing character by what someone does and says rather than by the narrator's description. |
| Performance | Speech or behaviour designed for an audience; in *Gatsby*, nearly everyone performs. |
| Old money / new money | Inherited wealth (East Egg, the Buchanans) versus recently made wealth (West Egg, Gatsby). |

## The text in context

F. Scott Fitzgerald (1896-1940) published *The Great Gatsby* with Charles Scribner's Sons in April 1925. He was already famous for *This Side of Paradise* (1920) and for stories that named the Jazz Age. *Gatsby* sold modestly in his lifetime and became a classic only after his death. It entered the public domain in the United States on 1 January 2021, which is why we can quote it freely in this course.

The novel is set in the summer of 1922, among the new suburbs of Long Island and the speakeasies and hotels of Manhattan, during Prohibition and a stock-market boom. Fitzgerald invents two peninsulas, West Egg and East Egg, facing each other across a bay: the first for new fortunes and the second for inherited ones. Between Long Island and the city lies the valley of ashes, the dumping ground the commuter trains and cars must cross.

For AP purposes, *Gatsby* is ideal for the literary argument essay (Q3): it is short, its narrator is a ready-made subject for questions about perspective, and its characters make choices whose motives the reader has to infer.

## Close reading

### 1. The narrator's second paragraph

> And, after boasting this way of my tolerance, I come to the admission that it has a limit.

**Observe.** Two words judge Nick's own preceding paragraph: "boasting" and "admission." The first paragraph presented him as someone "inclined to reserve all judgements"; this one says that was a boast.

**Interpret.** Nick wants credit for tolerance and honesty at once, so he confesses the limit of his tolerance. The confession is itself a bid for trust.

**Function.** From the second paragraph, the reader is told to read Nick's judgments as judgments. That is what 4.C asks you to do: describe a perspective, including the parts the narrator does not fully see.

### 2. Scorn and the gorgeous

> Only Gatsby, the man who gives his name to this book, was exempt from my reaction—Gatsby, who represented everything for which I have an unaffected scorn. If personality is an unbroken series of successful gestures, then there was something gorgeous about him [...]

**Observe.** "Only ... exempt" isolates Gatsby. The dash repeats his name and then adds a damning clause. The next sentence turns on a conditional ("If personality is ... then").

**Interpret.** Nick separates what Gatsby *represented* (the new money, the display, the vulgarity he scorns) from what Gatsby *was* (a "gift for hope"). The conditional matters: "something gorgeous" is true only if personality is "an unbroken series of successful gestures." Nick's praise contains a doubt about whether gestures add up to a self.

**Function.** The sentence builds the ambivalence the whole novel lives on. Ask of any later scene: is Nick admiring the man, scorning what he represents, or doing both?

### 3. Daisy's "beautiful little fool"

> 'All right,' I said, 'I'm glad it's a girl. And I hope she'll be a fool—that's the best thing a girl can be in this world, a beautiful little fool.'

**Observe.** Daisy quotes herself, so the line is a story she tells about a story. Before it come "utterly abandoned" and "Tom was God knows where." After it, she claims she has "seen everything" and exclaims, "Sophisticated—God, I'm sophisticated!"

**Interpret.** Her speech holds two things at once: a real recognition that her world rewards women for beauty and not-knowing, and a performance of world-weariness. Nick feels "the basic insincerity of what she had said" and then sees "an absolute smirk." But insincerity in the delivery does not make the grievance false.

**Function (1.E).** Daisy's choices of words make her complex: she understands the trap and chooses to perform inside it. That combination prepares us for her final choice in chapter 7, when she stays with Tom.

### 4. The smile

> It understood you just so far as you wanted to be understood, believed in you as you would like to believe in yourself, and assured you that it had precisely the impression of you that, at your best, you hoped to convey. Precisely at that point it vanished—and I was looking at an elegant young roughneck [...]

**Observe.** Three parallel clauses, each turning back to "you." Then "Precisely at that point" (echoing the "precisely" before it) and a dash into "an elegant young roughneck."

**Interpret.** The smile flatters by mirroring. The parallelism builds the spell; the dash breaks it. "Elegant" and "roughneck" clash, just as Gatsby's "elaborate formality of speech" "just missed being absurd."

**Function.** Nick's syntax enacts his experience of Gatsby: enchantment, then a cut to scrutiny. That rhythm, charm and then a check, runs through every meeting between them.

## From reading to writing

**Prompt.** How does Fitzgerald use Nick's narration in chapter 1 to shape the reader's attitude to Gatsby before Gatsby appears?

**Model paragraph.**

> Fitzgerald lets Nick introduce Gatsby through a sentence that refuses to settle, so the reader learns to hold admiration and suspicion together before Gatsby speaks a word. **[Claim: names the technique and its effect.]** Nick says Gatsby "represented everything for which I have an unaffected scorn," yet in the next breath finds "something gorgeous about him." **[Evidence: two quotations set side by side.]** The word "represented" is the hinge: Nick's scorn is for what Gatsby stands for, the display of new money, while his admiration is for "an extraordinary gift for hope," a quality of the man himself. **[Commentary: explains the precise wording, not just the meaning.]** Even the praise is conditional, since the "gorgeous" quality exists only "if personality is an unbroken series of successful gestures," a definition that hints Gatsby may be a sequence of poses rather than a self. **[Commentary goes further: finds the doubt inside the praise.]** By the time Gatsby appears in chapter 3, the reader is ready to be charmed by his smile and to notice, with Nick, the "roughneck" behind it. **[Link to the line of reasoning: connects the technique to the later scene.]**

**What a weaker version looks like.** "Nick likes Gatsby but also doesn't like him. He says he has scorn for him but then says he is gorgeous. This shows that Nick has mixed feelings." The claim is true but obvious; the quotations are paraphrased rather than analysed; nothing explains *how* the language creates the effect. The model earns its points by explaining "represented" and the conditional "if."

## Common misreadings

- **"Nick is objective because he says he reserves judgment."** He says so and then admits his tolerance "has a limit." Treat his self-description as a claim to test.
- **"Nick hates the rich."** He scorns certain things and is drawn to the people who embody them: Daisy, Jordan, Gatsby himself.
- **"Daisy is just shallow."** Her speech shows insight and pain as well as performance. Shallow characters do not diagnose their own position.
- **"The valley of ashes is just a description of scenery."** Setting does work here: it is the waste the glamour produces, and the Wilsons live in it.
- **"Gatsby's parties show he loves fun."** He stands apart, does not drink, and watches. The parties are a means to something; chapter 4 begins to say what.
