# Week 03 · Sequence and Argument in "The Necklace"

**CED skills:** 3.B Explain the function of a particular sequence of events in a plot · 7.A Develop a paragraph that includes a claim that requires defense with evidence from the text, and the evidence itself
**Big Ideas:** Structure (STR), Literary Argumentation (LAN); in review, Character (CHR), Setting (SET), Narration (NAR)
**Read:** Guy de Maupassant, "The Necklace" ("La Parure," 1884), in the early English translation titled "The Diamond Necklace," [Project Gutenberg #3080](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3080) (*Original Short Stories*, Volume IV)
**Spiral:** Week 1's narrator and character skills and week 2's setting and plot order all return. "The Necklace" is a third story built on a gift or ornament and a final revelation, so the unit closes by comparing how three writers order events to reach their endings.

## What you need to be able to do

- **3.B** Explain why an event comes where it does: what a sequence of events builds, contrasts, withholds or reverses.
- **7.A** Write a paragraph with a claim that genuinely needs defending and evidence that proves it, followed by commentary.
- **Review 1.A, 2.A, 3.A, 4.A, 4.B** Read Mathilde through details, read her home as a setting seen through her eyes, describe the narrator and his judgments, and describe how the plot orders and paces events.

In plain language: be able to explain why the ball must come right before the loss, why we learn the truth about the diamonds only in the last line, and how to turn that explanation into a paragraph that argues rather than retells.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sequence | The order in which events are presented. Skill 3.B asks for its function. |
| Causal chain | A series of events in which each causes the next. |
| Reversal | A turn of fortune to its opposite (Greek *peripeteia*). |
| Juxtaposition | Placing two things side by side so that each sharpens the other. |
| Withheld information | A fact delayed or concealed to shape the reader's experience. |
| Twist ending | A last-moment revelation that changes the meaning of what came before. |
| Situational irony | A gap between what is expected and what happens. |
| Claim | An arguable interpretation that the text can prove. |
| Evidence | A short quotation or precise reference that supports a claim. |
| Commentary | Explanation of how the evidence supports the claim. |
| Line of reasoning | The logical path that connects claim, evidence and commentary across a paragraph or essay. |

## The text in context

Guy de Maupassant (1850-1893) was the great French master of the short story, a protégé of Gustave Flaubert who published around three hundred stories in barely more than a decade, many first in Paris newspapers. "La Parure" appeared in the newspaper *Le Gaulois* in February 1884. Its world is the Paris of small government clerks, people respectable but not rich, living on fixed salaries in a society that measured worth by appearance. Maupassant's narrators observe with a cool, exact eye and tend to let events, rather than commentary, deliver the judgment.

The story has been translated into English many times. Because later translations are still under copyright, this course quotes an early translation that Project Gutenberg reproduces from a multi-volume collection of Maupassant's stories, where the title is "The Diamond Necklace." The story is famous for its final line; read to the end before looking anything up.

## Close reading: how the sequence works

### 1. Opening with desire, not action

> The girl was one of those pretty and charming young creatures who sometimes are born, as if by a slip of fate, into a family of clerks. She had no dowry, no expectations, no way of being known, understood, loved, married by any rich and distinguished man; so she let herself be married to a little clerk of the Ministry of Public Instruction.

**Observe.** No event happens in this paragraph. We get a type ("one of those pretty and charming young creatures"), a mistake of "fate," a list of what she lacks, and a passive marriage: she "let herself be married." Her husband is "a little clerk."

**Interpret.** The paragraph presents Mathilde's own view of her life: she was meant for better and fate misplaced her. "Let herself be married" suggests resignation without contentment.

**Function (3.B).** Beginning with desire rather than action makes the whole plot a test of that desire. Every later event (the invitation, the dress, the jewels, the loss, the decision to conceal it) follows from the hunger set out here. The phrase "a slip of fate" also announces the theme of chance that the ending will turn upside down.

### 2. A narrator who judges in a single clause

> Mathilde suffered ceaselessly, feeling herself born to enjoy all delicacies and all luxuries. She was distressed at the poverty of her dwelling, at the bareness of the walls, at the shabby chairs, the ugliness of the curtains. All those things, of which another woman of her rank would never even have been conscious, tortured her and made her angry.

**Observe.** The first two sentences stay close to her feelings: "suffered ceaselessly," "distressed." Then a clause steps outside: "another woman of her rank would never even have been conscious" of these things.

**Interpret.** The narrator lets us feel her misery and then measures it. Her home is ordinary; her suffering is out of proportion to it.

**Function.** This small judgment (review 4.B) creates ironic distance. We understand Mathilde but do not simply share her view, which prepares us for a plot in which her way of seeing things costs her far more than her circumstances ever did.

### 3. Juxtaposition: the ball and the loss

The ball is Mathilde's one night of fulfilment: she is admired, sought after, noticed by the Minister himself. Within hours, back in their apartment, she discovers the necklace is gone.

**Function.** Placing the loss directly after the triumph is a **reversal**. The sequence makes her success and her ruin a single event seen from two sides: the night that proved she could shine is the night that destroys her. Notice the hinge between them. Leaving the ball, she hurries away so that the other women, wrapped in rich furs, will not see her modest everyday wraps. Her shame about appearances shapes the very moment of loss.

### 4. Pacing: one night against ten years

The ball, the departure and the frantic search are told as scenes, hour by hour. Then ten years pass in a few paragraphs: the servant dismissed, the attic, the heavy housework, the husband's extra copying work at night, Mathilde's hands grown red and her manner hard.

**Function.** The imbalance is the point (review 3.A). A few hours of display determine a decade of labour, and the brisk summary makes the decade feel like a grinding, undifferentiated weight.

### 5. Withholding: the last line

At the end, walking on the Champs-Élysées one Sunday, Mathilde meets Madame Forestier, still young and beautiful, and tells her everything. Her friend reveals that the original necklace was imitation, worth at most five hundred francs. The story ends there.

**Function.** Delaying this fact is what makes the plot work. Through the ten years, the reader believes, as the Loisels do, that the sacrifice was necessary. The final line recasts it as needless, the result not of fate but of a choice: had Mathilde confessed the loss, the truth would have come out at once. The story gives no reaction scene, so the reader must do the reinterpreting.

## From reading to writing: the claim-and-evidence paragraph

Skill 7.A is the foundation of every AP essay. It has two demands: a **claim that requires defence**, and **evidence** that defends it. Commentary, which you practised in weeks 1 and 2, is what connects them.

**Step 1: test your claim.** Ask whether a reasonable reader could disagree.

| Not a claim (a fact) | A claim (needs defence) |
|---|---|
| Mathilde borrows a necklace from Madame Forestier. | Mathilde borrows the necklace not to enjoy beauty but to be seen as someone she is not. |
| The necklace turns out to be fake. | The fake necklace exposes that everything Mathilde wanted was itself a kind of imitation. |
| The Loisels work for ten years. | The ten years of labour give Mathilde a strength her years of dreaming never did. |

**Step 2: choose evidence that proves the arguable part.** If the claim is "Mathilde's pride, more than bad luck, ruins her," evidence that the necklace was lost (bad luck) does not help; the decision to conceal the loss (pride) does.

**Step 3: write commentary that answers "so what?"** Explain what the evidence reveals and how it supports the claim.

A model paragraph:

> [Claim] In "The Necklace," Maupassant shows that Mathilde's misery comes less from her circumstances than from her habit of comparing them with fantasy. [Evidence] The narrator lists the "bareness of the walls" and "the shabby chairs," but adds that these were things "of which another woman of her rank would never even have been conscious." [Commentary] The home is ordinary; what tortures Mathilde is the gap between it and the life she imagines, so her suffering is self-generated. [Evidence] Even the "little Breton peasant who did her humble housework" stirs in her only "despairing regrets and bewildering dreams." [Commentary] Having a servant is a sign of modest comfort, yet Mathilde sees in it only what she lacks; the detail shows that no circumstance could satisfy a mind that measures everything against luxury. [Link] This habit of comparison is what will make a borrowed ornament seem worth any risk.

A weak version:

> Mathilde is unhappy in "The Necklace." She does not like her house. She has a servant from Brittany. Then she gets invited to a ball and borrows a necklace.

The first sentence is a fact, not a claim; the evidence is paraphrased loosely; there is no commentary; the last sentence drifts into plot summary.

## Common misreadings

- **"The story is about bad luck."** The loss is chance; the ruin is a choice. The sequence (loss, then concealment, then debt) makes that distinction visible.
- **Blaming Madame Forestier.** She lends freely and never claims the jewels are real; the Loisels never ask.
- **Seeing Monsieur Loisel as a fool.** He is generous (the four hundred francs), loyal and hardworking. His sacrifices are quieter than his wife's and just as real.
- **Reading the ten years as pure punishment.** The story also suggests that Mathilde becomes capable and enduring in a way she never was while dreaming; this is a fruitful complexity for essays.
- **Writing summary and calling it analysis.** Retelling the plot in order is the most common way a paragraph loses commentary points.

## Unit 1 connections and looking ahead

All three stories in this unit end in irony built on what the reader does not know until the end. O. Henry withholds Jim's sale of the watch and then lets his narrator reframe the outcome as wisdom. Joyce withholds nothing factual but lets the boy's romance build until the dark hall forces him to see it. Maupassant withholds a single fact and gives no commentary at all. Comparing them is good practice for the **Q3 literary argument**, where you must make a claim about a literary concept (here: sacrifice, illusion, a revelation) and defend it with evidence from a work you know well.

In Unit 2, the claim-and-evidence paragraph moves to poetry, where evidence is often a single word or line. In Unit 3, *Frankenstein* asks you to explain conflict and the function of significant events across a whole novel (3.E, 3.F), building directly on this week's work with sequence.

## Self-check

1. List the links in the causal chain from the invitation to the ten years of debt. Which link is a choice rather than chance?
2. Why must the ball come immediately before the loss? What would be lost if the necklace were lost on the way *to* the ball?
3. Explain the function of withholding the truth about the necklace until the final line.
4. Turn the fact "The Loisels replace the necklace" into a defensible claim.
5. Find two details in the opening that support the claim "Mathilde's misery is self-inflicted," and write commentary for one of them.
6. Compare the endings of "The Gift of the Magi" and "The Necklace": both are ironic; how do the narrators' choices make them feel different?
