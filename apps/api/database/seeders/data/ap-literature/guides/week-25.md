## What you need to be able to do

- **3.D** Find the contrasts a poem sets up (past and present, surface and depth, speaker and listener, what is said and what is meant) and explain what each one does for the poem's meaning.
- **6.D** Recognise an allusion, say what story or text it calls up, and explain what that borrowed material adds to, or ironises in, the speaker.

In plain language: in a dramatic monologue the poet never steps in to tell you what to think. You have to hear the gap between what the speaker means to say and what he actually reveals, and you have to know enough of the stories he names to measure him against them. This is the last poetry unit before the exam, so every week now ends in a full Q1 essay.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Dramatic monologue | A poem spoken by one character (not the poet) to a silent listener at a moment of pressure; the speaker reveals more than he intends. |
| Silent auditor | The listener whose presence, reactions and identity we infer only from the speaker's words. |
| Dramatic irony | The reader understands something the speaker does not, often about the speaker himself. |
| Verbal irony | Saying one thing and meaning another, knowingly. |
| Situational irony | An outcome that reverses what the situation led us to expect. |
| Contrast | A juxtaposition of unlike things (images, people, tones, times) that makes each clearer and creates tension. |
| Juxtaposition | Placing two elements side by side so the reader compares them. |
| Allusion | A reference, direct or indirect, to a story, person, text or event outside the poem. |
| Enjambment | A sentence running over the end of a line without a pause. |
| Heroic couplet | Rhymed pairs of iambic pentameter lines. |
| Blank verse | Unrhymed iambic pentameter. |
| Euphemism | A mild phrase standing in for a harsh fact ("all smiles stopped"). |

## The text in context

Robert Browning (1812-1889) published "My Last Duchess" in *Dramatic Lyrics* (1842). The heading "Ferrara" places the speaker at an Italian Renaissance court, and readers usually identify him with Alfonso II d'Este, Duke of Ferrara, whose young first wife died in 1561, three years after the wedding, and who then negotiated a new marriage with the family of the Count of Tyrol. Browning never names him; he gives us only the voice. The poem is 56 lines of iambic pentameter in rhymed couplets, a single unbroken speech to an envoy.

Alfred Tennyson (1809-1892) wrote "Ulysses" in 1833, weeks after the sudden death of his closest friend, Arthur Henry Hallam, and published it in *Poems* (1842). Tennyson later said the poem expressed his own sense that he had to go forward and face life after that loss. The speaker is Homer's Odysseus, home on Ithaca years after the Trojan War, but the plot of a last voyage beyond the known world comes from Dante's *Inferno* (Canto XXVI), where Ulysses tells how he sailed past the limits of the world and drowned. The poem is 70 lines of blank verse in three verse paragraphs, each with a different audience.

Both poems appeared in the same year and both perfected the form now called the dramatic monologue. Put side by side, they show its range: Browning's speaker condemns himself; Tennyson's may be heroic, or may be another proud man rationalising the abandonment of his duties. In each case the reader must decide, because the poet never says.

## Close reading

**1. Courtesy as control.**

> Will't please you sit and look at her? I said
> "Fra Pandolf" by design, for never read
> Strangers like you that pictured countenance,
> The depth and passion of its earnest glance,
> But to myself they turned (since none puts by
> The curtain I have drawn for you, but I)

*Observe.* The first sentence is a question, but it is an order: the guest will sit. The Duke explains that he names the painter "by design", and a parenthesis tells us the portrait is kept behind a curtain only he may draw.

*Interpret.* Everything here is about who controls looking. The Duke chooses when the guest sits, what he looks at and what he is told. The curtain is the key detail: in life the Duchess smiled at whomever she liked; in paint she smiles only when her husband allows it.

*Function.* The contrast between the living wife and the painted one (3.D) is the poem's hidden argument. The Duke prefers the portrait, and the reader begins, before any confession, to suspect why.

**2. The boast that convicts.**

> Who'd stoop to blame
> This sort of trifling? Even had you skill
> In speech—which I have not—to make your will
> Quite clear to such an one, [...]
> E'en then would be some stooping; and I choose
> Never to stoop.

*Observe.* "Stoop" appears three times in ten lines. The aside "which I have not" claims a lack of eloquence in the middle of an eloquent speech.

*Interpret.* The Duke means to show dignity: he was too great to correct a wife over trifles. The reader hears something else: a man who would not speak to his wife, and who will, two lines later, report that he "gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together."

*Function.* This is dramatic irony built from contrast. The speaker's intended meaning (my pride is noble) and the reader's understanding (his pride is murderous) sit side by side in the same words. The false modesty of "which I have not" teaches us to distrust his whole account (4.D in review).

**3. The allusion that sums him up.** The poem's last words point to a bronze: "Notice Neptune, though, / Taming a sea-horse, thought a rarity, / Which Claus of Innsbruck cast in bronze for me!" A sea-god mastering a wild animal is an allusion to classical myth, and it is also an emblem. The Duke turns from a wife he had silenced to a sculpture of taming, shown to the envoy of his next bride, and the final word is "me". The allusion's function (6.D) is to compress the Duke's idea of marriage into a single object.

**4. Borrowed heroes in "Ulysses".**

> It may be that the gulfs will wash us down:
> It may be we shall touch the Happy Isles,
> And see the great Achilles, whom we knew.

*Observe.* Two parallel clauses ("It may be") set drowning beside arrival; the second leads to Achilles, who died at Troy.

*Interpret.* The Happy Isles are the Isles of the Blessed, the home of dead heroes. So both outcomes are death. Ulysses does not deny it; he turns it into a reunion, and "whom we knew" reminds his crew that they belong in that company.

*Function.* The allusion lets an old man make dying sound like the completion of a legend. A reader who also hears Dante's drowned Ulysses behind the poem will see a second function: the heroic language may be the same restless pride that Dante punished. Allusion here creates the poem's central ambiguity.

## Contrast is the engine of both poems

List the contrasts before you write; they are your line of reasoning.

- **"My Last Duchess":** the living Duchess and the painted one; her open warmth ("her looks went everywhere") and his closed control (the curtain); the Duke's courteous manner and the violence it reports; the "nine-hundred-years-old name" and the "bough of cherries" she valued as much; the dead wife and the dowry of the next one.
- **"Ulysses":** home and voyage; the "still hearth" and "barren crags" of Ithaca and the "untravell'd world"; Telemachus's "slow prudence" and his father's restlessness ("He works his work, I mine"); age and will ("Made weak by time and fate, but strong in will"); the slow evening ("the slow moon climbs") and the sudden imperative "Push off".

For each contrast, ask the AP function question: *what does placing these two things together make the reader see?* In Browning the answer is usually that the speaker's view is exposed. In Tennyson it is usually that the speaker's resolve is tested and then asserted.

## From reading to writing

Q1 asks you to "analyze how the poet uses literary elements and techniques to convey" something. A model paragraph for the Browning prompt in this week's quiz:

> **Claim:** Browning reveals the Duke's need to own his wife through the contrast between the curtained portrait and the living woman whose "looks went everywhere." **Evidence:** The Duke tells his guest that no one "puts by / The curtain I have drawn for you, but I," a parenthetical boast that he alone decides when the Duchess's "earnest glance" may be seen. **Commentary:** In life, by his own complaint, she "liked whate'er / She looked on," spending her smiles as freely on a sunset as on him; in paint, her smile appears only at his hand. The contrast shows that the portrait is the marriage the Duke wanted, a wife reduced to a possession, and the reader, noticing how much he prefers the image to the woman, begins to suspect what became of her before he admits it.

*Why it works:* the claim names a technique (contrast) and an idea (possession); the evidence is short and embedded; the commentary explains *how* the contrast produces the meaning and ties it back to the claim. It also shows the reader's experience of the poem, which is often what lifts commentary to a 4.

*A weak version:* "Browning uses imagery of a curtain. The curtain shows that the Duke is controlling. This is important because it shows his character." The claim is vague, the evidence is named but not quoted, and the commentary only repeats the claim.

## Common misreadings

- **Taking the Duke at his word.** He says he lacks "skill / In speech" and that the daughter, not the dowry, is his "object". The poem trains you to read against both claims.
- **Missing the listener.** The envoy's identity arrives late and changes everything: the story of the last Duchess is told to the representative of the next.
- **Treating "all smiles stopped" as fading love.** "I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together" is a cause and its effect. Most readers take it as an order for her death; at minimum it is an act of force, not a cooling marriage.
- **Reading "Ulysses" as simply inspirational.** The closing line is stirring, but the poem also dismisses Penelope ("Match'd with an aged wife") and the people of Ithaca ("a savage race"). The best essays hold both readings.
- **Naming an allusion without explaining it.** "Achilles is an allusion to Greek myth" earns nothing. Say what the story adds: Achilles died at Troy, so seeing him means dying, and Ulysses makes death sound like a homecoming.
