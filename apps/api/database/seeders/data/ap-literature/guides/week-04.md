# Week 04 · Speakers and Masks, Reading Character in a Poem

**CED skills:** 1.A Describe what textual details reveal about a character, that character's perspective and motives · 5.B Explain the function of specific words and phrases
**Big Ideas:** Character (CHR), Narration (NAR), Word choice (FIG)
**Read:** Paul Laurence Dunbar, "We Wear the Mask" (1895; [Poetry Foundation](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/44203/we-wear-the-mask)) · Edwin Arlington Robinson, "Richard Cory" (1897; [Project Gutenberg #313](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/313)) · Emily Dickinson, "I'm nobody! Who are you?" (1891; [Project Gutenberg #2679](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2679)) · Langston Hughes, "Mother to Son" (1922; in *The Weary Blues*, [Project Gutenberg #74745](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/74745))
**Spiral:** Unit 1 taught you to identify a narrator (4.A) and point of view (4.B), to read setting details (2.A), and to explain why a story orders events as it does (3.A, 3.B). Every one of those moves works on poems. A speaker is a narrator in a smaller space, and a poem's last line can be placed as deliberately as the last sentence of "The Gift of the Magi".

## What you need to be able to do

- **1.A** Point to specific words in a poem and say what they reveal about a character: who the speaker is, how the speaker sees the world, and what the speaker wants. In poems the most important character is usually the speaker, but poems also portray other people (Richard Cory, the son in "Mother to Son").
- **5.B** Explain why a poet chose a particular word or phrase: what it means literally, what it suggests, and what work it does in the poem. "Explain the function" always means "what does this do for the poem?", never just "what does it mean?"

In plain language: by the end of this week you should be able to read a short poem twice, name the speaker in one accurate sentence, and pick out three words that tell you who that speaker is.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Speaker | The voice that says the poem. Treat it like a narrator: a constructed voice, not automatically the poet. |
| Persona | A character the poet invents to speak the poem (the mother in "Mother to Son"). From the Latin for a mask. |
| Addressee | Whoever the speaker talks to: a son, "you", the world, God. Shifts in addressee are often turning points. |
| Line | The unit of verse; it ends where the poet breaks it, whether or not the sentence ends there. |
| Stanza | A group of lines set off by space; the poem's paragraph. A quatrain is a four-line stanza. |
| End-stopped line | A line that ends with punctuation, so the sense pauses with the line. |
| Enjambment | A sentence running on past the line break, so the break falls mid-thought and puts pressure on the words around it. |
| Refrain | A phrase or line repeated at intervals, often at the ends of stanzas ("We wear the mask"). |
| Diction | Word choice. Its effects come from denotation (dictionary meaning) and connotation (associations). |
| Tone | The speaker's attitude toward the subject or audience, as shown by diction and syntax. |
| Dialect | A regional or social variety of a language, here used to give a speaker a particular voice ("I'se been a-climbin' on"). |

## How to read a poem the first time

Most students' first trouble with poetry is mechanical, so fix the mechanics first.

1. **Read by sentences, not by lines.** Find the periods. "Richard Cory" has only a handful of sentences in sixteen lines; read each one through to its full stop, ignoring the line ends, and the poem becomes ordinary English.
2. **Then read by lines.** Now notice what the line breaks do. Where a sentence breaks across lines, the last word of a line and the first word of the next get extra weight.
3. **Name the speaker and addressee.** Who is talking, and to whom? Use pronouns: "we" or "I"; "you", "thee" or "them".
4. **Mark the stanzas.** Write one phrase in the margin for what each stanza does.
5. **Circle surprising words.** A word that is odder, stronger or more specific than it needs to be is usually doing work you can explain.

## The texts in context

**Paul Laurence Dunbar (1872-1906)** was the son of formerly enslaved parents in Dayton, Ohio, and became one of the first Black American poets with a national readership. He wrote both in standard English and in dialect. "We Wear the Mask" first appeared in *Majors and Minors* (1895) and was collected the next year in *Lyrics of Lowly Life*. It is a rondeau-like poem built on only two rhyme sounds, with a short refrain.

**Edwin Arlington Robinson (1869-1935)** wrote many portraits of the people of "Tilbury Town", a fictional New England town based on his hometown of Gardiner, Maine. "Richard Cory" appeared in *The Children of the Night* (1897), in the depression years after the Panic of 1893, which gives the townspeople's hunger its edge.

**Emily Dickinson (1830-1886)** published almost nothing in her lifetime. Her poems were edited and published after her death; "I'm nobody! Who are you?" appeared in *Poems, Second Series* (1891). Those early editors regularized her punctuation, so the dashes and capitals you see in modern editions can differ from the 1891 text quoted here.

**Langston Hughes (1901-1967)** was a central figure of the Harlem Renaissance. "Mother to Son" first appeared in *The Crisis* in December 1922 and was collected in *The Weary Blues* (1926). It is a dramatic speech: a mother talks to her son, in her own vernacular, at a single moment.

## Close reading

### 1. A collective speaker: "We Wear the Mask"

> We wear the mask that grins and lies,
> It hides our cheeks and shades our eyes,—
> This debt we pay to human guile;

**Observe.** The speaker is "we", not "I". The mask is the subject of two verbs: it "grins" and "lies". Line 3 calls wearing it a "debt" paid "to human guile".

**Interpret.** A plural speaker turns the poem from a private confession into testimony for a group. The verbs make the mask active: it is not just a covering but a performance, a fixed smile that deceives. "Debt" says the performance is owed, not chosen; "guile" (cunning, trickery) locates the original dishonesty in the world that demands it.

**Function.** These three lines establish the speakers' perspective (1.A) through diction (5.B): they are people forced into a defensive deception by a deceitful world. Everything that follows (the rhetorical question to "the world", the cry to Christ) develops that perspective. When an AP question asks what a detail "reveals about the speaker's perspective", this is the reasoning it wants: word, connotation, what it shows about the person speaking.

Notice too where the poem turns. Stanzas 1 and 2 speak about the world ("Why should the world be over-wise"). Stanza 3 speaks to Christ: "We smile, but, O great Christ, our cries / To thee from tortured souls arise." A change of addressee is one of the most reliable signals of a shift in a poem. Here it lets us overhear what the mask hides, so the poem performs a small unmasking even as it ends, "We wear the mask!"

### 2. The watchers as speakers: "Richard Cory"

> Whenever Richard Cory went down town,
> We people on the pavement looked at him:

**Observe.** The speaker is again "we": "We people on the pavement". They look; Cory walks. The poem's details about him are all things that can be seen or heard in public: dress, manners, a greeting, a walk.

**Interpret.** The townspeople look up at Cory from the pavement, socially and almost physically. Their diction makes him royal: "from sole to crown", "imperially slim", "richer than a king". A neighbor becomes a monarch in their eyes, and the line "he was always human when he talked" gives them away: they are surprised and grateful that someone so far above them was courteous.

**Function.** Robinson builds a character (Cory) entirely from another character's perspective (the town's). That is why the last line works. After "So on we worked, and waited for the light, / And went without the meat, and cursed the bread", the poem ends: "And Richard Cory, one calm summer night, / Went home and put a bullet through his head." The flat, plain statement comes with no explanation, because the speakers have none. The structure (fifteen lines of surface, one line of catastrophe) makes the reader share their ignorance. This is Unit 1's skill 3.B, the function of a sequence, at work in sixteen lines.

### 3. Word by word: "I'm nobody! Who are you?"

> I'm nobody! Who are you?
> Are you nobody, too?
> Then there's a pair of us — don't tell!
> They'd banish us, you know.

**Observe.** Short lines, exclamations and questions; the speaker talks directly to "you", the reader. "Don't tell!" makes a secret of being nobody. "They" would "banish" the pair.

**Interpret.** The speaker is delighted, not ashamed, to be nobody, and treats the reader as a fellow conspirator. The second stanza explains why: "How dreary to be somebody! / How public, like a frog / To tell your name the livelong day / To an admiring bog!" Fame becomes croaking your own name all day to a swamp.

**Function.** The comparison (a frog, a bog) does the character work: it shows a speaker with a sharp, comic sense of what public life is worth. A good answer about this poem names a perspective ("the speaker values privacy over fame and mocks those who seek an audience") and supports it with specific words ("dreary", "public", "admiring bog").

### 4. Dialect as character: "Mother to Son"

> Well, son, I'll tell you:
> Life for me ain't been no crystal stair.

**Observe.** The speech opens conversationally ("Well, son") and in vernacular ("ain't been no"). The crystal stair is defined by negation. The lines that follow list what her stair did have: "tacks in it, / And splinters, / And boards torn up, / And places with no carpet on the floor— / Bare."

**Interpret.** The mother's voice is plain, honest and warm ("honey"); she does not pretend life is easy. The one-word line "Bare." isolates the poorest image on the page. Her verbs are continuing ones: "I'se been a-climbin' on, / And reachin' landin's, / And turnin' corners". Her life is still in progress.

**Function.** Her diction and dialect characterize her as a person who has endured without bitterness, and that character gives her advice its authority: "So boy, don't you turn back." When she ends, "I'se still climbin', / And life for me ain't been no crystal stair", the repeated line frames everything she has said. A reader who dismisses the dialect as "incorrect English" misses that it is the source of her dignity and credibility in the poem.

## From reading to writing

Skill 7.A, from Unit 1, asks for a paragraph with a claim that needs defending and the evidence that defends it. In poetry the evidence is usually a few words, not a stanza.

**Model paragraph (on "Richard Cory")**

> Robinson's townspeople reveal more about themselves than about Richard Cory. **[claim]** Their diction turns a well-dressed neighbor into royalty: he is a gentleman "from sole to crown", "imperially slim", and "richer than a king". **[evidence]** These are not facts about Cory so much as measures of how far beneath him the speakers feel, standing "on the pavement" while he walks by. **[commentary]** Even their praise that he was "always human when he talked" shows they expected coldness from someone so high. **[more evidence and commentary]** Because the poem never lets us past their admiring gaze, Cory's suicide in the final line exposes the limits of what the watchers could see. **[link to larger meaning]**

**A weak version:** "Richard Cory is rich and everyone likes him, but then he kills himself, which shows money can't buy happiness." This summarizes the plot and states a moral, but it never explains a word of the poem or the role of the speakers. The AP readers call this "summary instead of commentary."

## Common misreadings

- **"The speaker is the poet."** Hughes is not a mother; Robinson does not say he envied Cory. Say "the speaker" unless the poem gives you a reason to say more.
- **Reading each line as a separate sentence.** Lines 13-16 of "Richard Cory" are one sentence; reading them as fragments loses the "So... And... And" momentum that rushes to the end.
- **Over-reading the mask as literal.** Dunbar's speakers do not wear a physical mask; it is their public face, their smiles and songs.
- **Treating Dickinson as sad.** The exclamation marks, the "don't tell!" and the comic frog are playful. You may argue the playfulness hides something, but you must first see the play.
- **Taking dialect as a sign of ignorance.** In "Mother to Son" the vernacular is a deliberate artistic choice that makes the mother's wisdom sound earned.

## Connections and looking ahead

Unit 1's characters and this week's masked speakers share a gap between a public face and a private self: Mathilde Loisel in "The Necklace" lives for the appearance of wealth she does not have. Next week you will study how a poem's **structure** and **contrasts** make meaning (Blake's paired songs, Sonnet 73); the stanza-by-stanza habit you start this week is the foundation. In week 6, comparisons (simile and metaphor) take center stage; you have already met two this week, the frog and the crystal stair. On the AP exam, questions on speaker and character make up a large share of the multiple-choice items on poetry passages, and the Q1 poetry essay always rewards a precise account of who is speaking and how they see their subject.

## Self-check

1. In one sentence, who is the speaker of "We Wear the Mask", and to whom do the speakers turn in the third stanza?
2. Name two words in "Richard Cory" that make Cory sound royal, and explain what they reveal about the townspeople.
3. Why does Robinson save the suicide for the last line? Answer in terms of the speakers' knowledge.
4. What does the comparison to "an admiring bog" reveal about Dickinson's speaker's view of fame?
5. Explain the function of the one-word line "Bare." in "Mother to Son".
