## What you need to be able to do

- **1.C** Explain the *function* of contrasting characters: what a contrast reveals about a character, how it sharpens a scene, or how it builds a story's meaning.

The skill is easy to do badly. "Miss Brill is old and the couple are young" is a contrast, but it is not yet analysis. You earn the skill when you can say what the contrast *does*: for example, that the lovers' youth and cruelty expose the fantasy of belonging Miss Brill has built from watching others. Mansfield's story is ideal practice because almost every character is a foil, and many of them work by **likeness** rather than difference.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Contrasting characters | Characters set against each other so that each one's qualities become clearer. |
| Foil | A character whose differences or resemblances highlight another's; a foil by likeness can be more revealing than one by opposition. |
| Close third person | Third-person narration that stays inside one character's mind and often uses her idiom. |
| Free indirect discourse | A character's thoughts given in the narrator's sentences without quotation marks ("No doubt somebody would have noticed"). |
| Epiphany | A sudden moment of insight; Miss Brill's "They were all on the stage" is a false one, followed by a true one she cannot name. |
| Dramatic irony | The reader understands what the character does not, as when Miss Brill describes "cupboards." |
| Pathos | The quality in a text that moves the reader to pity. Mansfield's control of it is a sophistication point in itself. |

## The text in context

Katherine Mansfield (1888-1923) was born in Wellington, New Zealand, and settled in England as a young woman. She spent much of her last years in the south of France and in Switzerland for her health; she died of tuberculosis at thirty-four. "Miss Brill" first appeared in *The Athenaeum* on 26 November 1920 and was collected in *The Garden Party and Other Stories* in 1922.

Mansfield is a master of the plotless-seeming modern story, built from small observed details and a single turn. "Miss Brill" covers one Sunday afternoon in the public gardens (the *Jardins Publiques*) of a French resort town during the opening of "the Season." Nothing dramatic happens; a woman listens to a band, watches people, overhears two sentences and goes home. Everything depends on the contrasts Mansfield arranges and on a narration that lets us stand inside Miss Brill's happiness while seeing around it.

## Close reading

### 1. Self-correction as a habit

> And when she breathed, something light and sad—no, not sad, exactly—something gentle seemed to move in her bosom.

**Observe.** A feeling is named ("sad"), retracted ("no, not sad, exactly") and replaced ("gentle"). The dashes make the correction visible.

**Interpret.** Miss Brill edits her own emotions as she has them. The sadness is there; she will not let it stay.

**Function.** This is perspective (4.C), and it sets up 1.C: a woman who cannot name her own loneliness will not recognize it in others either. The same correction returns later ("not sadness—no, not sadness") when the band plays, and the story ends with "something crying" that she still will not call herself.

### 2. The people in cupboards

> They were odd, silent, nearly all old, and from the way they stared they looked as though they'd just come from dark little rooms or even—even cupboards!

**Observe.** A list of judgments ("odd, silent, nearly all old"); a comic-gothic flourish ("even—even cupboards!"); the exclamation of someone pleased by her own wit.

**Interpret.** Miss Brill describes the bench-sitters from above, as a spectator. She is unmarried, alone, no longer young, and she sits on a bench every Sunday staring at people.

**Function (1.C).** Here is a foil by likeness. The contrast Miss Brill draws (them versus me) is one the story will erase: at the end she goes home to "the little dark room—her room like a cupboard." The repeated image is Mansfield's way of letting the reader recognize her before she does.

### 3. The ermine toque

> Now everything, her hair, her face, even her eyes, was the same colour as the shabby ermine, and her hand, in its cleaned glove, lifted to dab her lips, was a tiny yellowish paw.

**Observe.** The woman has faded to the colour of her fur; "shabby," "cleaned glove," "tiny yellowish paw." The fur and the woman merge.

**Interpret.** Like Miss Brill, she is an aging woman in an old fur, carefully kept up. When the gentleman blows smoke in her face and walks off, she "smiled more brightly than ever."

**Function (1.C).** The episode is a rehearsal of the ending. Miss Brill watches it as theatre, hearing the drum beat "The Brute! The Brute!", and the contrast between her absorbed spectatorship and the reader's recognition creates the story's dramatic irony. When Miss Brill's own fur is mocked, the reader has already seen the scene once.

### 4. Hero and heroine

> They were beautifully dressed; they were in love. The hero and heroine, of course, just arrived from his father's yacht.

**Observe.** Two short clauses of admiration; then Miss Brill's casting ("of course"), complete with an imagined yacht.

**Interpret.** She gives the lovers the lead roles in her play, the opposite of herself in every visible way: young, paired, rich, desired.

**Function (1.C).** This is the story's sharpest contrast by difference, and Mansfield uses it as a reversal. The characters Miss Brill most romanticizes are the ones who say what everyone else's silence has hidden: "Why does she come here at all—who wants her?" The contrast collapses her fantasy from outside.

## From reading to writing

This unit has been preparation for **Q2, the prose fiction analysis essay**: an unseen passage (often 500-700 words), about forty minutes, and a prompt such as *analyze how the author uses literary elements and techniques to portray the complex relationship between a character and the people around her.* Here is a full plan for "Miss Brill," built the way you should build one in the exam room.

**1. Annotate for the prompt (5-8 minutes).** Mark every place the passage contrasts Miss Brill with someone else; circle diction that carries judgment ("odd," "funny," "stupid old thing"); note shifts (the band's changes, the section break).

**2. Thesis with tension.**

> Mansfield surrounds Miss Brill with characters who mirror her and characters who reject her, and lets her judge them all from inside a happy, self-editing narration, so that the reader recognizes her loneliness long before the young couple speak it aloud.

**3. Line of reasoning (three body paragraphs).**

- *Mirrors she does not see.* The bench-sitters from "cupboards," the silent old couple, the ermine toque: foils by likeness, each judged by Miss Brill and each a picture of her.
- *The play.* Her discovery that "They were all on the stage" contrasts actor and audience and puts her among the actors; her imagined line "I have been an actress for a long time" is unintentionally true.
- *The lovers and the dark room.* The foil by difference delivers the outside view; the final section's "room like a cupboard" and "something crying" close the gap between her view and ours.

**4. Commentary that explains.** For each quotation, say what technique it shows (foil, free indirect discourse, repeated image, irony) and how it supports the claim. One well-explained detail beats three listed ones.

**5. Sophistication.** Show complexity: Mansfield neither mocks Miss Brill nor merely pities her; the close third person makes the reader share her joy and see its fragility at once.

**A model body paragraph:**

> Mansfield's most painful contrast is not between Miss Brill and the young lovers but between Miss Brill and the old people she pities. From her bench she sees figures who are "odd, silent, nearly all old," and imagines them emerging from "dark little rooms or even—even cupboards!" The exclamation is the pleased wit of a spectator who thinks herself apart from the spectacle, and the free indirect style lets us hear her pleasure without the narrator's correction. Mansfield saves the correction for the last paragraph, where Miss Brill climbs to "the little dark room—her room like a cupboard." The repeated image turns her contrast into a likeness, so that the reader, not Miss Brill, finally sees that the woman who has spent the afternoon "sitting in other people's lives" has been describing her own.

**A weak version:**

> Miss Brill thinks the old people are odd. But she is old too. This is ironic. At the end she goes back to her room.

It notices the right contrast but never explains it: no technique is named, no quotation is analyzed, and "this is ironic" is a label, not commentary.

## Common misreadings

- **"Miss Brill is just foolish."** Mansfield's narration is tender as well as ironic. An essay that mocks her loses the complexity; one that only pities her loses the irony.
- **"The young couple are the villains."** They are careless and cruel, but the story's force is that they merely say aloud what the silent crowd's indifference has implied all afternoon.
- **"Nothing happens."** A single overheard exchange reverses everything. Look for the turn; in modern short fiction it is often small.
- **Missing foils by likeness.** Students find the obvious contrast (young lovers) and miss the more revealing resemblances (toque, bench-sitters, invalid).
- **Reading the last line as literal.** The fur is not crying. The displacement is Miss Brill's, and it shows that the insult has hurt her without bringing self-knowledge.

## Connections and looking ahead

All three of this unit's protagonists have a private vision overturned. Goodman Brown sees his village in a new light and never forgives it; Gilman's narrator turns her vision inward until it consumes her; Miss Brill's vision is broken by two strangers and she goes home in silence. Hawthorne's hedging narrator, Gilman's journal and Mansfield's close third person are three answers to the same question: how much should the reader know that the character does not? That question returns in Unit 6 with Hamlet (week 18 practises 1.C again with Hamlet and his foils) and in Unit 7 with Poe's narrator, whose reliability (4.D) is the whole story. On the AP exam, 1.C appears in multiple-choice stems such as "The contrast between X and Y serves primarily to," and it is one of the most productive lines of reasoning for Q2.
