# Week 09 · The Creature's Narrative: Evidence and Commentary

**CED skills:** 7.C Develop commentary that establishes and explains relationships among textual evidence, the line of reasoning, and the thesis · 7.D Select and use relevant and sufficient evidence to both develop and support a line of reasoning
**Big Ideas:** Literary Argumentation (LAN); with Character (CHR) and Narration (NAR)
**Read:** Mary Shelley, *Frankenstein* (1818), Volume II, Chapters III-IX, the creature's own story (1831 edition: Chapters 11-17). Text: [Project Gutenberg #41445](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/41445).
**Spiral:** character change (1.B) and significant events (3.E) from week 8 are now argued by the creature himself; conflict (3.F) and thesis (7.B) from week 7; narration and point of view from week 1.

## What you need to be able to do

- **7.D** Choose evidence that is *relevant* (it bears on your claim, not just on the topic) and *sufficient* (enough of it, from enough of the text, to carry the claim), and fit it into your sentences.
- **7.C** Write commentary that explains *how* the evidence supports the claim and *how* each claim advances the thesis. Commentary answers "so what?" and "how?", not "what happens?"

In plain language: this week you learn the difference between an essay that quotes and an essay that argues. Row B of the AP rubric (0-4 points) is mostly about this skill, and it is where most essays lose most of their points.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Commentary | Your explanation of how a piece of evidence supports your claim and connects to the thesis; analysis, not summary. |
| Relevant evidence | Evidence that directly supports the specific claim of the paragraph. |
| Sufficient evidence | Enough well-chosen evidence, from enough of the text, to make the claim convincing. |
| Embedded quotation | A short quotation woven into your own sentence grammatically. |
| Line of reasoning | The sequence of claims that leads to the thesis; commentary is what links them. |
| Bildungsroman | A narrative of a character's education and growth; the creature's story parodies and tragically reverses one. |
| Nested narrative | A narrative inside a narrative; the creature tells Victor, who tells Walton, who writes to Margaret. |
| Sympathetic narrator | A narrator whose account invites the reader's sympathy, which does not settle whether it is reliable. |

## The text in context: what the creature tells

**Ch. III.** The creature's first confused sensations: light and dark, hunger, cold, a forest near Ingolstadt, the moon. He finds a fire left by travellers, learns that it warms and burns, and cooks. A shepherd flees from him; villagers drive him off with stones. He shelters in a hovel built against a cottage and watches through a crevice: an old blind man, a young woman and a young man.

**Ch. IV.** He watches the family's poverty and tenderness, stops stealing their food, and secretly gathers their firewood. He learns words: fire, milk, bread, and their names, Felix, Agatha and their father. He sees his reflection in a pool and is horrified.

**Ch. V.** Safie, an Arabian woman, arrives; Felix teaches her the language from Volney's *Ruins of Empires*, and the creature learns with her, hearing history, wealth, rank and injustice, and asking what he is.

**Ch. VI.** The De Laceys' history: wealthy in Paris, ruined and exiled after Felix helped Safie's father, a Turkish merchant unjustly condemned, to escape; the merchant betrayed them; Safie fled to find Felix.

**Ch. VII.** He finds a portmanteau with three books, *Paradise Lost*, a volume of Plutarch's *Lives* and *The Sorrows of Werter*, and reads them as true histories. He finds Victor's journal in the pocket of the clothes he took from the laboratory and reads the record of his own creation. He reveals himself to the blind father, and Felix, Agatha and Safie return and drive him out.

**Ch. VIII.** Rage: the family leaves and he burns the cottage. Using the journal he travels toward Geneva, saves a girl from a river and is shot for it. Near Geneva he seizes a boy, learns he is a Frankenstein, and strangles him. He takes the miniature and places it in the dress of a woman sleeping in a barn: Justine.

**Ch. IX.** His demand: make a female companion "as deformed and horrible" as himself, and they will live in the wilds of South America, far from humankind. Victor refuses, then, persuaded, consents.

## Close reading: from evidence to commentary

**1. Choosing evidence.** Suppose your claim is *"The creature's narrative shows that he learns cruelty from humans rather than bringing it into the world."* Which of these is relevant and which is merely on topic?

- He finds fire and learns that it can both warm and burn. *(On topic: learning. Not relevant to cruelty.)*
- A shepherd flees and villagers attack him with stones before he has harmed anyone. *(Relevant: violence comes first from humans.)*
- He stops stealing the cottagers' food when he sees their poverty and gathers their wood in secret. *(Relevant: his first moral choices are generous.)*
- Felix strikes him when he clings to the old man's knees, and "Cursed, cursed creator!" opens the next chapter. *(Relevant: the turn to rage follows the final rejection.)*

Four details across four chapters, each tied to the claim: that is **sufficient**. One quotation from the glacier speech would not be; it would be the creature's assertion, not the evidence for it.

**2. Commentary that explains.** The creature on *Paradise Lost* (Vol. II, Ch. VII):

> Like Adam, I was created apparently united by no link to any other being in existence; but his state was far different from mine in every other respect. He had come forth from the hands of God a perfect creature, happy and prosperous, guarded by the especial care of his Creator; he was allowed to converse with, and acquire knowledge from beings of a superior nature: but I was wretched, helpless, and alone. Many times I considered Satan as the fitter emblem of my condition; for often, like him, when I viewed the bliss of my protectors, the bitter gall of envy rose within me.

*Weak commentary:* "This quote shows that the creature compares himself to Adam and Satan. This is an allusion to *Paradise Lost*." (It names the device and repeats the quotation. It does not say what the comparison does.)

*Strong commentary:* "The creature reads Milton's Adam as a measure of what he lacks. Each of Adam's blessings ('guarded,' 'allowed to converse') is a relationship, and the creature's list of what he is, 'wretched, helpless, and alone,' ends on the one word that names their absence. When he turns to Satan, the likeness is not pride or rebellion but envy at the sight of others' 'bliss,' so the allusion locates the origin of his malice in exclusion rather than in nature. This supports the claim that his cruelty is learned, because the feeling he shares with Satan arises from watching a family he cannot join."

Notice the moves: (a) a claim about the evidence, (b) attention to specific words, (c) the effect on meaning, (d) an explicit link back to the paragraph's claim.

**3. The turn (Vol. II, Ch. VIII).** The chapter opens:

> Cursed, cursed creator! Why did I live? Why, in that instant, did I not extinguish the spark of existence which you had so wantonly bestowed? I know not; despair had not yet taken possession of me; my feelings were those of rage and revenge.

*Observe:* the curse repeats; the word "wantonly" accuses Victor of careless, irresponsible making. *Interpret:* the creature frames his life as a gift given without thought for its consequences. *Commentary link (1.B, 3.E):* the expulsion from the cottage is the event that converts longing into "rage and revenge"; placing the curse at a chapter opening makes it the hinge of his account. The "spark of existence" recalls Victor's own phrase on the night of animation, "a spark of being," so the creature's narrative answers Victor's.

**4. The argument (Vol. II, Ch. IX).** "I am malicious because I am miserable" is the creature's thesis, stated in five words. A good essay does not simply agree or disagree. It weighs the evidence the narrative offers: the generous acts of Chapters IV-VI, the rejections of Chapters III and VII-VIII, and the murder of William, a child who had done nothing to him, which complicates the creature's account of himself as purely reactive.

## From reading to writing: building a body paragraph

A model paragraph using the evidence from close reading 1:

> The creature's narrative presents cruelty as something taught by human beings before it is practised by him. *(claim)* His first encounters with people are violent on their side only: a shepherd flees, and in the village he is attacked until he escapes to the fields. *(evidence, summarized concisely because the event matters more than the wording)* By contrast, the first moral choice he records is to stop stealing the cottagers' food when he sees their hunger, and then to gather their wood in secret. *(evidence that contrasts)* Shelley orders these episodes so that the reader sees generosity in the creature before seeing any harm, and when Felix strikes him, the opening cry of the next chapter, "Cursed, cursed creator!", turns his rage not on the family but on the one who "so wantonly bestowed" his life. *(commentary: order of events plus diction)* The sequence thus supports the thesis that Victor's abandonment, repeated by every human who sees the creature, is the source of the violence that follows, though William's murder will test whether that explanation is complete. *(commentary: link to thesis and a complicating turn)*

Count the sentences: one claim, two of evidence, two of commentary. Commentary does the most work; evidence is short and pointed.

## Common misreadings

- **"The creature is simply innocent."** He murders a child and frames an innocent woman, deliberately. Sympathy for his narrative and judgment of his acts can coexist, and the best essays hold both.
- **"The creature is simply a monster pretending."** His acts of kindness are recorded in detail, and no one in the novel contradicts them.
- **"More quotations means more evidence."** Sufficiency is about range and relevance, not quantity. Four short, well-chosen details beat a long block quotation.
- **"Commentary = explaining what the quote means."** Paraphrase is not commentary. Commentary explains how the words support your claim and why that matters to your thesis.
- **"The creature learned to read from Victor."** He learns by listening to Felix teach Safie.

## Connections and looking ahead

The creature's narrative is the centre of the nested structure (week 7) and the fullest example of a narrator arguing for himself (week 1's narrator skills at novel length). It answers Victor's account of the same events: the bedside, the abandonment, William, the miniature. Next week, the last volume shows what Victor does with the creature's demand, and you will write a complete literary argument. In Unit 4, the same commentary skills meet short fiction; in Unit 6, Hamlet's soliloquies will ask you to judge another eloquent self-justifier.

**On the AP exam:** Row B (Evidence and Commentary) carries four of the six points on every essay. A top-scoring response backs every claim with specific evidence and explains, consistently and throughout, how that evidence advances the line of reasoning. Practise writing commentary that is longer than your quotations.
