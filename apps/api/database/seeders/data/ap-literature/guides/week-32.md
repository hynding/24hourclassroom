## What you need to be able to do

- **7.B** Write a thesis for a literary argument that is defensible (someone could disagree), specific (about one work and the prompt's situation), and that points toward a line of reasoning and an interpretation of the work as a whole.
- **7.D** Choose evidence from across a work that is both relevant (it proves the claim it sits under) and sufficient (enough of it, from enough of the work, to convince).

Q3 is the only essay on the exam where you choose the text. That freedom is a trap if you have not prepared: you need two or three works you know well enough to write about from memory, with key moments ready for many kinds of prompt. This week finishes the last of those works and then practises the move from any prompt to a thesis and a plan.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Literary argument (Q3) | The third essay: a prompt describes a literary situation or concept; you choose a work and analyze how that element contributes to the meaning of the work as a whole. |
| Defensible claim | An interpretation that requires evidence and could reasonably be disputed. |
| Line of reasoning | The connected sequence of claims, one per body paragraph, that leads to the thesis. |
| Relevant evidence | Evidence that directly supports the specific claim it is used for. |
| Sufficient evidence | Enough evidence, drawn from across the work, to make the claim convincing. |
| Commentary | Your explanation of how evidence supports the claim; most Row B points live here. |
| Work as a whole | What the work means, not only what happens in it; every Q3 answer must reach it. |
| Sophistication | Row C: complexity of thought (tensions, alternatives, context) or a consistently effective style. |

## The text in context: Act III

Act III opens downstairs while the Helmers dance at a fancy-dress ball above. Mrs. Linden meets Krogstad; they were once in love, and she offers to marry him. Changed, he offers to recall his letter. She stops him: Helmer must know the truth, so that the marriage can rest on understanding. Helmer brings Nora home from the dance and is full of possessive desire; Dr. Rank calls to say goodbye in disguised terms and leaves two visiting cards marked with a black cross. Helmer opens the letter-box.

He reads Krogstad's letter and turns on Nora: she has ruined him, she is unfit to raise the children, they must keep up appearances. Then a second letter arrives returning the bond, and he forgives her, happily imagining a wife who becomes "at once his wife and his child." Nora, who has gone out, returns: "now I have changed my dress." She asks him to sit down, and for the first time in eight years they talk seriously. She tells him she has been a doll-wife, that she stopped loving him "this evening, when the miracle did not happen," and that she must leave to educate herself. She returns his ring, takes her travelling-bag and goes. Helmer, alone, hopes for "the miracle of miracles"; the street door shuts.

## Close reading

### 1. The diagnosis

> I mean I passed from father's hands into yours. You arranged everything according to your taste; and I got the same tastes as you; or I pretended to—I don't know which—both ways, perhaps; sometimes one and sometimes the other.

**Observe.** "Passed from father's hands into yours": Nora as an object handed on. Then a sentence that keeps qualifying itself ("or I pretended to—I don't know which—both ways, perhaps").

**Interpret.** The uncertainty is the point: she cannot tell whether her tastes were ever hers. A person who cannot locate her own preferences has, in effect, no self yet.

**Function.** This is what she means by leaving to "educate myself." The departure is the logical consequence of the diagnosis.

### 2. The duties argument

> HELMER. Before all else you are a wife and a mother.
> NORA. That I no longer believe. I believe that before all else I am a human being, just as much as you are—or at least that I should try to become one.

**Observe.** Nora repeats Helmer's "before all else" and changes the end. Then she qualifies: "or at least that I should try to become one."

**Interpret.** The parallel phrasing sets two definitions of a person side by side. The qualification keeps Nora honest: she does not claim to have become a full human being, only that she must try.

**Function.** The play's conflict shifts from plot (will the secret come out?) to argument (what is a wife, and what is a person?). That is why critics have called Act III a discussion rather than a dénouement.

## Q3: from prompt to plan in five minutes

Every Q3 prompt has the same skeleton. It describes a situation ("a character who recognizes that a role is false"; "a symbol that changes meaning"; "a conflict between loyalty and conscience"), asks you to choose a work, and asks you to analyze how that element "contributes to an interpretation of the work as a whole."

**Step 1: choose the work.** Ask which work you know best whose central situation matches the prompt. Do not pick the most impressive work; pick the one where you can name four or five moments, from beginning to end, without hesitation.

**Step 2: answer the prompt in one sentence about meaning.** Not "Nora realizes she is a doll" but "Nora's recognition that she has been a doll-wife frees her only by costing her everything the play first called happiness, so Ibsen makes self-knowledge both victory and loss."

**Step 3: build the line of reasoning.** Three steps that move, for example: the role as it is set up; the moment it breaks; what the break means. Each step is a body paragraph.

**Step 4: place the evidence.** Two specific moments per step, from different parts of the work. Precise references are fine; short exact phrases you are sure of are better.

**Step 5: check the end.** Your conclusion should arrive at "the work as a whole." If it only says what the character did, rewrite the thesis.

## The year's works for Q3

**A Doll's House.** Roles and recognition; secrets; marriage and money; the individual against social law; a dynamic heroine and a static husband. Key moments: the lark and the macaroons; "something in reserve"; Helmer's speech on lying mothers; Nora and Anna; the tarantella; the failed "miracle"; "doll-wife"; "duties towards myself"; the rings; the door.

**The Great Gatsby.** Self-invention; a dream against time; class; a narrator's divided judgment; static and dynamic characters. Key moments: Nick's opening concession; the green light in chapter 1; "San Francisco"; the reunion and "again a green light on a dock"; James Gatz and the "Platonic conception"; the Plaza ("I did love him once—but I loved you too"); the vigil; the empty funeral; "careless people"; "boats against the current."

**Frankenstein.** Creation and responsibility; isolation; knowledge and ambition; doubled characters; nested narrators. Key moments: Walton's ambition in the letters; Victor fleeing his creation; Justine's trial and Victor's silence; the creature's education with the De Laceys; his rejection; the demand for a companion; the destroyed female; Elizabeth's death; the pursuit into the ice; Walton turning back.

**Hamlet.** Delay; revenge and conscience; performance and seeming; corruption; a character's change. Key moments: the Ghost's command; the "antic disposition"; the play-within-the-play; sparing Claudius at prayer; Polonius killed behind the arras; Ophelia's madness; the graveyard; Hamlet's acceptance before the duel; the final deaths.

**The short stories.** Tight, single-situation works that suit focused prompts. "Young Goodman Brown" (a journey that destroys faith; a community's hidden sins); "The Yellow Wall-Paper" (confinement, perception, resistance); "Bartleby, the Scrivener" (passive refusal; a narrator's conscience); "The Tell-Tale Heart" (guilt and self-betrayal); "Miss Brill" (illusion and recognition). Short stories are allowed but risk thin evidence; use them when the prompt fits them precisely.

## Model theses on one prompt

**Prompt.** *A character comes to recognize that a role he or she has played, or been given, is false. Analyze how that recognition contributes to an interpretation of the work as a whole.*

- **A Doll's House.** "Nora's recognition that she has been a 'doll-wife' turns the comic domestic world of the first two acts into evidence against itself, so that Ibsen's play becomes an argument that a marriage can be real only between two people who have first become persons."
- **The Great Gatsby.** "Nick's recognition, by the end, that his pose of tolerance concealed a carelessness of his own (Jordan's 'bad driver') lets Fitzgerald indict Tom and Daisy without exempting the narrator, so that the novel's final 'we' implicates everyone in Gatsby's dream and its failure."
- **Frankenstein.** "When the creature, watching the De Laceys, recognizes that he has been made a monster by others' horror rather than by nature, Shelley shifts the novel's moral weight from the creature's crimes to his maker's abandonment, making Victor's refusal of the father's role the true origin of the tragedy."
- **Hamlet.** "Hamlet's recognition that he cannot be the conventional revenger the Ghost demands, visible in his self-reproach and his sparing of Claudius at prayer, turns Shakespeare's revenge play into a tragedy of conscience in which the hero's delay is his humanity."

Each thesis names the work, answers the prompt's specific situation, implies a line of reasoning and makes a claim about the whole work.

## From reading to writing: a full body paragraph with sufficient evidence

> Nora's recognition depends on the failure of a hope Ibsen plants long before the last act, so the play makes her awakening an argument rather than a sudden mood. **[Claim: one step in the line of reasoning.]** In Act II Nora tells Mrs. Linden that "the miracle" is coming, and later that "there's something glorious in waiting for the miracle": she expects Helmer, once he knows, to take the guilt on himself. **[Evidence 1: from the middle of the play.]** When he instead rages about his own ruin and then, once the bond is returned, forgives her as a man gains a wife who is "at once his wife and his child," the miracle fails twice: first in his anger, then in his relief. **[Evidence 2: from the climax.]** Nora names exactly this when she tells him she stopped loving him "this evening, when the miracle did not happen; for then I saw you were not the man I had imagined." **[Evidence 3: the recognition itself, in her words.]** The three moments together show that Nora leaves not on impulse but because a test she had set for the marriage, in secret, for years, has been answered. **[Commentary: explains how the evidence works together and why it matters to the claim.]** The "doll's house" collapses because it could not bear the truth it was built to hide. **[Link to the work as a whole.]**

**Why this is sufficient.** Three pieces of evidence, from Act II, the climax and the recognition itself; each is placed under the claim and explained. A weaker paragraph would quote "doll-wife" and stop.

## Common misreadings and Q3 traps

- **"Nora leaves because Helmer is cruel."** He is kind, as she says. She leaves because kindness has kept her a child.
- **"The ending says women should abandon their families."** The play makes a narrower and harder claim: Nora cannot be a good mother until she has become a person. Helmer's last hope shows the door is not simply shut.
- **Choosing a work you half remember.** Wrong names and invented scenes undercut Row B. Prepare two works thoroughly.
- **Writing a thesis about the work's plot, not its meaning.** "Gatsby loves Daisy" is not an interpretation.
- **Using the prompt's words as your thesis.** Restating the prompt earns no point.
- **Retelling the plot.** Commentary must explain, not narrate.
