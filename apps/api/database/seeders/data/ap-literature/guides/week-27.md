## What you need to be able to do

- **6.B** Identify a metaphor, including compressed ones a single word carries ("guttering"), and explain what it makes the reader see, feel or believe. In these poems metaphor is not decoration; it is how each poet argues.
- **7.E** Write so that your argument is easy to follow: embed short quotations in your own grammar, quote verse correctly, choose precise verbs, keep the literary present tense, and use transitions that show how ideas relate (especially *while*, *whereas*, *both ... but* in a comparison).

In plain language: this week you put the whole unit together in the form the exam uses. Q1 gives you a poem, or occasionally two, and 40 minutes. You need a claim, a line of reasoning built from the poem's techniques, and clean, controlled sentences that carry it.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Metaphor | An implied comparison that treats one thing as another without "like" or "as". |
| Extended metaphor (conceit) | A metaphor developed across several lines or a whole poem (Hardy's ship-and-iceberg marriage). |
| Implied metaphor | A comparison carried by a single verb or adjective ("guttering", "slouches"). |
| Vehicle and tenor | The image used (vehicle: a sea) and the thing it describes (tenor: a gas cloud). |
| Petrarchan sonnet | An octave (abbaabba) and a sestet, with a turn at line 9 (Millay). |
| Shakespearean sonnet | Three quatrains and a couplet (McKay). |
| Witness poetry | Poetry that testifies to suffering the speaker has seen and insists the reader see it too. |
| Point-by-point comparison | Organising a comparison so that each paragraph treats both texts on one idea. |
| Embedded quotation | A quotation built into the grammar of the writer's own sentence. |
| Literary present | Discussing what happens in a text in the present tense. |

## The texts in context

**Owen** (1893-1918) served as an infantry officer on the Western Front, was treated for shell shock at Craiglockhart War Hospital in 1917, where he met Siegfried Sassoon, returned to the front and was killed on 4 November 1918, a week before the Armistice. Almost none of his poetry appeared in his lifetime; Sassoon assembled *Poems* (1920). The course quotes that edition, which reads "Of gas-shells dropping softly behind" in line 8 and has a short line "Bitter as the cud" in the last stanza; many modern editions print later manuscript readings. The title and ending quote the Roman poet Horace (*Odes* III.2): "It is sweet and fitting to die for one's country."

**McKay** (1889-1948), born in Jamaica, published "If We Must Die" in *The Liberator* in July 1919, in the "Red Summer" of white mob attacks on Black neighbourhoods in Chicago, Washington and other cities. The poem names no race and no place, which is why readers in many later struggles have claimed it.

**Millay** (1892-1950) published the sonnet in *The Harp-Weaver and Other Poems* (1923), a year in which she became one of the best-known poets in America. It is not a war poem, but it is a poem of loss and of what memory keeps, and it belongs here as a study in the controlled metaphor and the Petrarchan turn.

**Hardy** wrote "The Convergence of the Twain" in 1912, weeks after the *Titanic* sank on its maiden voyage, for a fundraising performance for the families of the dead. It is not an elegy for the victims, whom it never mentions, but a meditation on human pride and indifferent fate. It shares Owen's suspicion of grand public language.

## Close reading: metaphor as argument

**1. Owen's sea.**

> Dim through the misty panes and thick green light,
> As under a green sea, I saw him drowning.

*Observe.* The "misty panes" are the lenses of a gas mask; the "thick green light" is the gas cloud seen through them. The comparison begins as a simile ("As under"), but "drowning" turns it into a statement of what is happening.

*Interpret.* Gas fills the lungs as water does, so a man dies of drowning in open air. The reader may never have seen gas, but everyone can imagine drowning.

*Function.* The metaphor makes witness possible. Owen's argument is that civilians who praise war have not seen it; the sea image lets them see it, and the dream stanza ("He plunges at me, guttering, choking, drowning") shows the image has never left the speaker. "Guttering", a candle sputtering out, compresses a second metaphor into one word.

**2. McKay's animals.** The sonnet opens by refusing a metaphor: "let it not be like hogs / Hunted and penned in an inglorious spot". The mob would make its victims livestock. McKay then turns the comparison on the attackers, "the mad and hungry dogs", "the monsters we defy", and ends with "Like men we'll face the murderous, cowardly pack". By the couplet the metaphor has been reversed: the hunted are men, the hunters a pack. The function of the metaphor is the poem's whole claim, that dignity is a matter of how one meets death.

**3. Millay's tree.** "Thus in the winter stands the lonely tree, / Nor knows what birds have vanished one by one, / Yet knows its boughs more silent than before". The octave confessed a paradox (she has forgotten her lovers, yet the rain is "full of ghosts"); the sestet finds an image that holds it. The tree cannot name the birds but feels the silence, and the final metaphor, "summer sang in me / A little while, that in me sings no more", turns the birdsong into her own lost youth. The volta makes feeling intelligible by placing it outside the self.

**4. Hardy's wedding.** The iceberg is the ship's "sinister mate"; their meeting is "The intimate welding of their later history"; they are "twin halves of one august event"; the collision is a "consummation" that "jars two hemispheres". A marriage is arranged in advance and completed on a set day, so the metaphor turns an accident into destiny. That is the poem's satire: "the Pride of Life that planned her" was planning a wedding it never imagined.

## From reading to writing: control (7.E)

Row B of the rubric rewards commentary, but commentary is only as good as the sentences that carry it. Five habits separate a controlled essay from a muddled one.

1. **Embed, do not drop.** Weak: *Owen describes the death. "He plunges at me, guttering, choking, drowning." This is powerful.* Strong: *Owen's participles, "guttering, choking, drowning," keep the soldier dying in the present of every dream.*
2. **Quote little.** Three to eight words are usually enough. Long quotations hide the words you are actually analysing.
3. **Quote verse correctly.** Use a slash for line breaks ("Thus in the winter stands the lonely tree, / Nor knows what birds have vanished"), brackets for changes, an ellipsis for omissions.
4. **Choose verbs that interpret.** Not *shows* and *uses*, but *exposes, reverses, dignifies, compresses, undercuts, insists, withholds*. The verb is often where the analysis lives.
5. **Signal the logic.** In a comparison, transitions do the arguing: *both ... but*, *whereas*, *while Owen ..., McKay ...*, *for this reason*, *by contrast*.

## The paired-poem essay

Q1 occasionally offers two poems. Plan for four minutes before you write.

- **Read both poems for the same question.** Here it is *attitude toward a noble death*. Note each speaker's answer in one phrase (Owen: a lie told to children; McKay: dignity chosen in resistance).
- **Find the hinge.** What do the poems share that makes their difference interesting? Both reject a death imposed by others; they disagree about whether it can be redeemed. That shared ground plus difference is your thesis.
- **Choose two or three points of comparison**, each a technique tied to the attitude: bodily imagery vs abstract "precious blood"; animal metaphor; address and structure (Owen's "My friend" and broken form, McKay's "O kinsmen!" and disciplined sonnet).
- **Organise point by point.** Each body paragraph treats both poems on one technique and ends by explaining what the difference shows.

A model comparative body paragraph:

> **Claim:** Both poets reject a death that degrades the body, but where Owen makes degradation the whole truth of war, McKay refuses to let it be the last word. **Evidence and commentary:** Owen forces his listener to "watch the white eyes writhing" and "hear ... the blood / Come gargling from the froth-corrupted lungs," and his comparisons, a face "like a devil's sick of sin," a life "guttering" out, insist that nothing in such a death is noble. McKay names the same threat in his first line, death "like hogs / Hunted and penned," but only to reject it; the attackers become "mad and hungry dogs," and by the closing couplet his people meet "the murderous, cowardly pack" "Like men." **Link to thesis:** Owen's animal and devil images strip the dying soldier of dignity to expose "The old Lie," whereas McKay's reversed animal metaphor gives dignity back to the dying, which is why the two poets, starting from the same horror, reach opposite verdicts on whether a death can be noble.

*Why it works:* every sentence keeps both poems in view; quotations are short and embedded; the verbs (*forces, insist, reject, strip, gives back*) interpret; the last sentence answers the prompt.

## Common misreadings

- **"Owen hates the soldiers' patriotism."** His target is those who teach the young that dying is "sweet and fitting", not the soldiers, whom he pities.
- **"McKay's poem glorifies violence."** It accepts that the speakers will probably die ("What though before us lies the open grave?") and argues about how. It is a poem of self-defence and dignity under attack.
- **"Millay's speaker is heartless."** Forgetting the lovers is her confession, but the "quiet pain" and the silent tree show grief. The candour is the feeling.
- **"Hardy mourns the Titanic's passengers."** The poem never mentions them. Its subject is vanity and fate; the tone is closer to grim satire than elegy.
- **Calling every comparison a metaphor.** "Like old beggars" is a simile; "drowning" in air is a metaphor. Labels matter less than function, but get them right.
- **Writing two essays in one.** In a paired prompt, a paragraph that never mentions the second poem is half a paragraph.

## Connections and looking ahead

This unit began with a speaker who reveals more than he intends (week 25) and ends with speakers who are determined to make us see (Owen) or to act (McKay). The weeks between taught you to find what objects carry (symbol) and where poems turn (shift); this week makes metaphor the engine of argument and makes your own writing the subject.
