## What you need to be able to do

- **6.B** Identify a metaphor, including one sustained over a whole poem, map its details onto its subject, and explain what the comparison lets the poet argue that plain statement could not.
- **7.B** Write a thesis for the Q1 poetry essay that makes a defensible interpretive claim and previews a line of reasoning.
- Put the unit together: plan and write a full Q1 essay in about 40 minutes, using form, imagery, personification, allusion and metaphor as evidence for an argument rather than as a checklist.

In plain language: by the end of the week you should be able to read an unseen poem, decide what it is really saying, and write a first paragraph that tells a reader exactly where your essay is going.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Tenor, vehicle | The subject of a metaphor (the lovers' souls) and the thing it is compared to (a pair of compasses). |
| Extended metaphor | A metaphor developed point by point across many lines or a whole poem. |
| Conceit | An ingenious, elaborate comparison; the *metaphysical conceit* links unlike things with argumentative precision. |
| Metaphysical poets | Seventeenth-century poets such as Donne and Herbert, known for wit, argument and learned imagery. |
| Blank verse | Unrhymed iambic pentameter ("Mending Wall"). |
| Pun | A word used in two senses at once ("rest" in "The Pulley"). |
| Defensible claim | An interpretation a reasonable reader could dispute but that the text can support. |
| Line of reasoning | The ordered set of claims that carries an essay from thesis to conclusion. |
| Commentary | The writer's explanation of how evidence supports the claim: the part of an essay that scores. |
| Sophistication | Row C: a complex, sustained understanding of the poem and its tensions, or a vivid, persuasive style. |

## The texts in context

**Donne, "A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning" (1633).** Izaak Walton's life of Donne says the poem was written for Donne's wife, Anne, before he travelled to the Continent in 1611; like almost all of his poems, it circulated in manuscript and was printed only after his death, in *Poems* (1633). Nine quatrains of iambic tetrameter rhyming ABAB make a calm, stepwise argument: do not weep, because our love is not the kind that weeps. Donne draws his figures from death-beds, theology, astronomy, metallurgy and geometry, and the compass in the last three stanzas has become the most famous conceit in English.

**Frost, "Mending Wall" (1914).** The first poem in *North of Boston*, published in London while Frost was living in England. Forty-five lines of blank verse in the rhythms of New England speech describe a spring ritual: two neighbours walk their boundary and lift fallen stones back into place. The wall is never declared a symbol, which is why it can carry so much.

**Herbert, "The Pulley" (1633).** Herbert, a priest in a small parish near Salisbury, died in 1633; his poems were published that year as *The Temple*. "The Pulley" retells creation as a scene in which God pours blessings from a glass and stops before the last one. Its four five-line stanzas (ABABA, lines of three, five, five, five and three stresses) narrow at each close. Its title names the governing metaphor, which never appears in the poem itself.

## Close reading

**1. The compass (6.B).**

> If they be two, they are two so
> As stiff twin compasses are two;
> Thy soul, the fixed foot, makes no show
> To move, but doth, if the other do.

*Observe:* "If they be two" concedes a point (our souls may seem separate) in order to answer it. The vehicle is a pair of drawing compasses: two legs, joined at the top. *Interpret:* the tenor is the lovers' souls. Her soul is the fixed foot at the centre, his the foot that travels. *Function:* the conceit is chosen because a compass can do what the speaker needs his argument to do. Its legs can separate widely without disconnecting; the fixed foot moves when the other moves ("leans and hearkens after it"); and the travelling foot draws a circle that brings it home: "Thy firmness makes my circle just, / And makes me end where I begun." The metaphor does not decorate the argument, it *is* the argument. Each mechanical fact is a reason not to mourn.

Notice that Donne has already offered a different metaphor for the same separation two lines earlier: the souls "endure not yet / A breach, but an expansion, / Like gold to airy thinness beat." Gold can be hammered into leaf as thin as air without a gap. Two vehicles, one tenor: the gold argues that distance stretches love without breaking it; the compass argues that it will bring him back. When you write, keep the two distinct.

**2. The wall (6.B, with 5.A).**

> He is all pine and I am apple orchard.
> My apple trees will never get across
> And eat the cones under his pines, I tell him.
> He only says, "Good fences make good neighbors."

*Observe:* the wall in "Mending Wall" is first entirely literal: stones, gaps, hunters, frost. *Interpret:* the speaker's joke about apple trees makes the practical point that this wall protects nothing. The neighbour answers with a proverb. *Function:* the wall becomes an extended metaphor for divisions maintained by custom rather than need: "Before I built a wall I'd ask to know / What I was walling in or walling out". Frost develops the figure through its details: the gaps no one sees made, the yearly ritual, the neighbour "like an old-stone savage armed" who "moves in darkness". But the speaker is the one who calls the neighbour to mend the wall each spring, and the neighbour gets the last line. A thesis on this poem should not simply side with the speaker; the metaphor's meaning includes his own part in the ritual.

**3. The pulley, unnamed (6.B, with 5.A).**

> "Yet let him keep the rest,
> But keep them with repining restlessness;
> Let him be rich and weary, that at least,
> If goodness lead him not, yet weariness
> May toss him to my breast."

*Observe:* "rest" has changed meaning since stanza 2. There it was repose, the blessing left "in the bottom" of the glass; here "the rest" is the remainder, all the other gifts. The pun becomes explicit in "restlessness". *Interpret:* humanity keeps the rest and lacks rest. *Function:* the title's pulley now makes sense. A pulley lifts a load when its rope is pulled down; weariness pulls on human beings, and that pull will "toss" them up "to my breast". What looks like a flaw in creation is its mechanism. Note "If goodness lead him not": God's design expects human failure and plans around it, which is a darker and more interesting idea than "God wants us to be restless".

## From reading to writing: the Q1 essay

The Q1 prompt gives you a poem and asks you to "analyze how the poet uses literary elements and techniques to convey" something specific: an attitude, a relationship, a complex understanding. You have about 40 minutes. A workable plan:

1. **Read the prompt first, then the poem twice** (5 minutes). Underline the prompt's key phrase ("a complex understanding of human restlessness"). Your thesis must answer *that*, not the poem in general.
2. **Annotate for movement** (5 minutes). Mark the turn, shifts in tone or address, and the two or three figures that carry the most weight. Ask: what changes between the start and the end?
3. **Write the thesis** (3 minutes). Claim + how. *Through [technique 1], [technique 2] and [technique 3], [poet] presents [subject] as [complex idea].* The techniques should follow the poem's order or its logic so they become your paragraphs.
4. **Write two or three body paragraphs** (22 minutes). Each opens with a claim tied to the thesis, uses two or three short embedded quotations, and spends at least as many words explaining as quoting.
5. **Close by extending, not repeating** (3 minutes). What does the poem's ending or its tension suggest beyond your thesis? This is often where Row C is earned.

**A model introduction for "The Pulley."**

> Herbert's "The Pulley" retells creation to explain an experience every reader knows: the sense that no blessing, however rich, brings peace. Through the image of God pouring out every gift but the last, a pun that lets humanity keep "the rest" but not rest, and the pulley named only in the title, Herbert argues that human restlessness is not a flaw in creation but a deliberate mercy, the force by which God draws people back to himself.

*Annotations.* Sentence 1 frames the poem's subject in one line without plot summary. Sentence 2 is the thesis: it is defensible (a reader could argue the poem is more about divine control than mercy), it answers the prompt's phrase ("complex understanding of human restlessness"), and it names three techniques in the order the essay will treat them: the glass (stanzas 1-2), the pun (stanzas 2-4), the title conceit (the ending).

**A model body paragraph (Row B, the 4-point kind).**

> The pun on "rest" carries the poem's paradox. In the second stanza "Rest" is the last blessing left "in the bottom" of God's glass, repose itself; by the last stanza "the rest" means everything else God has given, strength, beauty, wisdom, honour, pleasure. Humanity is allowed to "keep the rest" only "with repining restlessness", so the same syllable names what we have and what we lack. The wordplay is not a joke for its own sake: it shows abundance and dissatisfaction arriving together, as the line "Let him be rich and weary" makes explicit. Herbert's God keeps rest back not out of meanness but because, as he says, a contented man "would adore my gifts instead of me".

**A weak version (Row B, 2 points).** "Herbert uses a pun on the word 'rest'. He says 'Yet let him keep the rest.' This is a pun because rest has two meanings. This shows that people are restless." The paragraph names the device and quotes, but its commentary only restates the evidence. It never explains what the two meanings are or why the pun matters to the claim.

**Thesis checklist (7.B).** Does it answer the prompt's specific phrase? Could someone disagree? Does it go beyond "the poet uses imagery"? Does it suggest an order for the essay? If yes to all four, write.

## The rubric, row by row

| Row | Points | What earns it on a poetry essay |
|---|---|---|
| A · Thesis | 0-1 | A defensible interpretation that responds to the prompt. Not a summary, not a restatement, not a list of devices. |
| B · Evidence and commentary | 0-4 | 4: specific evidence throughout and consistent commentary that explains how it supports a line of reasoning. 3: the same, with some uneven or general commentary. 2: some specific evidence, but commentary mostly repeats, paraphrases or lists. 1: mostly summary. |
| C · Sophistication | 0-1 | Sustained complexity: exploring a tension or ambiguity, an alternative reading, a wider context, or a consistently vivid and persuasive style. |

Most of the score lives in Row B, and Row B rewards explanation. Readers are not counting devices; they are reading for how well you explain the relationship between a choice the poet made and the meaning you claim.

## Common misreadings

- **Treating the compass as a comparison of the lovers to a tool.** The vehicle is chosen for how its legs relate; the comparison is to the *relationship*.
- **Merging Donne's gold and compass into one figure.** They are two metaphors for one separation, and they argue different things.
- **Reading "Mending Wall" as a simple anti-wall poem.** The speaker starts the mending, and the neighbour has the last word. The poem keeps both positions in play.
- **Taking "Elves" seriously, or ignoring it.** It is a joke that shows the speaker knows the "Something" is beyond naming.
- **Missing the pun on "rest".** Without it, the last stanza reads as a contradiction.
- **Reading "The Pulley" as God being cruel.** The poem presents the withholding as purposeful and loving ("toss him to my breast"), though a sophisticated essay can explore its harder edge.
- **Writing a thesis that lists devices.** "Donne uses metaphor, imagery and diction to convey his view of love" earns no Row A point.
