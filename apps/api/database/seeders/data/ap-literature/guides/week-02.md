# Week 02 · Setting and Plot in "Araby"

**CED skills:** 2.A Identify and describe specific textual details that convey or reveal a setting · 3.A Identify and describe how plot orders events in a narrative
**Big Ideas:** Setting (SET), Structure (STR); in review, Character (CHR) and Narration (NAR)
**Read:** James Joyce, "Araby," in *Dubliners* (1914), [Project Gutenberg #2814](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2814)
**Spiral:** Week 1's questions about narrators come back with a new kind of voice: a first-person narrator who was inside the story as a boy and tells it as a man. Reading character through details (1.A) now extends to reading a character through the places around him.

## What you need to be able to do

- **2.A** Point to specific details of place, time, light, sound and objects, and describe what they convey about the setting and the people in it.
- **3.A** Describe how the plot orders events: what comes first, what is summarised and what is dramatised, and where the story stops.
- **Review 4.A, 4.B, 1.A** Describe the retrospective first-person narrator, explain what his point of view hides and reveals, and read the boy's motives through his actions and imaginings.

In plain language: be able to say what North Richmond Street, the dark house, the market and the bazaar *feel* like, why the story spends a page on one evening and a sentence on months, and why it ends exactly where it does.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Setting | The time, place and social world of a narrative, including its physical details and atmosphere. |
| Atmosphere | The emotional feeling a setting gives off, built from concrete details. |
| Plot | The arrangement of events as the reader receives them. |
| Exposition | Background the reader needs: place, people, situation. |
| Rising action | Events that build tension toward a turning point. |
| Climax | The point of highest tension or decisive change. |
| Resolution (denouement) | What happens after the climax; many modern stories omit it. |
| Pacing | The speed of narration: summary covers time quickly, scene renders it in detail. |
| Retrospective narration | A narrator telling events from a later point in life. |
| Epiphany | A sudden moment of insight; Joyce's term for revelations like the one that ends "Araby." |
| Quest | A journey toward a valued goal, the shape of many romances; "Araby" borrows and empties it. |

## The text in context

James Joyce (1882-1941) wrote the stories of *Dubliners* between 1904 and 1907, but publishers balked at their frankness and their use of real Dublin places and people, and the book did not appear until 1914. It is a sequence of fifteen stories arranged, Joyce said, in four groups: childhood, adolescence, mature life and public life. "Araby" is the third and last of the stories of childhood. Like most of the book, it is set in a real neighbourhood (North Richmond Street, where Joyce himself lived as a boy) and ends not with an event but with a moment of recognition.

A real bazaar called Araby, with an "Oriental" theme, was held in Dublin in May 1894 to raise money for a hospital. For a boy on a dull street, its name promised the exotic East of romances and stories. That gap between promise and reality is the story's subject.

## Close reading

### 1. The opening: setting as a closed world

> North Richmond Street, being blind, was a quiet street except at the hour when the Christian Brothers' School set the boys free. An uninhabited house of two storeys stood at the blind end, detached from its neighbours in a square ground. The other houses of the street, conscious of decent lives within them, gazed at one another with brown imperturbable faces.

**Observe.** The first word about the street is "blind." School is described as something that sets boys "free," implying confinement. An empty house stands at the closed end. The other houses are personified: "conscious of decent lives," they "gazed at one another with brown imperturbable faces."

**Interpret.** "Blind" means a dead-end street, but the word also hints at not seeing. The street is quiet, respectable and closed. The houses' "faces" are brown (drab) and imperturbable (unmoved, smug about their decency).

**Function.** Before any character appears, the setting establishes the world the boy needs to escape: dull, proper, enclosed. The adult narrator's irony is already present in "conscious of decent lives." Skill 2.A asks you to see that a setting is never just background; here it defines the pressure that will drive the boy's fantasy.

### 2. Light and colour: setting and mood

> When the short days of winter came dusk fell before we had well eaten our dinners. When we met in the street the houses had grown sombre. The space of sky above us was the colour of ever-changing violet and towards it the lamps of the street lifted their feeble lanterns.

**Observe.** Dusk comes early; the houses grow "sombre"; the sky is "ever-changing violet"; the street lamps lift "feeble lanterns" toward it.

**Interpret.** The boys live in darkness relieved by small, weak lights. The violet sky is the one beautiful, shifting element above the dull street; the lamps "lift" toward it as if reaching for something beyond them.

**Function.** This pattern of darkness and small lights recurs: the lamplight on Mangan's sister's neck and hair, the dark rainy room where the boy prays her name, the darkened bazaar, the voice calling "that the light was out." Following it lets you explain the ending, where the boy, "Gazing up into the darkness," finally sees himself clearly.

### 3. The market: setting by contrast

> We walked through the flaring streets, jostled by drunken men and bargaining women, amid the curses of labourers, the shrill litanies of shop-boys who stood on guard by the barrels of pigs' cheeks [...]. These noises converged in a single sensation of life for me: I imagined that I bore my chalice safely through a throng of foes.

**Observe.** One long sentence piles up sights and sounds: flaring lights, drunks, hagglers, curses, "shrill litanies," "pigs' cheeks." Then a short turn: "These noises converged in a single sensation of life for me." The boy imagines himself carrying a "chalice" through "foes."

**Interpret.** The narrator calls such places "the most hostile to romance." The boy does not escape the market; he absorbs it into his fantasy, turning a shopping errand into a sacred quest.

**Function.** The setting is built to contrast with the boy's inner world, and the contrast characterises him: he lives in imagination so intensely that even pigs' cheeks become part of a holy mission. The word "litanies," borrowed from prayer for the cries of shop-boys, shows how religious language saturates everything he perceives, which prepares for the bazaar's "silence like that which pervades a church after a service."

### 4. The ending: plot that stops at recognition

> I heard a voice call from one end of the gallery that the light was out. The upper part of the hall was now completely dark.
>
> Gazing up into the darkness I saw myself as a creature driven and derided by vanity; and my eyes burned with anguish and anger.

**Observe.** A voice announces "the light was out." The hall goes dark. In the last sentence the boy looks up into the darkness and "saw myself." The diction is harsh: "creature," "driven," "derided," "vanity." His eyes burn "with anguish and anger."

**Interpret.** The romance has collapsed: the bazaar was a shabby, closing commercial hall where a shop girl flirted with two young men and barely noticed him. What he sees is not the bazaar's failure but his own: the quest was built on "vanity," on his own self-dramatising imagination.

**Function.** The plot stops here. There is no scene of the boy going home or seeing Mangan's sister again; the epiphany is the climax and the ending. Skill 3.A asks how a plot orders events, and the answer here is that everything (the exposition, the devotion, the promise, the delays) is arranged to arrive at this single moment of self-recognition. The retrospective narrator matters too (review 4.B): "driven and derided by vanity" is the judgment of a man looking back, fused with the boy's burning eyes.

## Plotting "Araby"

It helps to map the story on one line:

1. **Exposition**: North Richmond Street, the dead priest's house and its books, winter evenings of play.
2. **Rising action**: the boy's devotion to Mangan's sister (summarised over weeks); the first conversation; his promise to bring her something from Araby.
3. **Complication**: the long Saturday wait; the uncle's late return; the visitor; the slow train.
4. **Climax and epiphany**: the dark bazaar, the flirting shop girl, "No, thank you," the light going out, the final sentence.

Notice the pacing: months of watching from the front parlour take a paragraph; one evening takes a third of the story. Where the narration slows down is where the meaning gathers.

## From reading to writing

A model claim-evidence-commentary paragraph on setting:

> [Claim] In "Araby," Joyce uses the bazaar's setting to turn the boy's sacred quest into an empty ritual. [Evidence] When he finally arrives, "Nearly all the stalls were closed and the greater part of the hall was in darkness," and he recognises "a silence like that which pervades a church after a service." [Commentary] The boy has treated his love as religion, praying the girl's name and imagining himself a bearer of a chalice; now the shrine he travelled to is a church whose service is already over. The sacred feeling is still there, but only as silence and dark. [Evidence] Minutes later, the shop girl's flirtation ("O, I never said such a thing!") fills that silence with trivial chatter. [Commentary] The setting has replaced worship with commerce and gossip, and the boy's devotion has nowhere left to go. [Link] The darkness of the hall is the outward form of the disillusionment he is about to name.

A weak version would say: "The bazaar is dark and quiet. This shows the boy is sad. Then the lights go out." Each sentence is true, but none explains *how* the details of setting convey the feeling, and nothing connects them to the boy's earlier religious imagination.

## Common misreadings

- **Treating setting as backdrop.** In "Araby" every place expresses a state of mind. Ask what a place makes the boy feel and what it shows about him.
- **Calling the girl the story's subject.** She is barely sketched. The subject is the boy's imagination and its collapse.
- **Assuming the narrator is a boy.** The vocabulary and judgments belong to an adult looking back.
- **Over-symbolising.** Start with the literal (a dead-end street, a closing bazaar), then build interpretation from it. "The apple tree means the Garden of Eden" is defensible only if you connect it to the story's pattern of lost innocence.
- **Expecting a resolution.** The story ends at the epiphany on purpose; arguing that it is "unfinished" misses its design.

## Connections and looking ahead

Week 1 asked what an intrusive outside narrator does; "Araby" shows what a reflective inside narrator does. Both stories turn on a gift that fails: Della's chain and Jim's combs become useless, the boy never buys his gift at all. Next week, "The Necklace" adds a third version, a borrowed jewel whose loss reshapes a life, and asks you to explain why the plot puts its events in that particular sequence (3.B). Later in the year, setting returns in Hawthorne's "Young Goodman Brown" and Gilman's "The Yellow Wall-Paper" (weeks 11-12), where skill 2.B asks for the function of setting in a whole narrative.

On the exam, setting questions are a small but steady share of the multiple-choice section ("The description of the street in lines 1-5 primarily serves to..."), and the Q2 prose essay often hands you a passage dense with setting. The habit to build: quote a detail of place, then explain what it reveals about a character or a feeling.

## Self-check

1. Give two meanings of "blind" in the first sentence and explain how the story supports each.
2. List three details of light or darkness from different parts of the story and explain how they form a pattern.
3. Why does the story spend so long on the Saturday evening? What does the pacing do?
4. Which words in the last sentence belong to the boy's experience, and which to the adult narrator's judgment?
5. How is the market "hostile to romance," and how does the boy make it part of his romance anyway?
6. Write a one-sentence thesis about the function of the bazaar as a setting.
