## What you need to be able to do

- **5.C** Identify a symbol (an object, image or action that carries meaning beyond itself) and explain its function: what it stands for and what it does at its moment in the plot.
- **3.B** Explain why events come in the order they do: what each event makes possible, what the audience knows at each point, and how a later event changes the meaning of an earlier one.

Act III is the play's turning point. Before the Mousetrap, Hamlet investigates; after it, events run away with him. The three central scenes, the play, the prayer and the closet, only make full sense read in order.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Symbol | Something concrete that carries a larger meaning, made significant by repetition, placement or the characters' attention. |
| Play within the play | A performance staged inside the play; here *The Murder of Gonzago*, renamed *The Mousetrap*. |
| Dumb show | A mimed preview of a play's action, an older convention; the Mousetrap begins with one. |
| Dramatic irony | The audience knows something a character does not. |
| Reversal (peripeteia) | A turn of fortune in which an action produces the opposite of what was intended. |
| Climax (of the play scene) | Claudius rising and calling for lights: the moment the test succeeds. |
| Arras | A tapestry wall-hanging; a hiding place for eavesdroppers. |
| Closet | In Shakespeare, a private room, not a cupboard: Gertrude's closet is her chamber. |

## The text in context

Act III follows directly from Hamlet's plan at the end of Act II. In 3.1 Rosencrantz and Guildenstern report failure, Claudius and Polonius set Ophelia in Hamlet's path and hide, Hamlet speaks "To be, or not to be," and the meeting with Ophelia turns to bitterness. Claudius, having watched, decides that "Madness in great ones must not unwatch’d go" and resolves to send Hamlet to England.

In 3.2 Hamlet coaches the players, asks Horatio to watch the King, and sits at Ophelia's feet trading bawdy jokes. The play begins with a dumb show that mimes the whole crime, then the Player King and Queen talk in stiff rhymed couplets about love and remarriage. When the villain Lucianus pours poison into the sleeping King's ears, Claudius rises and calls for lights. Hamlet, exultant, mocks Rosencrantz and Guildenstern with a recorder and is summoned to his mother.

In 3.3 Claudius commissions the schoolfellows to take Hamlet to England, then tries and fails to pray. Hamlet finds him kneeling and decides not to kill him. In 3.4 Polonius hides behind the arras in Gertrude's closet. Hamlet, hearing a cry, stabs through it and kills him, then confronts his mother with two portraits until the Ghost appears, seen only by Hamlet, to "whet thy almost blunted purpose" (3.4.126-127 in the Folger numbering). He leaves dragging the body.

## Close reading

### 1. The aside that changes the play scene (3.1)

Polonius remarks that people often "sugar o’er / The Devil himself" with a show of devotion. Claudius, aside:

> O, ’tis too true!
> How smart a lash that speech doth give my conscience!
> The harlot’s cheek, beautied with plastering art,
> Is not more ugly to the thing that helps it
> Than is my deed to my most painted word:

**Observe.** It is the first time Claudius admits guilt, and only the audience hears it. The simile turns his public eloquence ("my most painted word") into make-up on a diseased face.

**Interpret.** The speech confirms the Ghost. It also gives Claudius a conscience, which will matter in 3.3.

**Function (3.B).** Because this aside comes before the Mousetrap, the audience watches the play scene knowing what Hamlet is trying to learn. Suspense shifts from "is he guilty?" to "will Hamlet see it, and then what?" Order of information is a tool of plot.

### 2. The play as a symbol (3.2)

Hamlet's advice to the players sets out what drama is for:

> to hold, as ’twere, the mirror up to nature; to show virtue her own image, scorn her own image, and the very age and body of the time his form and pressure.

When Claudius asks the title of the play, Hamlet answers: "The Mouse-trap. Marry, how? Tropically."

**Observe.** Two images of what the play is: a mirror and a trap. "Tropically" means figuratively; Hamlet is telling us to read the play as a figure.

**Interpret.** The Mousetrap reproduces the Ghost's account (a garden, a sleeping king, poison in the ear, a usurper who wins the wife) so that Claudius will see his own deed reflected and betray himself.

**Function (5.C).** The play within the play symbolises the power of art to expose hidden truth. It also extends the play's oldest symbol. The Ghost said that "the whole ear of Denmark" had been "Rankly abus’d" by the story of his death, and he was killed by poison poured "in the porches of my ears." Now Hamlet pours a poisonous truth into the King's ears through a play. At the climax, as Lucianus poisons the sleeper, Hamlet glosses it: "He poisons him i’ the garden for’s estate." The King rises. "Give me some light:—away!"

### 3. The prayer and the closet: a sequence (3.3-3.4)

Alone, Claudius tries to repent and cannot, because he still has what the murder won him: "My crown, mine own ambition, and my queen" (3.3.59 in the Folger numbering). Hamlet enters, sees him kneeling, draws, and stops. To kill him at prayer would send him to heaven, he reasons, which would be "hire and salary, not revenge" (3.3.84 in the Folger numbering). He goes. Then Claudius rises:

> My words fly up, my thoughts remain below:
> Words without thoughts never to heaven go.
> (3.3.102-103 in the Folger numbering)

In the next scene, hearing a voice behind the arras, Hamlet strikes at once: "How now? a rat? Dead for a ducat, dead" (3.4.29 in the Folger numbering). It is Polonius.

**Observe.** Two chances at a hidden enemy in two consecutive scenes. In the first, Hamlet has time and does not act; in the second, he has no time and does.

**Interpret.** The couplet after his exit shows that the reason for waiting was based on an appearance: the prayer was empty. The killing that follows shows that action without thought finds the wrong victim.

**Function (3.B).** The order creates both irony and causation. Irony: the audience alone learns the prayer failed, so Hamlet's restraint looks tragically mistaken. Causation: the impulsive killing makes Hamlet the killer of a father, creates Laertes's revenge, drives Ophelia mad and gives Claudius his excuse to ship Hamlet abroad. Every line of Act IV grows from these two minutes.

### 4. The portraits (3.4)

> Look here upon this picture, and on this,—
> The counterfeit presentment of two brothers.
> (3.4.63-64 in the Folger numbering)

**Observe.** "Counterfeit presentment" means a painted likeness, and also something false that presents itself.

**Interpret.** The pictures turn Act I's "Hyperion to a satyr" into objects Gertrude must look at.

**Function (5.C).** They symbolise the choice she made, and they bring back the play's concern with surfaces, since a portrait is a seeming. They are the closest Hamlet comes to a Mousetrap for his mother.

## From reading to writing

**Prompt-style task:** Explain how a symbol in Act III contributes to the meaning of *Hamlet* as a whole.

**Model paragraph:**

> In *Hamlet* the ear is where corruption enters, and the Mousetrap makes that symbol literal. The Ghost told Hamlet that he was killed by poison poured "in the porches of my ears," and that "the whole ear of Denmark" had been "Rankly abus’d" by the official story of his death: the crime against the body and the lie told to the nation are the same act. Act III fills that ear with listeners: Claudius and Polonius hide to hear Hamlet and Ophelia, and Polonius hides again behind the arras in the closet. Hamlet's response is to fight poison with poison. His play pours its own story into the King's ears, and at the instant Lucianus empties his vial Claudius rises and calls for lights. The symbol therefore carries the play's argument about truth: in a court where hearing is corrupted, truth can only be administered like a counter-poison, and those who listen in secret, like Polonius, may die of it.

**Why it works.** The symbol is traced through several scenes (1.5, 3.1, 3.2, 3.4) and the commentary says what it stands for at each point. The final sentence makes a claim about the whole play.

**A weak version:** "The poison in the play is a symbol of evil. Claudius used poison and Lucianus uses poison. This shows that Claudius is evil." It names a symbol, gives it a vague meaning and never explains how it works in the plot.

## Common misreadings

- **"Hamlet spares Claudius because he is merciful."** He spares him to damn him more surely later. The motive is savage, whatever else it covers.
- **"Claudius's reaction to the dumb show proves he is innocent."** The text records no reaction to the mime; he breaks at the spoken poisoning. Critics have argued about why, but the scene gives Hamlet and Horatio enough.
- **"'To be, or not to be' is only about suicide."** It is also about whether to endure or resist, and about why thought prevents action. Its generality is the point.
- **"Hamlet knows Polonius is behind the arras."** He asks "Is it the King?" He strikes at whoever is there, hoping it is Claudius.
- **"Gertrude sees the Ghost."** She does not, and her honest blindness reopens the question of Hamlet's sanity.
- **"A closet is a cupboard."** It is a private room.
