## What you need to be able to do

- **1.E** Read a character in a play the way you read Gatsby last week: through what she says and does, to whom, and when. A play has no narrator to steer you, so the complexity must be inferred from speech, action and stage directions.
- **3.F** Explain how a conflict works: where it comes from, what kinds of conflict are stacked on one another, and what the conflict forces characters to reveal.

Acts I and II of *A Doll's House* are a masterclass in building a conflict from a secret. By the end of Act II, the audience knows everything Helmer does not, and the letter that will tell him is sitting in his locked letter-box.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Stage direction | The playwright's instructions in brackets: setting, movement, tone ("[Thoughtfully, half-smiling]"). Evidence, like any line. |
| Dramatic irony | The audience knows what a character does not; Helmer's lectures on debt to the wife who saved him with a loan. |
| Exposition | Background information; Ibsen delivers it through Nora's conversation with Mrs. Linden. |
| Confidant(e) | A character to whom another reveals secrets, so the audience can hear them (Mrs. Linden). |
| Foil | A character whose contrast reveals another (Mrs. Linden, Krogstad and Anna are all foils for Nora). |
| Internal and external conflict | A struggle within a character, and a struggle with an outside force. |
| Well-made play | The 19th-century form built on a secret, a letter and timed suspense, which Ibsen uses and then breaks. |
| Problem play | A drama that puts a social question (here, marriage and the law) at the centre. |

## The text in context

Henrik Ibsen (1828-1906) was Norway's great dramatist. *A Doll's House* (*Et Dukkehjem*) was published in Copenhagen in December 1879 and staged there the same month. Its ending, in which a wife leaves her husband and children, caused scandal across Europe; a German production was even given a revised ending in which Nora stays. William Archer, the Scottish critic who did most to introduce Ibsen to English audiences, published his translation in 1889 and revised it for his *Collected Works*. In his translation Mrs. Linde is "Mrs. Linden" and the nurse is "Anna"; you will see other names in modern versions.

Under Norwegian law at the time, a married woman could not take out a loan without her husband's consent. That is why Nora needed a co-signer and why she forged her dying father's name. Keep that fact in view: Nora's crime is partly a product of the law that made her a legal minor.

## Close reading

### 1. The first exchange

> HELMER. [*In his room.*] Is that my lark twittering there?
> NORA. [*Busy opening some of her parcels.*] Yes, it is.
> HELMER. Is it the squirrel frisking around?

**Observe.** Helmer speaks from his study without looking; Nora answers while doing something else (opening parcels, hiding macaroons a moment later). His names are of small creatures.

**Interpret.** The marriage is a game both play: he names her a pet, she plays the part. The staging (he behind a door, she busy) shows them not quite present to each other.

**Function (1.E).** Nora's cheerful compliance and her secret macaroons are the first evidence of a double self: the role she performs and the woman acting it.

### 2. "Something in reserve"

> Of course I mean when Torvald is not so much in love with me as he is now; when it doesn't amuse him any longer to see me dancing about, and dressing up and acting. Then it might be well to have something in reserve. [*Breaking off.*] Nonsense! nonsense! That time will never come.

**Observe.** The dash before "not so pretty" just before this, the list of performing verbs, the economic phrase "in reserve," and then a stage direction that cuts her off.

**Interpret.** Nora knows Torvald's love is tied to her charm and that charm fades. She thinks like a banker ("reserve") about her own marriage, then refuses the thought.

**Function.** The passage reveals a complexity the play will spend two more acts unfolding: Nora is not naive about her marriage; she has chosen not to look straight at what she knows.

### 3. Helmer's lecture on lying parents

> HELMER. Because in such an atmosphere of lies home life is poisoned and contaminated in every fibre. Every breath the children draw contains some germ of evil.
> NORA. [*Closer behind him.*] Are you sure of that?

**Observe.** Helmer speaks in generalities and medical metaphors ("poisoned," "contaminated," "germ"). Nora's stage direction puts her behind him, where he cannot see her face; her question is short.

**Interpret.** Helmer means Krogstad; every word lands on Nora. He goes on: "Nearly all cases of early corruption may be traced to lying mothers."

**Function (3.F).** Dramatic irony turns an external threat (Krogstad) into an internal conflict (Nora's fear for her children). The act ends with her alone: "Corrupt my children!—Poison my home! [...] It's not true! It can never, never be true!"

### 4. Nora and Anna

> NORA. Do you think they do? Do you believe they would forget their mother if she went quite away?
> ANNA. Gracious me! Quite away?

**Observe.** Nora asks a hypothetical question. Anna's repetition of "Quite away?" shows alarm. Nora then asks about Anna's own child, given up for a "good place."

**Interpret.** "Quite away" holds two possibilities, death and departure, and Nora is weighing both. Anna's story shows another way a woman has been forced to leave her child: poverty.

**Function.** The scene deepens Nora's internal conflict and plants the ending. When she leaves in Act III, the audience has already watched her imagine it, and has heard that children may not forget.

## From reading to writing

**Prompt.** How does Ibsen use Nora's conversation with Mrs. Linden to reveal a more complex character than the one Helmer sees?

**Model paragraph.**

> Ibsen uses Nora's confession to Mrs. Linden to show that the "lark" of the opening scene is a calculating, proud and partly self-deceived adult. **[Claim: sets the surface against the depth.]** Asked whether she will ever tell Torvald, Nora says she might, "when I'm—not so pretty," when it no longer amuses him "to see me dancing about, and dressing up and acting," because "then it might be well to have something in reserve." **[Evidence: short quotations woven into the sentence.]** The verbs of performance show that Nora knows her place in the marriage is to entertain, and "in reserve" is the language of a banker, the very profession her husband has just entered: she has been managing her marriage as she managed the loan. **[Commentary: explains diction and links it to the play's world.]** Yet the stage direction "[Breaking off]" and her "Nonsense! nonsense!" show her refusing the knowledge she has just voiced, so the complexity is not only that she is cleverer than Helmer thinks, but that she has chosen not to think about what she knows. **[Commentary: uses a stage direction as evidence, and adds a second layer.]** That half-buried knowledge is what Act III will force into the open. **[Link: function in the play.]**

**What a weaker version looks like.** "Nora is not a silly woman. She borrowed money to save her husband's life. She says she will tell him when she is not pretty. This shows she is smart." The claim is reasonable, the evidence is summary, and the commentary ("this shows she is smart") is too general to earn more than a point.

## Common misreadings

- **"Nora is simply childish in Act I."** She plays childish. Her secret, her work and her calculations prove otherwise.
- **"Helmer is a cartoon villain."** He is affectionate, principled in his way and sincerely proud of his home. That makes his limits more telling.
- **"Krogstad is the antagonist, so he is the conflict."** He triggers it. The deeper conflicts are Nora against herself and Nora's morality against the law, and eventually against her husband's.
- **"Nora committed fraud for selfish reasons."** She forged to spare her dying father and save her husband. The play asks whether the law should care; Krogstad says it does not.
- **"The stage directions are just instructions to actors."** On the exam and in your essays they are evidence.
