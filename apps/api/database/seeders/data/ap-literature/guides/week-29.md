# Week 29 · Revelations and the Reunion, The Great Gatsby Chapters 4-6

**CED skills:** 3.E Explain the function of a significant event or related set of significant events in a plot
**Big Ideas:** Structure (STR), with Character (CHR) and Narration (NAR) in support
**Read:** F. Scott Fitzgerald, *The Great Gatsby* (1925), chapters IV-VI ([Project Gutenberg #64317](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317))
**Spiral:** Nick's perspective (week 28), reliability (weeks 18 and 22), sequence and order (weeks 2-3, 19), symbol (weeks 19, 24, 26), simile and metaphor (weeks 6 and 16), allusion (weeks 14 and 25).

## What you need to be able to do

- **3.E** Pick out the events that matter in a stretch of plot and explain what each one *does*: what it reveals, what it changes, what it sets up, and how it connects to the work's larger meaning.

Chapters 4-6 are built almost entirely of significant events, and most of them are revelations. The reader learns Gatsby's past three times over, watches him get what he wants, and sees what that costs him. Your task this week is to move beyond "and then" into "which means that."

A useful set of questions for any event:

1. **What do we know after it that we did not know before?**
2. **What relationship has changed?**
3. **What does it make us expect, hope or fear?**
4. **What would the work lose if it were cut?**

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Significant event | An event that changes knowledge, relationships or direction; the AP stem is "the function of" the event. |
| Revelation | An event in which hidden information comes to light; in *Gatsby*, usually a story someone tells. |
| Turning point | The event after which the action moves in a new direction. Chapter 5, the reunion, at the centre of nine chapters. |
| Delayed exposition | Background released late and in pieces rather than at the start. |
| Achronological order | Events told out of time order; Nick moves Gatsby's history forward "with the idea of exploding those first wild rumours." |
| Foil | A character whose contrast sharpens another (Wolfshiem to Gatsby). |
| Mock-heroic | Inflated language for a small thing ("ordered divine retribution to stand by"). |
| Allusion | A reference to another text; "His Father's business" echoes Luke 2:49. |

## The text in context

The middle third of the novel moves from rumour to fact. In chapter 4 Gatsby drives Nick to New York and tells him a polished life story; Nick laughs inwardly, then is half convinced by a medal from Montenegro and a photograph from Oxford. At lunch Gatsby introduces Meyer Wolfshiem, who he says fixed the 1919 World Series. That afternoon, at the Plaza Hotel, Jordan Baker tells Nick about Louisville in 1917: Daisy, eighteen, in love with a young officer named Jay Gatsby; her marriage to Tom in June 1919 with a $350,000 string of pearls; the letter she wept over the night before. Gatsby, Jordan explains, bought his house so that Daisy would be just across the bay.

Chapter 5 is the reunion at Nick's house: rain, embarrassment, joy, a tour of the mansion, the famous shirts. Chapter 6 gives the truth about James Gatz and Dan Cody, then shows Tom and Daisy at one of Gatsby's parties, where Daisy is repelled by West Egg and Tom begins asking questions. It ends with Gatsby insisting that the past can be repeated.

## Close reading

### 1. The legend and the laugh (chapter 4)

> "I am the son of some wealthy people in the Middle West—all dead now." [...] "What part of the Middle West?" I inquired casually. "San Francisco."

**Observe.** A geographical blunder, delivered without a flicker, then Nick's "I see." Then Gatsby's rajah speech, which Nick says made him "restrain my incredulous laughter."

**Interpret.** Gatsby's history is made from phrases ("educated at Oxford," "like a young rajah"), not facts. But within pages Gatsby produces a real medal and a real photograph, so the legend is not wholly false.

**Function (3.E).** This first account sets the reader's baseline: Gatsby is a fabricator, perhaps a criminal. Each later revelation will revise that baseline. The event teaches the reader how to read the rest of the book: by comparing versions.

### 2. The green light goes out of the sky (chapter 5)

> Compared to the great distance that had separated him from Daisy it had seemed very near to her, almost touching her. It had seemed as close as a star to the moon. Now it was again a green light on a dock. His count of enchanted objects had diminished by one.

**Observe.** "Had seemed" twice; then the flat "Now it was again." The vocabulary of fairy tale ("enchanted objects") meets the language of counting ("diminished by one").

**Interpret.** The light was a symbol only while it marked distance. With Daisy at his side, Gatsby loses the thing he looked at for five years. The simile "as close as a star to the moon" is double-edged: in the night sky the two look close, but in fact they are vastly apart.

**Function.** The sentence marks the turning point in miniature. Possession reduces enchantment; the novel's second half will play out that law on a larger scale.

### 3. Tumbling short (chapter 5)

> There must have been moments even that afternoon when Daisy tumbled short of his dreams—not through her own fault, but because of the colossal vitality of his illusion. It had gone beyond her, beyond everything.

**Observe.** "There must have been" is Nick inferring, not seeing. The dash protects Daisy ("not through her own fault"). "Colossal vitality" gives the illusion life and size, as if it were a living thing that had outgrown its object.

**Interpret.** Gatsby has not loved Daisy so much as built something with her name on it, "decking it out with every bright feather that drifted his way." The real woman cannot compete with the creation.

**Function.** This explains in advance why the reunion cannot hold, and it shifts the reader's question from "Will he win her?" to "What will happen when she fails to match the dream?" Every event in chapter 7 answers that question.

### 4. The history moved forward (chapter 6)

> He told me all this very much later, but I've put it down here with the idea of exploding those first wild rumours about his antecedents [...]

> The truth was that Jay Gatsby of West Egg, Long Island, sprang from his Platonic conception of himself.

**Observe.** Nick openly breaks chronology and gives a reason. The history he places here is the real one: a North Dakota farm boy, James Gatz, who renamed himself at seventeen when he rowed out to Dan Cody's yacht.

**Interpret.** "Platonic conception" alludes to Plato's ideal forms: Gatsby made himself as a copy of an ideal. "Son of God ... about His Father's business" (Luke 2:49) adds a note of devotion, quickly undercut by "a vast, vulgar, and meretricious beauty."

**Function.** Placed just after the reunion, the truth about Gatsby's origins shows the reader what the reunion really is: one invented self trying to recover a moment from its own past. The arrangement also prepares the irony of chapter 6's ending, when Gatsby insists, "Can't repeat the past? Why of course you can!"

## From reading to writing

**Prompt.** Explain the function of the reunion in chapter 5.

**Model paragraph.**

> The reunion in chapter 5 functions as the novel's turning point because it fulfils Gatsby's dream and, in the same afternoon, begins to empty it. **[Claim: names the function precisely.]** When Gatsby shows Daisy the green light, Nick suspects that "the colossal significance of that light had now vanished forever," and the next sentences reduce it to "again a green light on a dock." **[Evidence.]** The light could only mean something across water; once Daisy is on his arm, the symbol has no distance left to measure, and Gatsby's "count of enchanted objects" drops by one. **[Commentary: explains the mechanism.]** Nick extends the same law to Daisy herself, who "tumbled short of his dreams ... because of the colossal vitality of his illusion," so the problem the rest of the novel faces is no longer reaching her but making her equal to five years of imagining. **[Commentary: widens the claim from object to person.]** That is why Gatsby, in chapter 6, demands that she tell Tom she never loved him: only by erasing the years can the real Daisy fit the dream, and the impossibility of that demand drives the confrontation at the Plaza. **[Link to later events: shows consequence.]**

**What a weaker version looks like.** "In chapter 5 Gatsby and Daisy meet again. Gatsby is nervous and almost breaks the clock. Then he shows her his house and his shirts and she cries. This is important because it is the first time they have met in five years." Everything is accurate, and none of it is about function. It answers "what happens?" not "what does it do?"

## Common misreadings

- **"Chapter 4 proves Gatsby is a liar."** It proves he embroiders. The medal and the photograph are real, and chapter 7 confirms a short stay at Oxford. The truth is mixed, and that is the point.
- **"Daisy disappoints Gatsby because she has changed."** Nick says the opposite: "not through her own fault." The illusion grew.
- **"The novel is told in order."** Nick openly rearranges: Gatsby's history comes in chapter 6, the full Louisville story in chapter 8.
- **"The green light always means money."** Here it means distance and longing; it becomes "again a green light on a dock" when the distance closes. Symbols in *Gatsby* shift with context.
- **"Wolfshiem is just colour."** He is a foil and a clue: the polish of Gatsby's manner against the source of his money.

## Connections and looking ahead

- **Earlier in the course.** *Frankenstein* also tells its backstory late and through embedded narrators; the creature's story (week 9) revises what Victor told us, as Jordan's and Nick's versions revise Gatsby's. In *Hamlet*, the play-within-the-play (week 19) is a significant event designed to reveal; Gatsby's tea party at Nick's is designed to reunite, and reveals more than intended.
- **This unit.** Week 30 follows the conflicts that the reunion set loose: the Plaza Hotel confrontation, Myrtle's death and the ending. You will ask which characters change and which do not (1.B), and how conflict (3.F) produces meaning.
- **The exam.** Section I passages from novels often include a key event; the stem "the function of the event in the passage as a whole" asks exactly what this week practises. In Q3, an event-based argument ("the moment a character's illusion meets reality") is a reliable approach to many prompts.
