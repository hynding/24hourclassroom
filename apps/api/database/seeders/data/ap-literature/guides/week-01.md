# Week 01 · Narrator and Character in "The Gift of the Magi"

**CED skills:** 1.A Identify and describe what textual details reveal about a character's perspective and motives · 4.A Identify and describe the narrator · 4.B Identify and explain the function of point of view
**Big Ideas:** Character (CHR), Narration (NAR)
**Read:** O. Henry, "The Gift of the Magi" (1905), [Project Gutenberg #7256](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/7256)
**Spiral:** This is the first week of the course. Everything here (the claim-evidence-commentary paragraph, the habit of separating narrator from author, reading a character through details) returns every week until May.

## What you need to be able to do

- **1.A** Point to a specific detail (an action, a possession, a line of speech, a reaction) and say what it reveals about a character's perspective and motives.
- **4.A** Describe the narrator: inside or outside the story, how much it knows, how present its voice is, what attitude it takes.
- **4.B** Explain what the point of view does: what it lets the reader see, what it withholds, and how it shapes the reader's response.

In plain language: by Friday you should be able to say "the narrator is an outside voice who keeps joking with us," and then explain why that matters, using a short quotation as proof.

## Starting the course: what AP Literature asks of you

The AP English Literature and Composition exam is three hours long. **Section I** is 55 multiple-choice questions on five passages, a mix of prose fiction and poetry, in one hour; it is 45% of your score. **Section II** is three essays in two hours, 55% of your score: **Q1** analyses a poem, **Q2** analyses a passage of prose fiction, and **Q3** is a literary argument about a work you choose. Each essay is scored out of 6: one point for a defensible **thesis** (Row A), up to four for **evidence and commentary** (Row B), and one for **sophistication** (Row C).

The course is organised around seven skill categories from the College Board's Course and Exam Description: character, setting, structure, narration, word choice and imagery, comparison (simile, metaphor and their relatives) and literary argument. Each week puts two or three skills in focus, and quizzes keep returning to earlier ones. This week starts with the two that carry the most weight on the multiple-choice section: **character** and **narration**.

**Close reading** is the method behind all of it. It means reading a short stretch of text slowly, more than once, and asking of every choice that catches your attention: *what is there, what does it suggest, and why is it there?* We will practise it in three steps every week:

1. **Observe**: name exactly what is on the page (a word, a detail, a sentence pattern).
2. **Interpret**: say what it suggests about a character, a narrator or an idea.
3. **Explain the function**: say what the choice *does* in the story or to the reader.

Most weak answers stop at step 1 ("the narrator uses humour"); most strong ones reach step 3 ("the humour keeps Della's poverty from becoming pitiful, so we admire her rather than pity her").

**The claim-evidence-commentary paragraph** is the unit of AP writing. A **claim** is an arguable statement about the text. **Evidence** is a short quotation or precise reference. **Commentary** explains how the evidence supports the claim. The essay rubric gives four of its six points to evidence and commentary, so commentary is where the score is. A good rule of thumb: write at least as many words of commentary as you quote.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Narrator | The voice that tells a story. Created by the author; not the author. |
| Speaker | The equivalent term for the voice of a poem. |
| First-person narration | Told by an "I" who takes part in the events. |
| Third-person narration | Told by a voice outside the events, which refers to the characters as "he," "she," "they." |
| Omniscient | Able to report any character's thoughts and any event. |
| Limited | Staying close to one character's perceptions and thoughts. |
| Intrusive (editorial) narrator | A narrator who comments, generalises, jokes or addresses the reader. |
| Point of view | The position from which a story is told, and what that position lets the reader know. |
| Direct characterization | The narrator states what a character is like. |
| Indirect characterization | The reader infers character from actions, words, possessions and others' reactions. |
| Motive | What a character wants, and why, at a given moment. |
| Tone | The narrator's attitude toward the subject. |
| Situational irony | An outcome that reverses what was expected. |
| Allusion | A reference to a well-known person, story or text outside the work (here, the biblical magi). |

## The text in context

"The Gift of the Magi" was written by William Sydney Porter, who published as **O. Henry**. It first appeared in a New York newspaper in December 1905 and was collected the next year in *The Four Million*, a book of stories about ordinary New Yorkers; the title answered a society figure's claim that only four hundred people in the city were worth noticing. O. Henry wrote hundreds of short newspaper stories at speed, and his trademarks are visible here: a chatty, showy narrator, comic vocabulary, a plot built to a surprise ending, and a closing turn toward sentiment.

The story is short and easy to follow, which is exactly why it is useful in week 1. Nothing about the plot is difficult; everything interesting is in *how* it is told. That makes it ideal for practising skills 4.A and 4.B, which account for the largest share of the multiple-choice section.

## Close reading

### 1. The opening sentences: a character built from details

> One dollar and eighty-seven cents. That was all. And sixty cents of it was in pennies. Pennies saved one and two at a time by bulldozing the grocer and the vegetable man and the butcher until one's cheeks burned with the silent imputation of parsimony that such close dealing implied.

**Observe.** The story opens with a sum, not a name. The sentences are fragments ("That was all."). The pennies come "one and two at a time," and the saving has made someone's "cheeks burn." The diction shifts from plain ("the grocer and the vegetable man") to inflated ("silent imputation of parsimony").

**Interpret.** Before we learn her name, we know Della's situation and what she has been willing to endure. "Bulldozing" shopkeepers is hard work for small gains, and the burning cheeks show shame at looking stingy. The fragments sound like someone counting, and recounting, in dismay.

**Function.** The details establish Della's motive (to give Jim a present) and her method (sacrifice of pride, repeated many times). They prepare us to believe the much larger sacrifice she is about to make. That is skill 1.A in action: a small detail reveals perspective and motive.

### 2. The narrator steps forward

> There was clearly nothing to do but flop down on the shabby little couch and howl. So Della did it. Which instigates the moral reflection that life is made up of sobs, sniffles, and smiles, with sniffles predominating.

**Observe.** "Flop" and "howl" are comic, slangy verbs. "So Della did it" is deadpan. Then the narrator interrupts with a "moral reflection," alliterating on *s* and ending on a joke.

**Interpret.** This is not Della's thought; it is the narrator's. The voice stands beside the scene and comments on it, like a storyteller who pauses to wink at the audience.

**Function.** The comment sets the story's tone: amused but sympathetic. By turning one woman's crying fit into a general truth about "life," the narrator invites us to smile at Della's distress without despising her. It also establishes the narrator as a character in its own right, with opinions we will learn to trust. That is skill 4.A (describe the narrator) moving into 4.B (explain the function).

### 3. Restriction: what the narrator does not tell us

> Jim stopped inside the door, as immovable as a setter at the scent of quail. His eyes were fixed upon Della, and there was an expression in them that she could not read, and it terrified her. It was not anger, nor surprise, nor disapproval, nor horror, nor any of the sentiments that she had been prepared for.

**Observe.** The narrator compares Jim to a hunting dog frozen at the scent of game. The expression is one "she could not read." Then comes a list of four emotions it is *not*.

**Interpret.** The narrator could tell us what Jim feels (we have already seen it report Della's feelings in detail) but chooses not to. We see Jim from Della's side of the room.

**Function.** The restriction keeps the reader exactly as puzzled and frightened as Della, so that Jim's explanation (he has sold the watch to buy combs for the hair she no longer has) lands as a double surprise. The point of view is not a neutral camera; it is the machine that produces the story's irony. Any time an AP question asks about the *function* of point of view, ask: *what does this way of telling hide, and what does the hiding make possible?*

### 4. The last word: a narrator who reframes the ending

> But in a last word to the wise of these days let it be said that of all who give gifts these two were the wisest. Of all who give and receive gifts, such as they are wisest. Everywhere they are wisest. They are the magi.

**Observe.** Just before this, the narrator called the couple "two foolish children in a flat who most unwisely sacrificed for each other the greatest treasures of their house." Now "wisest" is repeated three times, and the final sentence is four words long.

**Interpret.** The narrator voices the practical judgment (the gifts are useless, so the sacrifices were foolish) only to overturn it. By the measure of love, giving up one's greatest treasure for another person is the wisest gift of all. The allusion to the magi, the wise men who brought gifts to the infant Jesus, crowns them.

**Function.** Without this paragraph the story would end on a joke or a sigh. The narrator's commentary converts situational irony into a moral paradox and tells the reader how to feel. Whether that is moving or too neat is a fair question for discussion; either way, it shows how much power an intrusive narrator has over meaning.

## From reading to writing

Here is a model paragraph answering the question *How does the narrator shape our response to Della?* The labels in brackets are for you, not for an essay.

> [Claim] O. Henry's narrator treats Della's poverty with humour, and the humour makes her more admirable, not less. [Evidence] When Della collapses on "the shabby little couch" to "howl," the narrator turns her tears into a "moral reflection that life is made up of sobs, sniffles, and smiles, with sniffles predominating." [Commentary] The joke is aimed at life in general, not at Della: by making her trouble universal, the narrator lets us smile at the scene while staying on her side. [Evidence] The same light touch governs the description of the flat, which "did not exactly beggar description" but had that word "on the lookout for the mendicancy squad." [Commentary] Grand words for a cheap room make the poverty comic rather than grim, so when Della sells her hair the reader responds to her love and resolve, not to her misery. [Link] Humour, in this story, is a form of respect.

Why it works: the claim is arguable (someone could argue the humour belittles Della); each quotation is short and woven into a sentence; each is followed by commentary that explains *how* the words produce the effect; the last sentence ties the evidence back to the claim.

A weak version of the same paragraph:

> The narrator in "The Gift of the Magi" is third person. He says life is made up of "sobs, sniffles, and smiles, with sniffles predominating." This shows that the narrator is funny. Della is poor and sells her hair to buy Jim a present.

The first sentence labels without arguing. The quotation is followed by commentary that repeats it ("funny"). The last sentence is plot summary. On the AP rubric this would earn at most one of the four evidence-and-commentary points.

## Common misreadings

- **"The author says..."** when you mean the narrator. O. Henry built this voice; analyse it as part of the story.
- **"It's third person, so it's omniscient."** Third-person narrators can be limited or selective. This one reports Della's fears and keeps Jim's secret.
- **"The narrator uses 'I', so it's first person."** The narrator says "here I have lamely related to you," but never takes part in events. A first-person narrator is a participant.
- **Reading the humour as mockery.** The jokes keep the tone light; the verdict is admiration.
- **Summarising instead of analysing.** "Della sells her hair" is plot. "Selling the hair shows that she values Jim's happiness above her own pride" is analysis.
- **Confusing tone and mood.** The narrator's tone is wry and affectionate; the mood a reader feels may be amusement, suspense or tenderness.

## Connections and looking ahead

Next week's story, James Joyce's "Araby," is told by a first-person narrator: a man looking back on his boyhood. You will be asked the same 4.A and 4.B questions about a very different voice, one that never jokes with the reader and whose distance from his younger self is the key to the ending. In week 3, Guy de Maupassant's "The Necklace" pairs a plot built, like O. Henry's, on a final reversal with a narrator who judges its heroine more coolly.

On the exam, skills 4.A-4.D (narration) make up roughly a quarter of the multiple-choice section, and nearly every prose passage carries questions of the form "The narrator's attitude toward X is best described as..." or "The shift in point of view in line N serves to...". The Q2 prose essay almost always rewards a student who can explain how the telling shapes the reader's response, and skill 1.A, reading character through details, is the backbone of any Q2 response.
