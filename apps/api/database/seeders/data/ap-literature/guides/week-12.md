# Week 12 · Perspective and Power in "The Yellow Wall-Paper"

**CED skills:** 4.C Identify and describe details, diction or syntax that reveal a narrator's perspective · 1.D Describe how textual details reveal nuances and complexities in characters' relationships
**Big Ideas:** Narration (NAR), Character (CHR), Setting (SET)
**Read:** Charlotte Perkins Gilman, "The Yellow Wall-Paper" (1892): [Project Gutenberg #1952](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1952) (published there as "The Yellow Wallpaper")
**Spiral:** Week 1's first-person and point-of-view work (4.A, 4.B) becomes this week's close attention to *how* a narrator's sentences reveal her; week 11's character-and-setting relationship (2.C) moves indoors; week 5's contrast (3.D) and Unit 3's thesis and commentary (7.B, 7.C) carry into the Q2 essay.

## What you need to be able to do

- **4.C** Point to specific diction (word choice), syntax (sentence structure, punctuation, paragraphing) and selected details, and say what they reveal about how the narrator sees things, including what she does not see or will not say.
- **1.D** Describe how small details (an endearment, an interruption, a silence) reveal the nuances of a relationship, especially one that is not simply good or bad.

Gilman's story is a gift for both skills because its narrator is also its only witness. Every word John speaks reaches us through her, and every judgment she makes of him is softened, excused or hidden in a parenthesis. Q2 prompts very often ask about "the complex relationship between" two characters; this week you practise the word *complex*.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Perspective | The way a narrator or character sees events, shaped by knowledge, feeling, values and situation. |
| Diction | Word choice; here the narrator's "of course," John's "little girl," the paper's "bulbous eyes." |
| Syntax | Sentence structure: length, order, punctuation, paragraphing. Gilman's one-line paragraphs and dashes are syntax. |
| Epistolary / journal form | A narrative told through letters or diary entries; each entry is written at a moment, without knowledge of what comes next. |
| Unreliable narrator | A narrator whose account the reader cannot fully accept; reliability can differ from one kind of report to another. |
| Dramatic irony | The reader understands more than the narrator does. |
| Rest cure | A nineteenth-century treatment for "nervous" women: isolation, bed rest, feeding, and no intellectual work. |
| Condescension | Kindness that assumes the other person is lesser; John's tenderness often takes this form. |

## The text in context

Charlotte Perkins Gilman (1860-1935), writer, lecturer and economist of women's work, published the story in the *New England Magazine* in January 1892 under the name Charlotte Perkins Stetson. After the birth of her daughter in 1885 she suffered a severe depression and in 1887 was treated by the Philadelphia neurologist S. Weir Mitchell, who prescribed rest and told her, by her own later account, to limit intellectual life. In a short 1913 piece, "Why I Wrote The Yellow Wallpaper," she said the story was meant to save others from that treatment.

Two facts shape the reading. First, the narrator's husband and her brother are both physicians "of high standing"; medical and family authority are the same authority. Second, the story is a **secret journal**. The narrator is forbidden to "work" (write) and does so anyway, hiding the pages from John and from his sister Jennie. Every entry is therefore an act of resistance, and the form lets us watch her sentences change from week to week.

## Close reading

### 1. A criticism in brackets

> John is a physician, and perhaps—(I would not say it to a living soul, of course, but this is dead paper and a great relief to my mind)—perhaps that is one reason I do not get well faster.

**Observe.** The sentence begins a criticism ("perhaps"), stops, opens a parenthesis, and repeats "perhaps" before finishing. Inside the brackets: "of course," "dead paper," "relief."

**Interpret.** The criticism is so dangerous that she has to wall it off before she can say it. "Dead paper" means paper that cannot repeat what it hears, unlike the living people around her.

**Function (4.C).** The syntax shows her perspective as much as the words do: she sees clearly that John's disbelief may be harming her, and she also believes she is not allowed to say so. The reader learns to listen for what she brackets.

### 2. Praise that contains a complaint

> He is very careful and loving, and hardly lets me stir without special direction.

**Observe.** Two compliments ("careful," "loving") joined by "and" to a restriction ("hardly lets me stir").

**Interpret.** She presents the restriction as part of the love. The plain conjunction refuses to separate them.

**Function (1.D).** Here is the relationship's complexity in one sentence. John is not a cartoon tyrant; he is attentive, and his attention is control. An essay that calls him simply cruel or simply kind misses what the sentence shows.

### 3. A sentence cut off

> "Better in body perhaps"—I began, and stopped short, for he sat up straight and looked at me with such a stern, reproachful look that I could not say another word.

**Observe.** The dash breaks her sentence; John says nothing; a look does the silencing; "could not" rather than "did not."

**Interpret.** She was about to say that her mind is not better. He hears the direction of the sentence and stops it without a word.

**Function (1.D, 4.C).** Gilman lets punctuation record power. The unspoken half of the sentence is the story's subject, the narrator's mind, and the relationship is defined by its being unspeakable. Note also what she writes next: "So of course I said no more." "Of course" again: her submission described as natural.

### 4. The paper looks back

> There is a recurrent spot where the pattern lolls like a broken neck and two bulbous eyes stare at you upside-down.

**Observe.** A simile of injury ("like a broken neck"), grotesque bodily diction ("lolls," "bulbous"), a shift to "you."

**Interpret.** The narrator gives the paper a body and a gaze. The pattern watches her, as she is watched by John and Jennie.

**Function (4.C).** Her descriptions of the paper are where her perspective shows its strain. Track them through the story: "committing every artistic sin," then a broken neck, then a fungus, then bars with a woman behind. The more she is denied other outlets, the more meaning she pours into the wall.

## From reading to writing

A Q2 prompt on this story might read: *analyze how Gilman uses literary elements and techniques to portray the complex relationship between the narrator and her husband.* The trap is to write about John alone. The relationship lives in the narrator's sentences about John as much as in his words to her.

**Model paragraph (on the night conversation):**

> Gilman shows that John's affection is the very means by which he controls his wife, and she makes the reader hear it in the shape of their exchange. When the narrator says that her appetite is worse "in the morning when you are away," she offers evidence John has never seen; he answers not with a question but with "a big hug" and "Bless her little heart! ... she shall be as sick as she pleases!" The embrace and the pet phrase are warm, yet the slide into the third person turns her into a child being discussed in her own presence, and "as she pleases" recasts illness as whim. The pattern sharpens when she begins, "Better in body perhaps"—and a "stern, reproachful look" ends the sentence for her. Her report that "of course I said no more" completes the scene: she narrates her silence as the natural outcome. The relationship is complex because neither tenderness nor control can be subtracted from it; every endearment is also a closing door.

*Why it works:* the claim names the complexity; evidence comes from both speakers; each quotation is followed by an explanation of a technique (third-person shift, dash, "of course"); the final sentence returns to the claim and sharpens it.

**Weak version:**

> John is very controlling in this passage. He says "Bless her little heart!" which shows he doesn't care about her. The narrator is sad because he won't leave.

*Why it scores low:* "doesn't care" contradicts the evidence (the line is affectionate); the analysis ignores the narrator's own diction; the last sentence summarizes rather than explains.

**A thesis checklist for Q2 relationship prompts:**

1. Name both people and the quality of the relationship, ideally with a tension in it (love/control, gratitude/resentment, compliance/resistance).
2. Point toward techniques you will analyze, but make the claim the subject of the sentence, not the list of devices.
3. Ask: could a thoughtful reader disagree? If not, it is summary.

## Common misreadings

- **"The narrator is crazy, so we can't believe anything."** Reliability differs by kind of report. Her quotations of John are consistent and specific; her claims about the paper are symptoms. Essays that distinguish the two are stronger.
- **"John is a villain."** He is sincere, attentive and socially respected. The story's force comes from showing harm done by ordinary, approved love.
- **"The wallpaper is just a symbol of patriarchy."** It can carry that meaning, but on the exam you must show how the text builds it: bars by moonlight, the woman behind, the barred windows, the narrator creeping. Earn the symbol from details.
- **"The ending is a happy escape."** The narrator feels triumphant ("I've got out at last"), but the reader sees a woman who has merged with a hallucination. Hold both perspectives.
- **Ignoring the journal form.** Each entry is written in ignorance of what comes next, which is why her "I'm feeling ever so much better!" is ironic.
