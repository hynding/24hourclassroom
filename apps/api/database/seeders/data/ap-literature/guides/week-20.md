## What you need to be able to do

- **3.A** Describe how the plot orders its events: what is staged and what is only reported, what is told later than it happened, and which lines of action run side by side.
- **1.E** Explain how characters' own choices and words reveal complexities, and what those complexities do for the play. This week the skill is spread across several characters: Hamlet, Laertes, Claudius, Ophelia and Gertrude.

Act IV is the act of consequences. Hamlet has killed Polonius, and every scene shows what that act sets in motion. It is also an act of absence: for most of it the hero is offstage, and the play's most dramatic events, a sea fight and a drowning, reach us only as reports.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Reported action | An event that happens offstage and is narrated: Hamlet's escape (letter, 4.6), Ophelia's death (Gertrude, 4.7). |
| Delayed disclosure | Telling an event later than it happened; how Hamlet discovered the death warrant is held back until 5.2. |
| Parallel plot | A second line of action that mirrors the first; Laertes's revenge for Polonius runs beside Hamlet's for his father. |
| Unbated | Not blunted. A practice foil had a button on its point; an unbated sword did not. |
| Elegy | A lament for the dead; Gertrude's willow speech works as one. |
| Q2 / F | The Second Quarto (1604-05) and First Folio (1623). The soliloquy "How all occasions" appears only in Q2. |

## The text in context

The act moves quickly. Gertrude tells Claudius that Hamlet, "Mad as the sea and wind, when both contend / Which is the mightier," has killed Polonius (4.1). Hamlet hides the body and mocks his captors, telling the King that Polonius is at supper, "Not where he eats, but where he is eaten" (4.3). Claudius ships him to England, and, alone, reveals that his sealed letters order England to execute him: "Do it, England."

On the way to the ship Hamlet meets Fortinbras's army crossing Denmark to fight for a patch of Polish ground "That hath in it no profit but the name," and speaks his last soliloquy, "How all occasions do inform against me" (4.4). This scene is almost entirely absent from the Folio, so the soliloquy survives only in the Second Quarto and in modern editions that combine the texts.

Then Hamlet disappears. Ophelia, mad with grief, sings of death and betrayal and hands out flowers (4.5). Laertes breaks in at the head of a crowd, demanding his father; Claudius calms him and wins him. Sailors bring Horatio a letter: Hamlet was captured by pirates and is back in Denmark (4.6). Claudius and Laertes plan a fencing match with an unbated, poisoned sword and a poisoned cup in reserve, and Gertrude enters with news that Ophelia has drowned (4.7).

## Close reading

### 1. Hamlet's last soliloquy (4.4)

> How all occasions do inform against me
> And spur my dull revenge! What is a man,
> If his chief good and market of his time
> Be but to sleep and feed? a beast, no more.
> [...] Rightly to be great
> Is not to stir without great argument,
> But greatly to find quarrel in a straw
> When honour’s at the stake.
> [...] O, from this time forth,
> My thoughts be bloody, or be nothing worth!

**Observe.** The army is the "occasion" that accuses him. He argues that reason was given to us to be used, then praises fighting over "a straw" when honour is at stake, though the cause in front of him is "an egg-shell" for which twenty thousand men will "Go to their graves like beds."

**Interpret.** He uses Fortinbras to shame himself, as he used the Player in Act II. But the argument is uneasy: the example he holds up is also absurd.

**Function (1.E).** Look at the last line. He vows bloody *thoughts*. The choice of word, spoken as he leaves Denmark under guard, shows the will and the gap at once. The complexity is the same one Act I set up, now at its sharpest: Hamlet can resolve in language more forcefully than anyone in the play, and the resolution stays in language.

### 2. Laertes returns (4.5)

> To hell, allegiance! Vows, to the blackest devil!
> (4.5.149 in the Folger numbering)

Claudius, facing him, tells Gertrude, "Let him go, Gertrude; do not fear our person: / There’s such divinity doth hedge a king" (4.5.137-138 in the Folger numbering).

**Observe.** Laertes does in one scene what Hamlet has not done in four acts: confronts the man he blames, at the head of a crowd. He throws allegiance, vows and his own salvation away in a line.

**Interpret.** He is the avenger without scruple, and that is exactly what makes him easy for Claudius to steer. Within a few minutes the King has redirected his fury at Hamlet.

**Function (1.E and 1.C).** Each man's speech reveals him. Laertes's rashness throws Hamlet's caution into relief, and shows its value: Hamlet has never been anyone's instrument. Claudius's line about divinity, from a man who killed a king, shows nerve and hypocrisy at once.

### 3. The plot against Hamlet (4.7)

Claudius proposes a fencing match in which Laertes may choose "A sword unbated." Laertes goes further: "I will do’t: / And for that purpose I’ll anoint my sword" with a poison he has bought. Claudius adds a cup prepared "for the nonce" in case the blade fails. Asked what he would do to prove himself his father's son, Laertes says he would cut Hamlet's throat "i’ the church"; Claudius answers, "No place, indeed, should murder sanctuarize."

**Observe.** Each conspirator makes the plan worse: the unbated sword, then the poison, then the cup.

**Interpret.** The church line is a deliberate echo. Hamlet would not kill a praying Claudius for fear of saving his soul; Laertes would murder in a church. The murderer Claudius agrees that no place should shelter a murderer.

**Function (3.A).** The audience watches the whole trap assembled while its victim is absent. When Hamlet returns in Act V, every friendly gesture toward him will be loaded with what we know.

### 4. Ophelia's death reported (4.7.190-207)

> Her clothes spread wide;
> And, mermaid-like, awhile they bore her up;
> Which time she chaunted snatches of old tunes;
> As one incapable of her own distress,
> [...] but long it could not be
> Till that her garments, heavy with their drink,
> Pull’d the poor wretch from her melodious lay
> To muddy death.

**Observe.** Flowers, song, a branch that breaks ("an envious sliver"), a "weeping brook," clothes that float and then drag.

**Interpret.** Nature is given the feelings (envy, grief) and the agency; Ophelia is "incapable of her own distress." Nobody chooses anything. Whoever saw so much apparently did nothing, and the speech never says who that was.

**Function (3.A, with 4.D and 5.D).** Reporting the death turns it into an elegy. The audience never sees a decision, so the question of suicide is left to the gravediggers and the priest in Act V. The final contrast, "melodious lay" and "muddy death," is the one moment the speech lets the horror through.

## From reading to writing

**Prompt-style task:** Explain how the ordering of events in Act IV shapes the audience's expectations for Act V.

**Model paragraph:**

> Act IV removes Hamlet from the stage at the moment his enemies organise, and the gap between what we know and what he knows becomes the engine of the ending. We hear Claudius, alone, order England to put Hamlet to death ("Do it, England"); we watch Laertes return and be turned into the King's instrument; we see the plot assembled step by step, from "A sword unbated" to Laertes's "I’ll anoint my sword" to the King's reserve chalice. Meanwhile Hamlet's own adventures come to us only secondhand, in a letter that tells of pirates but withholds how he escaped the warrant. Shakespeare reverses the order of knowledge that governed Act III. There we knew Claudius's guilt before Hamlet proved it; here we know the trap before Hamlet walks into it. By the time he returns, the audience can only wait to see whether the man who has always been slow to act will be quick enough to survive someone else's plan.

**Why it works.** It names a structural choice (absence, reported events, staged plotting), supports each with specific evidence, and explains the effect: dramatic irony and dread. The comparison with Act III shows understanding of the play's design.

**A weak version:** "In Act IV Hamlet goes to England and comes back. Laertes wants revenge and Ophelia dies. Claudius and Laertes make a plan to kill Hamlet. This makes Act V exciting." Every sentence is plot; none explains how the order creates the excitement.

## Common misreadings

- **"Hamlet goes to England."** He never gets there; pirates return him to Denmark. Rosencrantz and Guildenstern sail on.
- **"Claudius proposes the poison on the sword."** The poison is Laertes's addition. Claudius proposes the unbated blade and the backup cup.
- **"Gertrude watched Ophelia drown."** The speech never says who saw it. Treat the report's source as a question, not a fact.
- **"Ophelia's flowers go to particular people according to the text."** The dialogue does not say who receives which; editions and productions decide.
- **"'How all occasions' is in every text of Hamlet."** It is in the Second Quarto only.
- **"Hamlet's vow at the end of 4.4 means he is ready to act."** He vows bloody thoughts, as he sails away. Read the words.
