# Week 24 · Symbol and Setting in "The Wife of His Youth"

**CED skills:** 5.C Identify and explain the function of a symbol · 2.C Describe the relationship between a character and a setting
**Big Ideas:** Figurative Language (FIG), Setting (SET), Character (CHR)
**Read:** Charles W. Chesnutt, "The Wife of His Youth" (1898), the title story of *The Wife of His Youth and Other Stories of the Color Line* (1899) ([Project Gutenberg #11057](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/11057)). About 6,000 words, in three numbered sections.
**Spiral:** symbol in *Hamlet* (week 19, 5.C); setting and character in "Young Goodman Brown" (week 11, 2.B and 2.C); allusion (week 14, 6.D); contrasts (week 5, 3.D); character change (weeks 8 and 23, 1.B). Week 22's narrator could not be trusted; week 23's could not act; this week's main character acts, publicly, against his own interest.

## What you need to be able to do

- **5.C** Identify and explain the function of a symbol. A symbol is a concrete thing (object, place, physical detail, action) that the text loads with meaning beyond itself. Explaining its *function* means showing how the text signals that meaning, how the meaning develops, and what the symbol lets the story say.
- **2.C** Describe the relationship between a character and a setting. Settings are not backdrops. A place can make a character, test him, tempt him or expose him, and a character can embrace or flee a setting. Here two settings meet in one doorway: the Northern city where Ryder has made himself, and the Southern plantation past he has left.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Symbol | A concrete detail that carries a meaning beyond its literal one, signalled by emphasis, repetition or a character's attention. |
| Colorism | Prejudice based on skin tone, including within a racial or ethnic group. |
| The color line | The social boundary drawn by race in American life; Chesnutt's subtitle for his collection, *Stories of the Color Line*. |
| Blue Vein Society | Chesnutt's fictional society of light-skinned Black residents of a Northern city, nicknamed from the idea that members must be "white enough to show blue veins." |
| Daguerreotype | An early photograph on a silvered plate (from 1839), often kept in a small hinged case. |
| Dialect writing | Spelling that represents a regional or social pronunciation. Chesnutt uses it for Liza Jane, and literary English for Ryder. |
| Juxtaposition | Placing two things side by side so that each defines the other. |
| Allusion | Here: Tennyson, Lincoln, Exodus, *Hamlet* and the Bible's "wife of thy youth." |

## The text in context

Charles W. Chesnutt (1858-1932) was born in Cleveland to free Black parents from North Carolina, grew up in Fayetteville, North Carolina, during Reconstruction, and returned to Cleveland in 1883, where he worked as a legal stenographer and was admitted to the Ohio bar. He was of mixed ancestry, light enough to have passed as white, and chose to identify as Black and to write about the color line from within. "The Wife of His Youth" appeared in the *Atlantic Monthly* in July 1898, and the collection followed in 1899, the same year as his *The Conjure Woman*. He was among the first African American fiction writers published widely by the major literary magazines; in 1928 he received the NAACP's Spingarn Medal.

Three pieces of history make the story legible.

1. **Colorism after emancipation.** In several Northern cities, a small Black elite, many of them descended from people free before the war and some of mixed ancestry, formed clubs and churches with exclusive social standards. Lighter skin was often, though not always, associated with that status. Chesnutt's Blue Veins are a satirical portrait drawn by an insider; he neither denies the prejudice nor treats the members as villains.
2. **Slavery and marriage.** Marriages between enslaved people had no legal standing in the slave states; couples could be separated by sale at any time. After emancipation some Southern states recognised such unions only if the couple were living together or registered them. When Ryder suggests to Liza Jane that her marriage might not "count," he is pointing to a real legal fact.
3. **The search.** After the war freed people searched for spouses, children and parents through churches, the Freedmen's Bureau and newspaper notices, sometimes for decades. Liza Jane's twenty-five years of searching are not melodrama; they reflect a common history.

**A note on language.** Chesnutt renders Liza Jane's speech in dialect and Ryder's in polished English. The contrast is deliberate: it marks the gulf of education and status between them that the ending refuses to treat as a gulf of worth. Read her lines aloud with respect, and when you quote them, quote exactly. Do not imitate or exaggerate the dialect in your own writing.

## Close reading

**1. The symbol is introduced.**

> Some envious outsider made the suggestion that no one was eligible for membership who was not white enough to show blue veins. The suggestion was readily adopted by those who were not of the favored few, and since that time the society, though possessing a longer and more pretentious name, had been known far and wide as the "Blue Vein Society," and its members as the "Blue Veins."

*Observe:* the symbol has a history. It starts as a gibe from outside ("envious outsider"), spreads among the excluded, and sticks.
*Interpret:* "blue veins" makes a physical trait into a test of belonging. The narrator's irony ("pretentious"; earlier, "By accident, combined perhaps with some natural affinity") lets the society's exclusiveness show without denouncing it.
*Function:* the symbol announces the story's subject, prejudice by colour inside a community that suffers from prejudice by colour, and it sets the measure that Ryder's final choice will overturn.

**2. A search for the right lady.**

> He marked the verse, and turning the page read the stanza beginning,— "O sweet pale Margaret, / O rare pale Margaret." He weighed the passage a moment, and decided that it would not do. Mrs. Dixon was the palest lady he expected at the ball, and she was of a rather ruddy complexion [...]

*Observe:* Ryder chooses Tennyson to praise the ladies, and rejects a stanza because his intended is not pale *enough* to fit it.
*Interpret:* the book of poems becomes a symbol of Ryder's ideal: cultivated, English, literary, and "fair" in both senses. Even his taste in verse is measured by complexion.
*Function:* the scene moves the colour line from the society to Ryder's own imagination, and it sets up the contrast that follows minutes later at his door.

**3. Two settings in one doorway.**

> She looked like a bit of the old plantation life, summoned up from the past by the wave of a magician's wand, as the poet's fancy had called into being the gracious shapes of which Mr. Ryder had just been reading.

*Observe:* the narrator compares two acts of conjuring: the poet's fancy produces "gracious shapes," and the past produces Liza Jane.
*Interpret:* the South Ryder left, with its slavery, sale and separation, arrives at the threshold of the Northern home he has built. The simile insists she is as real a summons as Tennyson's ladies, and more binding.
*Function (2.C):* Ryder's identity depends on the Groveland setting: his job, his house, his society, his planned marriage. Liza Jane brings the other setting with her, and the rest of the story is about which world Ryder will claim.

**4. The mirror and the toast.**

> When she had turned the corner, he went upstairs to his bedroom, and stood for a long time before the mirror of his dressing-case, gazing thoughtfully at the reflection of his own face.

At the ball, after telling her story as a hypothetical and hearing Mrs. Dixon answer "He should have acknowledged her," Ryder brings Liza Jane in:

> "Permit me to introduce to you the wife of my youth."

*Observe:* the narrator gives us no inner monologue at the mirror, only time ("for a long time") and attention ("thoughtfully").
*Interpret:* the mirror is the private reckoning. The face he sees is the one Liza Jane could not recognise, and the one she has kept faithful to in her faded daguerreotype. The decision is withheld until the public scene.
*Function:* the two symbols of the self, mirror and daguerreotype, frame Ryder's change. The ending turns his private recognition into public acknowledgment, before the very people whose standards he upheld, and the title becomes his own words.

## From reading to writing

The symbol paragraph has a predictable failure: "The blue veins symbolize racism." That sentence names a meaning and stops. A strong paragraph shows how the text builds the meaning and what the symbol makes possible. Model:

> Chesnutt never has his narrator condemn the Blue Veins; he lets their name do it. **(1)** The phrase begins as the jibe of "some envious outsider," is "readily adopted by those who were not of the favored few," and replaces the society's "longer and more pretentious name," so the symbol carries the community's mockery of its own elite. **(2)** Ryder absorbs that scale of value so thoroughly that he rejects a Tennyson stanza because Mrs. Dixon is "of a rather ruddy complexion" for "pale Margaret." **(3)** When he finally introduces "the wife of my youth," a woman "very black" who could never pass the society's test, to the Blue Veins assembled in his house, the symbol loses its power in the very room where it ruled. Chesnutt's indirection matters: by exposing colorism through a nickname and a man's choice of poetry rather than through a lecture, he lets his Black and white readers of 1898 see the prejudice from inside before they can defend against it. **(4)**

1. Claim about *how* the symbol works (indirect judgment).
2. Origin and development traced with embedded quotation.
3. The symbol moved into character: internalised value.
4. The turn and the commentary on effect, including a careful historical observation. Note the paragraph's caution: "Black and white readers of 1898" acknowledges that the *Atlantic*'s audience was largely white without claiming more than we know.

**A weak version:** "The blue veins symbolize racism. Ryder is racist against darker people. At the end he learns his lesson." It flattens the irony (the society is not simply "racist" in the way white supremacists were; it is a community reproducing a hierarchy imposed on it), it misses who named the symbol, and "learns his lesson" replaces analysis with a moral.

**Setting paragraph (2.C).** For a character-and-setting question, describe three things: what the setting gives the character (Groveland gives Ryder work, standing, a society); what it asks of him (distance from the plantation past and from darker people); and how the character responds when the setting is challenged (Liza Jane brings the South north, and he chooses her world's claim over his own society's standard).

## Common misreadings

- **"Ryder is a hypocrite and a villain."** Chesnutt makes him dignified, hard-working and capable of the hardest choice in the story. His prejudices are real; so is his integrity. Hold both.
- **"The Blue Veins are white people."** They are Black Americans, many of mixed ancestry. The story is about colour prejudice *within* a community, which is why it was daring in 1898.
- **"Liza Jane is a comic figure."** Her dialect and old-fashioned dress might have read as comic to some readers in 1898; the story's structure refuses that reading. She is faithful, brave (she warned Sam at the cost of her own punishment) and finally honoured.
- **Anachronism.** Do not import twenty-first-century vocabulary as if Chesnutt used it. Use the story's own terms when quoting, and use precise present-day terms ("colorism," "enslaved people") in your own commentary.
- **"The marriage didn't count, so Ryder had no obligation."** That is exactly the argument the story tests and rejects. Ryder raises the legal point; the ending answers it with a higher obligation.

## Self-check

1. Trace the "blue veins" symbol from its origin to the end of the story. How does its meaning change?
2. Why does Ryder reject the "pale Margaret" stanza, and what does that small choice reveal?
3. Explain how the description of Liza Jane as "a bit of the old plantation life" relates Ryder to two settings.
4. What do the daguerreotype and the mirror have in common as symbols? How do they differ?
5. Why does Ryder tell Liza Jane's story as a hypothetical at the ball? What does the guests' answer do?
6. Explain, with historical accuracy, why Ryder's point about the legal status of his marriage was not an invention.
