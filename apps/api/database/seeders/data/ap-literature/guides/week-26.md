## What you need to be able to do

- **5.C** Recognise when a concrete thing in a poem (a road, a bird, a grove) carries meaning beyond itself, and explain what that symbolic meaning does in the poem, using the details that open it.
- **3.C** See how a poem is built (stanzas, a turn, a change of tense, speaker or addressee) and explain what the structure does: where the poem's thinking changes direction and why it matters that it changes there.

In plain language: a Q1 prompt almost never says "find the symbol". It asks how the poet conveys a complex attitude. Symbols and shifts are two of the best routes into that answer, because a symbol carries the poem's idea and a shift shows the idea changing.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Symbol | A concrete object, person or action in the poem's world that also suggests a larger meaning. |
| Conventional symbol | One whose meaning is culturally shared (a cradle for birth, winter for death). |
| Contextual symbol | One whose meaning is built by the poem itself (Frost's roads, Hardy's thrush). |
| Shift (turn, volta) | A change in a poem's direction: of tone, tense, speaker, addressee, subject or argument. |
| Stanza | A group of lines set off by space; often a unit of thought. |
| Volta | The "turn" of a sonnet or other poem, often signalled by "but", "yet", "at once" or a stanza break. |
| Anaphora | Repetition of a word or phrase at the start of successive lines ("Surely ... / Surely ..."). |
| Chiasmus | A crossed pattern (A B / B A): "The best lack all conviction, while the worst / Are full of passionate intensity." |
| Paradox | An apparent contradiction that reveals a truth (a cradle that causes nightmares). |
| Darkling | In the dark, or growing dark; an old poetic word. |

## The texts in context

**Frost** (1874-1963) published "The Road Not Taken" as the opening poem of *Mountain Interval* (1916). It is the most misquoted poem in American literature: readers remember the last three lines as a celebration of nonconformity and forget the middle stanzas, which say the roads were the same.

**Yeats** (1865-1939) wrote "The Second Coming" in January 1919, after the First World War, during the Russian Revolution and as Ireland moved toward its war of independence. It was printed in *The Dial* in November 1920 and in *Michael Robartes and the Dancer* (Cuala Press, 1920). The course quotes the Cuala text, whose lines 13 and 17 ("a waste of desert sand", "Wind shadows") differ from the wording of later collections. Yeats built a private system of history in "gyres", two-thousand-year cycles, but the poem does not require it: its images work on their own.

**Hardy** (1840-1928) dated "The Darkling Thrush" December 1900, the last month of the nineteenth century, and first printed it on 29 December 1900. The course quotes the 1901 book text ("Like strings from broken lyres", "At once a voice outburst among"); later printings revised both lines.

**Hopkins** (1844-1889), a Jesuit priest, wrote "Spring and Fall" in 1880. Like nearly all his poems it appeared only after his death, edited by his friend Robert Bridges in 1918. The accents are Hopkins's own stress marks for his "sprung rhythm".

## Close reading

**1. Frost: a symbol undermined before it is claimed.**

> Then took the other, as just as fair,
> And having perhaps the better claim,
> Because it was grassy and wanted wear;
> Though as for that the passing there
> Had worn them really about the same,

*Observe.* "Perhaps", "as just as fair", "Though as for that", "really about the same": the stanza hedges every claim it makes.

*Interpret.* The roads symbolise choice, but the poem insists there was no real difference between them. When the final stanza says "I took the one less traveled by, / And that has made all the difference," the reader knows the "less traveled" road is a story the speaker will tell later.

*Function.* The symbol works ironically: it is about the meaning people *assign* to choices. The shift into the future tense ("I shall be telling this with a sigh") is the structural proof, since the claim of difference only appears in the imagined retelling.

**2. Yeats: from diagnosis to vision.**

> Surely some revelation is at hand;
> Surely the Second Coming is at hand.
> The Second Coming! Hardly are those words out
> When a vast image out of Spiritus Mundi
> Troubles my sight:

*Observe.* The first stanza never says "I"; this one does ("my sight"). "Surely" is repeated as if to reassure, and the exclamation summons the vision.

*Interpret.* The speaker reaches for the Christian promise that Christ will return to judge and redeem, and the words themselves call up something else: a sphinx-like "shape with lion body and the head of a man", with a "gaze blank and pitiless as the sun".

*Function.* The shift (3.C) turns general statement into personal prophecy, and the allusion (6.D in review) raises a hope that the symbol of the "rough beast" then destroys. Its destination, Bethlehem, makes the new age an inverted nativity.

**3. Hardy: the turn at the centre.** The first two stanzas build a dead world: Frost "spectre-gray", "Winter's dregs", the land as "The Century's corpse outleant". Then the third stanza begins, "At once a voice outburst among / The bleak twigs overhead". The poem's four stanzas split exactly in half, desolation and song, and the hinge is "At once". The symbol arrives with the shift: "An aged thrush, frail, gaunt, and small, / In blast-beruffled plume". Because the bird is as old and battered as the century, its song cannot be explained by circumstance, and that is what makes it a symbol of hope against the evidence. The last stanza then shifts again, from description to the speaker's reflection: he "could think" there was "Some blessed Hope, whereof he knew / And I was unaware." Structure keeps the ending honest: the hope belongs to the bird.

**4. Hopkins: a symbol explained, and a shift into knowledge.** "Leáves, like the things of man" makes the symbol almost explicit, and the poem then moves through time: present grief, a colder future heart ("Áh! ás the heart grows older / It will come to such sights colder"), then a stronger future, "And yet you wíll weep and know why." The final couplet completes the turn from leaves to the child: "It is the blight man was born for, / It is Margaret you mourn for." The title's "Fall" is autumn and also the Fall of Genesis, after which death entered the world.

## How to find a shift

Look in five places, and when you find one, ask what changes and why there.

1. **Stanza breaks**, especially when a stanza begins with a new word of time or contrast ("At once", "Surely", "Yet").
2. **Tense**: Frost moves from past choice to future retelling; Hopkins from present grief to future knowledge.
3. **Person and addressee**: Yeats moves from impersonal statement to "my sight"; Hopkins from the child's feeling to a universal law and back to her name.
4. **Sentence type**: a statement giving way to an exclamation or a question. Yeats ends on a question; Frost hesitates on a dash ("and I—").
5. **Tone**: Hardy from funeral to "ecstatic sound"; Yeats from dread to apparent hope to horror.

A shift is not a device to name; it is a place where the poem's meaning moves. The sentence to aim for is: "The shift from X to Y reveals Z about the speaker's attitude toward W."

## From reading to writing

A model Q1 body paragraph on "The Darkling Thrush":

> **Claim:** Hardy places the thrush's song at the exact centre of the poem so that its hope must be weighed against everything that came before it. **Evidence:** After two stanzas in which the land is "The Century's corpse outleant" and "every spirit upon earth / Seemed fervourless," the third stanza opens, "At once a voice outburst among / The bleak twigs overhead." **Commentary:** "At once" breaks the funeral rhythm the way the song breaks the silence, and "outburst" makes the song an eruption rather than a melody. Yet the singer is "aged," "frail, gaunt, and small," as worn as the century itself, so the poem refuses to explain its joy by youth or springtime. Because the hope arrives in so unlikely a body, the speaker cannot dismiss it, and because it arrives so suddenly, he cannot quite believe it; the structure produces exactly the wondering uncertainty of the final stanza.

*Why it works:* the claim is about structure *and* meaning; the commentary explains the effect of specific words ("At once", "outburst", "aged") and links the shift to the speaker's final attitude.

*A weak version:* "The thrush symbolises hope. Hardy uses a shift in stanza 3. This shows the speaker becomes hopeful." Each sentence is a label; none explains how the poem works, and the last is inaccurate, since the speaker never claims hope for himself.

## Common misreadings

- **"The Road Not Taken" as a manifesto for nonconformity.** Read lines 9-12 aloud. The roads were "really about the same". The poem is about how we narrate choices, and the sigh may be regret.
- **Decoding Yeats's symbols one-to-one.** "The falcon = the people, the falconer = God" closes down a symbol that works by suggestion. Offer a reading, then show the details that support it, and allow the image to mean more than one thing.
- **Making Hardy's speaker hopeful.** He hears hope; he does not share it. "I could think" and "I was unaware" are the evidence.
- **Ignoring Hopkins's grammar.** "Nor mouth had, no nor mind, expressed / What heart heard of, ghost guessed" means: neither speech nor thought has put into words what the heart has heard and the spirit (ghost) has guessed. The child's tears know something before she can say it.
- **Calling every change a shift.** A new image is not a shift unless the poem's attitude, direction or argument changes with it.
