## What you need to be able to do

- **3.F** Name the conflicts that drive a narrative (a character against another, against nature or society, against himself) and explain what each conflict *does*: what it reveals, what it sets in motion, what idea it tests.

In plain language: this is the week you stop writing "Victor is ambitious" and start writing "Shelley sets Victor's hunger for discovery against his need for human ties, so that the novel's first horror is not the creature but the cost of the isolation that made him."

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Frame narrative | A story told inside another story. Walton's letters frame Victor's narration, which in Volume II will frame the creature's. |
| Epistolary novel | A novel told through letters. *Frankenstein* opens as one: Walton writes to his sister, Margaret Saville, in England. |
| Retrospective narrator | A narrator telling events after they are over, who knows how they ended. Victor narrates his youth from Walton's ship, near death. |
| Internal conflict | A struggle inside a character: Victor's ambition against his conscience, his family loyalties and his health. |
| External conflict | A struggle with an outside force: another person, society, nature. Walton against the ice; later, Victor against the creature. |
| Foil | A character whose likeness to another sharpens their differences. Walton is Victor's foil from the first letter. |
| Defensible claim | A claim a reasonable reader could dispute, supportable with textual evidence. Not a fact, not a matter of taste. |
| Line of reasoning | The ordered set of reasons an essay gives for its thesis; each body paragraph advances one. |
| Prometheus | In Greek myth, the Titan who stole fire from the gods for humans and was punished; in some versions he shaped humans from clay. Shelley's subtitle. |

## The text in context

Mary Wollstonecraft Shelley (1797-1851) was the daughter of two radical writers, Mary Wollstonecraft and William Godwin, to whom *Frankenstein* is dedicated. She began the story in the summer of 1816 near Geneva, in the company of Percy Bysshe Shelley and Lord Byron, and the novel appeared anonymously on 1 January 1818 in three volumes. Its title page carries an epigraph from Milton's *Paradise Lost*, Adam's complaint to God: did I ask you, Maker, to mould me man from clay? Keep that epigraph in mind; it predicts the creature's argument in week 9.

The novel belongs to a moment when electricity, chemistry and anatomy seemed to promise control over life itself. Victor's education moves from the old alchemists (Cornelius Agrippa, Paracelsus, Albertus Magnus) to modern chemistry at the university of Ingolstadt, and Shelley lets both traditions feed his ambition. The book is often called the first science fiction novel, but for AP purposes the more useful label is a **Gothic novel built as nested narratives**: each narrator tells his story to a listener with a reason to hear it.

## How the opening is built

Read the structure before the plot. The chronological story begins with Victor's childhood in Geneva, but the novel begins near its own end: Walton's ship trapped in Arctic ice, a giant figure glimpsed on a dog-sledge on 31 July, and the next morning a half-frozen stranger pulled aboard who is pursuing that figure. Only in Walton's journal of 19 August does Victor begin his story. That ordering makes three things happen at once:

1. **Suspense shifts from *what* to *how*.** We know Victor will end ruined and the creature will survive to cross the ice. The question becomes how he got there.
2. **Walton becomes the first audience and the first test.** He is an explorer who wants glory and confesses in Letter II that he has no friend. Victor tells his story *to warn him*.
3. **Every later statement is filtered.** Victor narrates from the end, ill and bitter, so his account of his youth is shaped by what he now knows.

Volume I, Chapters I-IV, then run fast: Victor's happy childhood with Elizabeth (in 1818 his cousin, adopted into the household) and his friend Henry Clerval; his boyhood discovery of Cornelius Agrippa, which his father dismisses as "sad trash"; the lightning that shatters an old oak; his mother's death of scarlet fever, caught while nursing Elizabeth, just before he leaves for Ingolstadt; the professors Krempe, who scorns his old reading, and Waldman, who inspires him; nearly two years of secret work; the night of animation; his flight; Clerval's arrival; and a months-long fever.

## Close reading

**1. Walton's breeze (Letter I).** Walton writes from St. Petersburgh:

> I am already far north of London; and as I walk in the streets of Petersburgh, I feel a cold northern breeze play upon my cheeks, which braces my nerves, and fills me with delight.

*Observe:* the cold "braces" and "delight[s]" him. *Interpret:* Walton reads hostile nature as an invitation; his desire converts a warning into a promise. *Function:* the first conflict in the book, a man against the polar ice, is presented as something the man wants. When Victor later describes his own work in the same language of eagerness and delight, we already know how to read it: the conflict with nature is chosen, and the chooser cannot see the danger.

**2. "I have no friend, Margaret" (Letter II).**

> But I have one want which I have never yet been able to satisfy; and the absence of the object of which I now feel as a most severe evil. I have no friend, Margaret: when I am glowing with the enthusiasm of success, there will be none to participate my joy; if I am assailed by disappointment, no one will endeavour to sustain me in dejection.

*Observe:* the balanced clauses (success / disappointment; joy / dejection). *Interpret:* Walton's ambition and his loneliness are one condition seen from two sides. *Function:* this sets up the internal conflict the novel tests in every narrator, the pull between solitary achievement and human connection. The creature will make almost the same complaint in Volume II.

**3. Victor's warning (Vol. I, Ch. III).** Breaking off his story, Victor turns to Walton:

> Learn from me, if not by my precepts, at least by my example, how dangerous is the acquirement of knowledge, and how much happier that man is who believes his native town to be the world, than he who aspires to become greater than his nature will allow.

*Observe:* a direct address to the listener, interrupting the past-tense narrative. *Interpret:* Victor frames his life as a cautionary tale. *Function:* the interruption reminds us the frame is active. The claim is also contestable: is knowledge itself dangerous, or the secrecy and isolation with which Victor pursues it? That disagreement is exactly the kind of tension a thesis can be built on.

**4. The night of animation (Vol. I, Ch. IV).**

> How can I describe my emotions at this catastrophe, or how delineate the wretch whom with such infinite pains and care I had endeavoured to form? His limbs were in proportion, and I had selected his features as beautiful. Beautiful!—Great God!

*Observe:* "catastrophe" names a success; Victor repeats his own word "beautiful" and breaks it off with a dash. *Interpret:* the gap between design and result opens inside one sentence. *Function:* the central conflict of the novel, creator against creation, begins here as a conflict inside the creator. He rejects the being before it has done anything, and the chapter's plot follows from that judgment: flight, fever, abandonment.

## From reading to writing: building a thesis

A **thesis** for the AP essay is one or two sentences that make an arguable claim about meaning and how it is made. Compare:

- *Weak (fact):* "In Chapter IV, Victor brings the creature to life and then runs away." Nobody can disagree; there is nothing to prove.
- *Weak (topic, no claim):* "This essay will discuss conflict in the opening chapters of *Frankenstein*." Names a subject, not an interpretation.
- *Weak (opinion):* "Victor is a terrible person." Defensible only as a feeling; it makes no claim about how the text works.
- *Defensible:* "In the opening of *Frankenstein*, Shelley frames Victor's story with Walton's letters so that the conflict between ambition and human connection appears first as a choice a listener can still make, turning Victor's ruin into an argument addressed to the reader."

Why the last one works: it is **about interpretation** (what the frame does), it is **arguable** (one could argue the frame mainly builds suspense), and it **establishes a line of reasoning**: (1) Walton's letters show ambition and loneliness together; (2) Victor's narrative repeats that pattern and makes it fatal; (3) Victor's interruptions turn the pattern into a warning. Each becomes a body paragraph.

A model body paragraph, annotated:

> Shelley makes the night of creation a conflict inside Victor before it is a conflict with the creature. *(claim tied to thesis)* Victor recalls that he "had selected his features as beautiful," then repeats the word as an exclamation, "Beautiful!—Great God!" *(evidence, embedded)* The dash cuts his own aesthetic judgment off in the middle, as if the plan and the result could not share a sentence; the creator's eye, not the creature's act, decides that the being is a "wretch." *(commentary: how the detail works)* Because Victor's horror comes before the creature has done anything, the conflict that drives the rest of the novel is set in motion by rejection, which supports the reading that Shelley locates the danger less in knowledge than in what Victor does with what he has made. *(commentary: link to line of reasoning)*

## Common misreadings

- **"Frankenstein is the monster."** Victor Frankenstein is the creator. The creature has no name; Victor calls him "the wretch," "the dæmon" and worse, and those names are Victor's judgments.
- **"The novel opens with Victor."** It opens with Walton's letters, and the frame is part of the meaning.
- **"Victor builds the creature in a castle with lightning."** That is the film tradition. In the novel he works in a solitary chamber in Ingolstadt and does not describe his method; he refuses to tell Walton the secret.
- **"Victor is simply evil."** He is generous with his family, adored by Clerval and Elizabeth, and sincerely idealistic: he imagines a new species blessing him as its creator. The internal conflict is real.
