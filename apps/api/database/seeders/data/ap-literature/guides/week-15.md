# Week 15 · Imagery and Personification

**CED skills:** 5.D Identify and explain the function of an image or imagery · 6.C Identify and explain the function of personification
**Big Ideas:** Figurative Language (FIG), Structure (STR), Literary Argumentation (LAN)
**Read:** John Keats, *"To Autumn"* (1820) ([Poetry Foundation](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/44484/to-autumn)); Emily Dickinson, *"Because I could not stop for Death"* (1890 text, "The Chariot") ([Project Gutenberg #12242](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/12242)); William Wordsworth, *"Composed upon Westminster Bridge, September 3, 1802"* (1807) ([Poetry Foundation](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/45514/composed-upon-westminster-bridge-september-3-1802))
**Spiral:** last week's sonnet form (Wordsworth's Petrarchan pattern again) and allusion (the *ubi sunt* question in Keats); simile from week 6; structure and contrast from week 5; the speaker from week 1.

## What you need to be able to do

- **5.D** Find the images in a passage, say which senses they engage, and, the part that scores, explain what they do: the mood, idea, contrast or shift they build, and how a sequence of images makes an argument by its order.
- **6.C** Recognise personification, describe the human role or temperament it gives a thing (a season, a city, death), and explain why the poem needs that thing to have a personality.
- Tell personification from **apostrophe** (addressing the absent or non-human), and both from simple description.

In plain language: when a question asks about "the imagery in lines 9-12", you should be able to say in one sentence what the images have in common and what that pattern means.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Image | A concrete detail that appeals to a sense. |
| Imagery | The pattern of images in a passage or poem, taken together. |
| Visual, auditory, tactile, olfactory, gustatory, kinesthetic | Sight, sound, touch, smell, taste, movement. |
| Personification | Giving human traits, feelings or actions to something non-human. |
| Apostrophe | Speaking to someone absent or something non-human ("thou", "O Death"). |
| Pathetic fallacy | Ruskin's 1856 term for attributing human feeling to nature; use it neutrally. |
| Catalogue | A list of images or items, often building a sense of range or abundance. |
| Euphemism | A mild expression for something harsh (a grave as "a house"). |
| Common meter | Quatrains alternating iambic tetrameter and trimeter (8-6-8-6), the meter of many hymns. |
| Synaesthesia | Describing one sense in terms of another; looser uses include transferring a verb from one realm to another ("clouds bloom"). |

## The texts in context

**Keats, "To Autumn" (1820).** Written in September 1819, after a walk near Winchester, and published in 1820 in Keats's last collection, alongside "Lamia" and "Isabella". Keats, trained as a surgeon-apothecary, had nursed his brother through tuberculosis and would die of it himself in Rome in February 1821, aged twenty-five. The poem is often called the most perfect of his odes. It is three eleven-line stanzas, each beginning with an ABAB quatrain and continuing for seven more lines: one line longer than the ten-line stanzas of his spring odes, so that each stanza seems to linger before it closes.

**Dickinson, "Because I could not stop for Death" (1890).** Dickinson published almost nothing in her lifetime. After her death in 1886 her sister found hundreds of poems, and Mabel Loomis Todd and T. W. Higginson edited the first selection, *Poems* (1890). They titled this one "The Chariot", regularised her dashes and capitals into conventional punctuation, and left out one stanza of her manuscript (a stanza about the chill of the evening). We quote that 1890 text, which is in the public domain; later scholarly editions restore her own punctuation and the missing stanza, and it is worth knowing that the poem you meet elsewhere may look different.

**Wordsworth, "Composed upon Westminster Bridge" (1807).** Another Petrarchan sonnet from *Poems, in Two Volumes*, its title, as usually printed, dated September 3, 1802. Wordsworth, the poet of the Lake District, wrote it after crossing the Thames at dawn on his way out of London. That the poet of "valley, rock, or hill" should find his greatest calm in a city is the poem's surprise.

## Close reading

**1. Ripeness felt in the hands (5.D).**

> To swell the gourd, and plump the hazel shells
> With a sweet kernel; to set budding more,
> And still more, later flowers for the bees,
> Until they think warm days will never cease,
> For summer has o'er-brimm'd their clammy cells.

*Observe:* the verbs are tactile ("swell", "plump"), the kernel is "sweet", the honeycomb "clammy" and "o'er-brimm'd". The syntax overflows too: "more, / And still more" runs across the line break. *Interpret:* abundance is pressed on the senses until it spills. *Function:* the stanza is one long sentence of infinitives ("to load and bless... To bend... to swell") with no main verb of its own, so the whole stanza is suspended in the ripening process. But the bees "think warm days will never cease", and they are wrong: the only hint of winter in the stanza is someone else's mistake. Abundance at its peak is already the beginning of its end.

**2. A season with a body (6.C).**

> Thee sitting careless on a granary floor,
> Thy hair soft-lifted by the winnowing wind;
> Or on a half-reap'd furrow sound asleep,
> Drows'd with the fume of poppies, while thy hook
> Spares the next swath and all its twined flowers

*Observe:* the second stanza turns from the season's work to the season as a person, seen in harvest roles: the thresher on the granary floor, the reaper asleep in the furrow, later the gleaner and the cider-maker. *Interpret:* every role is caught at rest. Autumn is "careless", "sound asleep", "Drows'd"; the hook "Spares" the next row. *Function:* the personification lets Keats slow time down. A harvest is an ending, the cutting of what has grown, and Autumn the reaper keeps postponing it. The image of the hair "soft-lifted by the winnowing wind" fuses the figure with the work: the same breeze that blows chaff from grain plays in the season's hair. By the end of the stanza Autumn is watching "the last oozings hours by hours", and the line's slow sound makes the reader wait with her.

**3. Images in sequence (5.D) and a courteous Death (6.C).**

> We passed the school where children played,
> Their lessons scarcely done;
> We passed the fields of gazing grain,
> We passed the setting sun.

*Observe:* three images joined by an anaphora ("We passed"). *Interpret:* childhood at play, grain ripe in the field, the sun going down: the stages of a life, from youth through maturity to its close. *Function:* the order makes an argument. The speaker is not living these stages but riding past them, carried by a driver who "knew no haste". The grain is "gazing", watching her go by, so the living world becomes the observer and she the observed. And the poem has set this up with personification: Death "kindly stopped", shows "civility", shares the carriage with "Immortality". A Death with manners is more unsettling than a skeleton, because the speaker has accepted the invitation before she understands it; only later does she "surmise" that the horses' heads are "toward eternity".

**4. A city asleep (6.C, with week 6's simile).**

> This City now doth, like a garment, wear
> The beauty of the morning; silent, bare,
> Ships, towers, domes, theatres, and temples lie
> Open unto the fields, and to the sky

> Dear God! the very houses seem asleep;
> And all that mighty heart is lying still!

*Observe:* a simile that makes the city a person in a garment; a catalogue of buildings that "lie / Open"; a closing personification of houses asleep and a heart at rest. *Interpret:* the city is beautiful as a body is beautiful in sleep. *Function:* the garment is put on "now", for this hour, and the "smokeless air" implies every other hour's smoke. The final line's "lying still" is peace, but it carries a shadow of death, which may be why the line ends in an exclamation. Wordsworth's speaker loves London most when it is not behaving like London.

## From reading to writing

The commonest weakness in Q1 essays is the image tour: "Keats uses visual imagery... He also uses auditory imagery... He also uses personification." Each sentence names a device, none explains one, and the essay has no line of reasoning. The fix is to make every image serve a claim. Here is a model paragraph on Keats's third stanza.

> Keats ends the poem not with the silence of winter but with autumn's own music, and its sounds carry both consolation and mourning. Having dismissed the "songs of spring" ("thou hast thy music too"), the stanza fills the evening with sound: gnats in a "wailful choir", lambs that "loud bleat", "Hedge-crickets", the red-breast's whistle and swallows that "twitter in the skies". Yet the music is shaded by endings. The gnats "mourn", rising and sinking "as the light wind lives or dies"; the clouds "bloom the soft-dying day"; and the "gathering swallows" are flocking to leave. Keats never names winter, but every sound is the sound of something about to go, so the consolation he offers is not that the season will last but that its ending is itself a kind of music.

*Annotations.* Sentence 1 is a claim with tension ("both consolation and mourning"). The evidence comes as short embedded phrases grouped by function: first the music, then its shadow. The commentary explains what the pattern of sound images does, including the inference about the swallows, which a reader must supply. The last sentence links the paragraph back to a larger reading of the poem.

A **weak version**: "In stanza three Keats uses a lot of auditory imagery, such as gnats, lambs, crickets and birds. This creates a peaceful mood. He also personifies the gnats as mourning." The images are named, the mood is asserted, and the personification is noticed but not explained. Why do the gnats mourn? What does it change that the last sound is birds getting ready to leave?

**Thesis patterns for imagery and personification.** Try: *Through [image pattern] and [personification of X as Y], [poet] presents [subject] as [complex idea].* For Dickinson: "Through the courtly personification of Death and a sequence of images that turns a lifetime into passing scenery, Dickinson presents dying as a gentle, almost social journey whose real destination dawns on the speaker only after it is too late to turn back." That thesis is defensible, names two techniques, and sets up a line of reasoning that follows the poem's order.

## Common misreadings

- **"Dickinson's speaker is afraid of Death."** The poem's calm is the point. Fear would be easier to read; the speaker's composure, and Death's politeness, are what make the poem unsettling.
- **Reading the "house" literally.** It is the grave. The understatement is a choice, and a question about its function expects you to explain the euphemism.
- **Treating the 1890 text as Dickinson's final version.** It is an edited text. When you quote, quote what is in front of you, but don't build an argument on punctuation the editors supplied without knowing it.
- **Calling every human-sounding phrase personification.** "The roof was scarcely visible" is an image, not personification. Ask: does the thing receive a human trait or action?
- **Reading "To Autumn" as only happy, or only sad.** It is a poem of fullness that knows it is ending. The answer that keeps both will usually be the keyed answer.
- **Reading "smokeless" as a fact about London.** It is a fact about dawn. The word implies the opposite condition at every other hour.
- **Forgetting the volta in Wordsworth.** The sestet turns from what the speaker sees to what he feels, and ranks the city above "valley, rock, or hill".

## Connections and looking ahead

Last week's sonnets taught you to find a turn; Wordsworth's bridge sonnet turns at line 9 as precisely as "The world is too much with us". Keats's *ubi sunt* question is an allusion to a tradition rather than a single text, the same 6.D skill. Week 16 moves from single images and personifications to the extended metaphor, a comparison sustained across a whole poem (Donne's compass, Frost's wall, Herbert's pulley), and to writing the full Q1 essay. In Unit 7, imagery returns in prose: Poe's "The Tell-Tale Heart" (week 22) builds dread out of sound images, and you will use the same 5.D moves on fiction. On the exam, imagery and personification questions appear in nearly every poetry set, and Q1 prompts often ask about a speaker's response to a place or a season, exactly this week's material.
