# Week 23 · Change and Relationship in "Bartleby, the Scrivener"

**CED skills:** 1.B Explain the function of a character changing or remaining unchanged · 1.D Describe how textual details reveal nuances and complexities in characters' relationships with one another
**Big Ideas:** Character (CHR), Setting (SET), Narration (NAR)
**Read:** Herman Melville, "Bartleby, the Scrivener: A Story of Wall-Street" (1853) ([Project Gutenberg #11231](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/11231)). About 14,000 words: read it in two sittings, breaking at the Sunday discovery.
**Spiral:** dynamic and static characters (week 8, *Frankenstein*, 1.B); relationships between characters (week 12, 1.D); contrasting characters (week 13 and *Hamlet*, 1.C); last week's narrative reliability (4.D) and imagery (5.D); setting and its function (week 11, 2.B); allusion (week 14, 6.D).

## What you need to be able to do

- **1.B** Explain the function of a character changing or remaining unchanged. Not just "who changes," but what the change, or the refusal to change, *does* in the story: what it reveals, what it measures, what it makes the reader think.
- **1.D** Describe how textual details reveal nuances and complexities in characters' relationships. Relationships in literature are rarely one thing. Here an employer is also a benefactor, a jailer, a brother "of Adam," and a man trying to get rid of a problem. You need to show those layers in specific details: a screen, a word, a gesture, a move across town.

In plain language: be able to explain why Bartleby never changes, how the narrator does, and what their strange bond says about what one person owes another.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Static character | A character who does not change in values or understanding. Not the same as "flat": Bartleby is static but deeply enigmatic. |
| Dynamic character | A character who changes in some important way over the course of the work. |
| Foil | A character whose contrast with another highlights qualities in both (Turkey and Nippers as each other's foils; Bartleby as the narrator's). |
| Scrivener | A law-copyist who writes out legal documents by hand and checks copies against originals. |
| Master in Chancery | A court officer in equity cases; the narrator's lucrative post, abolished by the New York constitution of 1846. |
| Passive resistance | Refusal without force or argument. The narrator: "Nothing so aggravates an earnest person as a passive resistance." |
| Enigma | A character or event whose meaning is withheld, so that interpretations reveal their interpreters. |
| Allusion | A reference to another text or history: Petra, Marius at Carthage, Job's "kings and counselors." |

## The text in context

Herman Melville (1819-1891) had been famous for South Seas adventures (*Typee*, 1846) before *Moby-Dick* (1851) and *Pierre* (1852) failed with readers. "Bartleby" was one of the first magazine stories he turned to afterward; it appeared anonymously in *Putnam's Monthly Magazine* in two parts, November and December 1853, and was collected in *The Piazza Tales* (1856). Some readers have connected the copyist who will no longer copy to a writer who would no longer produce what the market wanted, but the story does not require that reading, and you should not assume it.

The setting is real and specific: a law office on Wall Street in the 1840s, where copies of deeds and contracts were made by hand. The narrator's world of "bonds and mortgages and title-deeds" is the world of the new commercial city. The story's subtitle, "A Story of Wall-Street," invites you to see the walls in it, and there are many.

Melville makes an unusual choice of narrator. The lawyer is reasonable, humane by his own lights, humorous and self-aware. He is not a murderer like Poe's narrator. Yet he is telling the story of a man who died after he abandoned him, and his account is shaped by that. Reliability is less about facts here than about the meaning he gives them.

## Close reading

**1. The first refusal.**

> Imagine my surprise, nay, my consternation, when without moving from his privacy, Bartleby in a singularly mild, firm voice, replied, "I would prefer not to."

*Observe:* Bartleby does not move "from his privacy" (behind the screen); his voice is "mild" and "firm"; his verb is "prefer."
*Interpret:* the formula refuses without defying. "I will not" would be insubordination; "I cannot" would be incapacity. "I would prefer not to" states a wish and leaves the employer with nothing to correct. The narrator's "surprise, nay, my consternation" shows how completely he took obedience for granted.
*Function (1.D):* the detail reveals that the relationship depended all along on cooperation the narrator did not know he needed. The power of the employer turns out to be a habit of the employee.

**2. The screen.**

> I procured a high green folding screen, which might entirely isolate Bartleby from my sight, though not remove him from my voice.

*Observe:* the narrator places Bartleby in his own room, close enough to call, but walled off from sight.
*Interpret:* the arrangement wants the convenience of a servant without the presence of a person. It is the relationship in miniature: command without contact.
*Function:* the screen is the first of many walls, and it explains why the narrator never knows Bartleby. He designed the office so that he would not have to see him.

**3. The Sunday discovery: change begins.**

> For the first time in my life a feeling of overpowering stinging melancholy seized me. Before, I had never experienced aught but a not-unpleasing sadness. The bond of a common humanity now drew me irresistibly to gloom. A fraternal melancholy! For both I and Bartleby were sons of Adam.

*Observe:* "For the first time in my life." The narrator contrasts a "not-unpleasing sadness" (the comfortable melancholy of a man who reads sad stories) with a feeling that stings.
*Interpret:* this is the hinge of his change (1.B). The complacent man who believed "the easiest way of life is the best" recognises kinship with someone who has nothing.
*Function:* it sets the test that the rest of the story applies. Feeling is not action. The next pages show him deciding Bartleby is a charge from "Providence," then dropping that resolve when his colleagues talk, then moving offices to escape. Bartleby's constancy makes each wavering visible.

**4. The last explanation.**

> Dead letters! does it not sound like dead men? Conceive a man by nature and misfortune prone to a pallid hopelessness, can any business seem more fitted to heighten it than that of continually handling these dead letters, and assorting them for the flames?

*Observe:* the narrator builds this on what he calls a "vague report" he cannot confirm: that Bartleby once worked in the Dead Letter Office.
*Interpret:* the image is powerful: messages of hope, pardon and charity that never arrive, burned unread. But it is also a comfort to the narrator: it locates the cause of Bartleby's despair in a past job and a change of administration, not in a Wall Street office.
*Function:* this is where last week's skill (4.D) returns. The story ends with the narrator's interpretation, and a sophisticated reading notices how much he needs it. "Ah Bartleby! Ah humanity!" can be heard as hard-won fellow-feeling or as a sigh that turns a specific failure into a universal one. Both readings have evidence.

## From reading to writing

This week's essay skill is the literary-argument move of explaining *function*: not "the narrator changes" but what his change does for the work. Here is a model paragraph.

> Melville keeps Bartleby perfectly still so that the narrator's movement becomes the story. **(1)** Bartleby's answer never varies, from the "singularly mild, firm voice" of his first refusal to his flat "I know where I am" in the Tombs, and Melville never grants him an explanation. **(2)** Against that fixed line, the narrator's responses rise and fall: on the Sunday he discovers Bartleby living in the office he feels "for the first time in my life a feeling of overpowering stinging melancholy," yet within weeks he is moving his business across town to escape the man he called a brother "of Adam." **(3)** The constancy of one character therefore works as a measure of the other's inconstancy. What Bartleby's stillness exposes is not a villain but a decent man whose sympathy, sincere each time it appears, keeps stopping at the point where it would cost him his ease. **(4)**

1. Claim about function, not just a fact about character.
2. Evidence of constancy, framed as a pattern (first scene to last).
3. Evidence of change, with quoted turning point and its reversal.
4. Commentary that names the interpretation, with a qualification ("sincere each time") that adds complexity rather than a verdict.

**A weak version:** "Bartleby is a static character because he always says 'I would prefer not to.' The narrator is a dynamic character because he changes. At the end he feels sorry for Bartleby." Every sentence is true; none explains anything. Labels ("static," "dynamic") are where analysis starts, not where it ends.

**The relationship paragraph (1.D).** For a relationship question, work in layers. Name at least two roles the characters play to each other, and find a detail for each: employer (the order to check copy), benefactor (the money left under the paperweight; the dinner paid for in the Tombs), host or jailer (the screen; the office he cannot leave), brother (the "sons of Adam" passage), and finally mourner. The nuance is that the narrator plays all of these roles, often at once.

## Common misreadings

- **"Bartleby is lazy."** He copies a great deal at first and at night. His refusal begins with a checking task and widens; laziness does not explain a man who prefers to starve.
- **"The narrator is a cruel boss."** He is unusually patient, and he gives money, time and even an offer of his home. The story's power comes from a *good* man failing, not a bad man succeeding.
- **"The Dead Letter Office explains everything."** It is presented as a rumour the narrator cannot confirm. Use it as evidence about the narrator's need for meaning, not as a solved mystery.
- **"Bartleby is just mentally ill."** The text gives signs of depression or withdrawal, but reducing him to a diagnosis erases the story's questions about work, charity and responsibility. If you raise the possibility, treat it as one reading among others, supported with evidence.
- **Ignoring the comedy.** Turkey and Nippers are funny. Their predictable eccentricities are foils for Bartleby's unpredictable one, and the shift from comedy to tragedy is part of the story's design.

## Connections and looking ahead

- **Back:** Victor Frankenstein (weeks 7-10) changes in knowledge but cannot change his conduct toward his creature; the narrator here has the same problem in a gentler key. Compare Hamlet, who delays and changes, with Bartleby, who refuses and does not. Last week Poe's narrator was unreliable in his judgment of himself; this narrator is unreliable mainly in the comfort he finds in explanations.
- **Next week:** Mr. Ryder in "The Wife of His Youth" faces a choice the narrator here evades: whether to acknowledge a person whose claim on him costs him status. Chesnutt gives you a character who *does* change, at a cost, in public.
- **On the exam:** Question 3 (literary argument) often asks about a character's change, constancy or relationship. "Bartleby" is a strong choice because both kinds of character appear in one short work, and because its ending supports more than one interpretation, a natural route to Row C.
