# Week 22 · Reliability and Imagery in "The Tell-Tale Heart"

The story is about 2,200 words: read it twice, once for the plot and once with a pencil, marking every place the narrator tells you how to judge him.
**Spiral:** first-person point of view and the narrator as a character (week 1, 4.A and 4.B); diction that reveals perspective (week 12, "The Yellow Wall-Paper," 4.C); the unreliable speech of Polonius and Hamlet (week 18, 4.D); imagery (week 15, 5.D); allusion (week 14, 6.D). Unit 7 now pushes all of it toward Row C of the essay rubric: complexity, ambiguity and alternative readings.

## What you need to be able to do

- **4.D** Explain how a narrator's reliability affects a narrative. That means more than saying "the narrator is unreliable." You need to show *which* part of the account you doubt (the facts, the interpretation of the facts, or the judgment passed on them), point to the textual evidence that creates the doubt, and explain what the doubt does to the story: what it makes the reader feel, notice or conclude.
- **5.D** Identify and explain the function of an image or imagery. An image is language that appeals to a sense. Its *function* is what it does in this text: reveal a character's perspective, build an atmosphere, carry a theme, link two moments, or (this week) give the reader evidence the narrator does not mean to give.

In plain language: by Friday you should be able to take any sentence of Poe's narrator, say what he wants you to believe, say what you actually believe, and explain how the words produced the difference.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Unreliable narrator | A narrator whose account the reader has textual reason to doubt in its facts, interpretations or judgments. |
| Fallible vs untrustworthy | A fallible narrator is mistaken (limited knowledge, youth, illness); an untrustworthy one has a motive to mislead. Poe's narrator is chiefly fallible: he reports in detail but misreads himself. |
| Frame of narration | The situation in which the story is told: here, a speaker defending his sanity to a listener after the events. |
| Dramatic irony | The reader understands something the speaker does not. |
| Imagery | Language appealing to the senses: visual, auditory, tactile, kinetic. |
| Gothic | A mode built on dread, enclosure, darkness, decay and transgression; Poe moves it from the castle into the mind. |
| Unity of effect | Poe's principle, from his 1842 review of Hawthorne's *Twice-Told Tales*, that a tale should be readable at one sitting and that every element should serve a single preconceived effect. |
| Ambiguity | A deliberate openness the text sustains with evidence on more than one side. Different from vagueness. |

## The text in context

Edgar Allan Poe (1809-1849) earned his living as a magazine editor and critic, and his tales were written for magazines. "The Tell-Tale Heart" first appeared in January 1843 in *The Pioneer*, a Boston monthly edited by James Russell Lowell that lasted three issues, and Poe revised it for the *Broadway Journal* in 1845. The text most anthologies reprint, and the one quoted here, follows the 1850 collected *Works*.

The story belongs to a group of Poe's first-person "confession" tales, alongside "The Black Cat" (1843) and "The Imp of the Perverse" (1845), in which a murderer explains himself and in explaining himself gives himself away. Poe's theory of the short story matters here. In his review of Hawthorne he argued that the writer should decide on an effect first and invent incidents to produce it; nothing should be wasted. That is why this story has no names, no town, no date, no backstory for the narrator or his relation to the old man. The narrow focus is not a lack of detail. It is a choice that leaves the reader alone with one voice.

That isolation is what makes reliability the central question. There is no second witness, no omniscient narrator to correct the record, no letter or diary from the old man. Every judgment you make about the narrator has to be built from his own words.

## Close reading

**1. The opening argument.**

> TRUE! — nervous — very, very dreadfully nervous I had been and am; but why *will* you say that I am mad? The disease had sharpened my senses — not destroyed — not dulled them. [...] I heard all things in the heaven and in the earth. I heard many things in hell. How, then, am I mad?

*Observe:* the passage is an argument with a premise ("The disease had sharpened my senses"), evidence ("I heard all things") and a conclusion phrased as a challenge ("How, then, am I mad?"). It is also broken by dashes and exclamations.
*Interpret:* the evidence undermines the conclusion. Hearing "many things in hell" is offered as a credential, but it is exactly the kind of claim that would make a listener think him mad. The italic *will* tells us the listener has already decided.
*Function:* the opening teaches the reader how to read the rest. From the first paragraph we judge the narrator by the gap between what he argues and what his argument reveals. The story's suspense is not "will he do it?" (he tells us he did) but "how much will he show us without knowing it?"

**2. The image that replaces a man.**

> I think it was his eye! yes, it was this! One of his eyes resembled that of a vulture — a pale blue eye, with a film over it. Whenever it fell upon me, my blood ran cold; and so by degrees — very gradually — I made up my mind to take the life of the old man, and thus rid myself of the eye forever.

*Observe:* the visual image is precise and ordinary: pale, blue, filmed over, the eye of an old man, probably clouded by a cataract. The comparison to a vulture is the narrator's.
*Interpret:* the simile converts a frailty into a predator. A vulture waits for death; the narrator imagines the old man watching him the way a scavenger watches the dying. The last clause shows the result: he will kill the man to be rid of the *eye*.
*Function:* imagery here is how the narrator does his moral reasoning. By shrinking the person to a single feature he makes murder thinkable. For the reader, the mild literal image beside the violent reaction ("my blood ran cold") is the clearest evidence of disproportion. This is the 5.D and 4.D skills working together: the image is where the unreliability shows.

**3. The ray of light.**

> It was open — wide, wide open — and I grew furious as I gazed upon it. I saw it with perfect distinctness — all a dull blue, with a hideous veil over it that chilled the very marrow in my bones; but I could see nothing else of the old man's face or person: for I had directed the ray as if by instinct, precisely upon the damned spot.

*Observe:* a single beam lights one point in a dark room. The narrator says he could see "nothing else of the old man's face or person." The eye has become "a hideous veil," and the target is "the damned spot."
*Interpret:* the lantern beam is a picture of his attention. Everything human is in darkness; only the obsession is lit. "The damned spot" alludes to Lady Macbeth's sleepwalking scene ("Out, damned spot!"), where a murderer's guilt surfaces through a disordered mind.
*Function:* the image does double duty. For the narrator it shows precision and control ("as if by instinct, precisely"). For the reader it shows dehumanisation, and the Shakespearean echo foreshadows how this story will end: with guilt that speaks through the senses.

**4. The sound that will not stop.**

> And now have I not told you that what you mistake for madness is but over acuteness of the senses? — now, I say, there came to my ears a low, dull, quick sound, such as a watch makes when enveloped in cotton. I knew that sound well, too. It was the beating of the old man's heart. It increased my fury, as the beating of a drum stimulates the soldier into courage.

*Observe:* the narrator interrupts the climax to renew his argument with the listener; then an auditory image and two similes (the watch, the drum).
*Interpret:* "I knew that sound well, too" is ambiguous. He has just said he knew the old man's groan because the same groan had welled up in his own bosom; now he "knows" the heartbeat. A reader may ask whether the heartbeat he hears in the room is his own. The drum simile makes him a soldier charging bravely, a heroic frame no reader shares.
*Function:* the shift from sight to sound marks the turn of the plot. The eye is destroyed; the sound will survive the murder and return under the floorboards. The watch simile, repeated word for word at the end, ties the two scenes together.

## From reading to writing

Here is a body paragraph for a prose-analysis essay on the final scene. Read the annotations after it.

> Poe ends the story by letting the narrator's prized senses testify against him. While the officers "chatted pleasantly, and smiled," the narrator hears "a low, dull, quick sound — much such a sound as a watch makes when enveloped in cotton," the same simile he used on the night of the murder. **(1)** The repetition binds the two scenes: the sound that "increased [his] fury" in the dark chamber now increases his terror in daylight, and the muffled watch suggests both a hidden thing and time running out. **(2)** Yet everything the narrator *reports* about the officers contradicts what he *concludes*. They chat and smile; he decides "They heard! — they suspected! — they knew!" **(3)** The reader is left with two explanations the story never chooses between: the heart beats only in his guilt, or the "sound" is his own pulse, the over-acute hearing he once offered as proof of sanity now turned on himself. Either way, the evidence has come from inside the narrator, so the confession is the final proof of the claim he spent the story denying. **(4)**

1. Claim plus precise evidence, with the quotation embedded in the writer's own sentence.
2. Commentary on *function*: why the repetition matters, and what the simile's two halves (muffled, mechanical) contribute.
3. The move that makes reliability visible: separate what the narrator reports from what he infers.
4. Row C thinking: two readings weighed, and the paragraph connects them back to the story's opening argument rather than declaring one "correct."

**A weak version** reads: "The narrator is unreliable because he is crazy. At the end he hears the heart beating and confesses. This shows that guilt will always come out." It labels instead of analysing ("unreliable because he is crazy"), summarises the plot, and ends with a moral so general it could be about any crime story. It quotes nothing, and it never asks what the narrator's account is doing.

**Thesis models for this week.**

- *Reliability:* "By letting the narrator argue for his sanity with the very senses and methods that betray him, Poe turns the story into a trial in which the defendant supplies the evidence for his own conviction."
- *Imagery:* "Poe moves the story from the eye to the heart, from what the narrator cannot bear to see to what he cannot stop hearing, so that the images of his obsession become the measure of a guilt he never names."

## Common misreadings

- **"Unreliable means lying."** Poe's narrator is strikingly candid about what he did. What fails is his interpretation of it. Say which layer you doubt.
- **"The police heard the heart."** Nothing they do shows it. Treat the narrator's conclusion as a conclusion, not a fact.
- **"The old man must have been evil."** The narrator himself says "He had never wronged me." The vulture comparison is his, and tells us about him.
- **"It's obviously supernatural" (or "obviously not").** The story keeps the question open. A strong essay states the possibilities and explains why the ambiguity serves Poe's effect.
- **Diagnosing the narrator.** Avoid modern labels ("he has paranoid schizophrenia"). They are unprovable from the text and they replace analysis with a verdict. Describe what his words show: fixation, projection, grandiosity, contradiction.
- **"The narrator is a servant / son / caretaker."** The relation between the two men is never stated. Do not build a reading on a fact you have supplied.

## Connections and looking ahead

- **Back:** In week 12 the narrator of "The Yellow Wall-Paper" also narrates her own unravelling, but she writes in a journal and the reader sympathises with her against the men who confine her. Poe's narrator speaks to accuse his listener and invites no sympathy. Comparing them is a good exercise in how reliability and sympathy can separate. Hamlet (weeks 17-21) gives you a character who *pretends* to be mad; Poe gives you one who insists he is not.
- **Next week:** Melville's lawyer in "Bartleby, the Scrivener" is a different problem: sane, sensible, likeable, and still not to be trusted on the meaning of what he tells. Reliability is not only a feature of murderers.
- **Week 24:** Chesnutt's narrator is third person and largely reliable, but he narrows to Mr. Ryder's point of view. You will see that limited third person also raises questions of perspective.
- **On the exam:** Section I prose passages regularly ask about the narrator's attitude, the effect of a shift in perspective, or what a detail "reveals" about the narrator. In Section II, Question 2 (prose fiction analysis) often hands you a first-person passage; the essays that earn Row C usually see the gap between the speaker's view and the reader's.
