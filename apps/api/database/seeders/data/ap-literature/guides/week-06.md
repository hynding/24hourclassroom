# Week 06 · Simile and Metaphor, Comparison as Argument

**Spiral:** Week 4 gave you the speaker and the single word (4.A, 1.A, 5.B); week 5 gave you structure and contrast (3.C, 3.D). Comparisons use all of them: a simile reveals a speaker's perspective, and a sonnet's turn is often a turn from one comparison to another. The claim-and-evidence paragraph from Unit 1 (7.A) returns this week as the unit's writing goal.

## What you need to be able to do

- **6.A** Identify a simile (a comparison using "like", "as" or "than") and explain what it contributes to the poem.
- **6.B** Identify a metaphor (a comparison that identifies one thing with another), including implied and extended metaphors, and explain its function.
- **7.A** Write a paragraph about a poem that makes a claim needing defense and supports it with well-chosen evidence.

In plain language: when you see a comparison, you should be able to say what is being compared to what, which qualities carry over, and why the poet wanted those qualities in the poem. Then you should be able to build a paragraph on that explanation.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Simile | An explicit comparison with "like", "as" or "than": "O my Luve is like a red, red rose". |
| Metaphor | A comparison that says or implies one thing is another: "Hope is the thing with feathers". |
| Tenor | The subject of a comparison (love, hope, the speaker's soul). |
| Vehicle | The image used to describe the tenor (a rose, a bird, a river). |
| Ground | The qualities tenor and vehicle share: what the comparison carries over. |
| Implied metaphor | A comparison made through a verb or detail ("perches in the soul") rather than stated. |
| Extended (controlling) metaphor | A metaphor developed over several lines or a whole poem. |
| Conceit | An elaborate or surprising extended comparison; Sonnet 130 mocks the stock conceits of love poetry. |
| Hyperbole | Deliberate exaggeration ("Till a' the seas gang dry"). |
| Petrarchan conventions | The stock comparisons of Renaissance love poetry (eyes like suns, lips like coral, cheeks like roses), named after the Italian poet Petrarch. |

## How to explain a comparison

Every comparison question on the AP exam is answered the same way.

1. **Name the tenor and the vehicle.** "Dickinson compares hope (tenor) to a small bird (vehicle)."
2. **Find the ground.** Which qualities of the vehicle matter here? A bird is small, sings, perches, can be battered by storms, needs food. The poem chooses some and denies one: this bird never asks for a crumb.
3. **State the function.** What does the comparison argue or reveal? "The metaphor presents hope as slight in appearance but tireless, most audible in hardship, and entirely generous."

Students lose points by stopping at step 1 ("This is a metaphor comparing hope to a bird"). Identifying the device is never enough: the CED skill is to **explain its function**.

## The texts in context

**Shakespeare's sonnets** (1609) include poems to a young man (1-126), among them Sonnet 18, and poems about a woman often called the "dark lady" (127-152), among them Sonnet 130. Love poets of the period inherited from Petrarch a set of conventional comparisons; Sonnet 18 tries one and finds it too small, while Sonnet 130 parodies a whole list of them.

**Robert Burns (1759-1796)**, Scotland's national poet, collected and rewrote traditional songs. "A Red, Red Rose" dates from 1794 and was printed in song collections of the 1790s. Its Scots words ("luve", "bonnie lass", "gang dry", "weel") give it the voice of a folk song. The Poetry Foundation text quoted here mixes Scots and standard spellings ("luve" and "love" both appear).

**Emily Dickinson**'s poem appeared in *Poems, Second Series* (1891), five years after her death, under the editors' title "Hope". The editors did not keep the quotation marks around "Hope" that later scholarly editions print from her manuscript, so the 1891 text begins "Hope is the thing with feathers".

**Langston Hughes** wrote "The Negro Speaks of Rivers" as a teenager, on a train crossing the Mississippi, and it appeared in *The Crisis* in June 1921, among the first poems he published nationally. Collected in *The Weary Blues* (1926), it was dedicated there to W. E. B. Du Bois. Its long lines and repeated refrain draw on the cadences of the spirituals and the Bible.

## Close reading

### 1. A comparison that fails upward: Sonnet 18

> Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?
> Thou art more lovely and more temperate:
> Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May,
> And summer's lease hath all too short a date:

**Observe.** The poem opens with a question about comparison itself, and the second line answers: the beloved is "more lovely and more temperate". Then summer's faults: rough winds, a short "lease", a sun ("the eye of heaven") sometimes too hot and sometimes "dimm'd".

**Interpret.** The simile is proposed and found inadequate. "Lease" is a metaphor from property: summer only rents its beauty for a short term. At line 9 the poem turns: "But thy eternal summer shall not fade". Now "summer" is a metaphor for the beloved's youth and beauty, and it will not end, "When in eternal lines to time thou grow'st".

**Function.** The comparison does argumentative work. By showing that nature's summer decays, Shakespeare sets up the claim of the couplet: "So long lives this, and this gives life to thee." "This" is the poem. The failed simile becomes proof that only poetry can preserve beauty, so the sonnet is as much about the power of verse as about the beloved.

### 2. A comparison that fails downward: Sonnet 130

> My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun;
> Coral is far more red, than her lips red:
> If snow be white, why then her breasts are dun;
> If hairs be wires, black wires grow on her head.

**Observe.** Every line tests a stock comparison (sun, coral, snow, golden wire) and finds it false. "Nothing like" negates a simile; "far more red, than" turns a comparison against her; "If hairs be wires" takes a metaphor so literally it becomes absurd.

**Interpret.** The target is the convention, not the woman. The final quatrain sets plain fact against the most inflated comparison of all: "I grant I never saw a goddess go,— / My mistress, when she walks, treads on the ground". Then the couplet: "And yet by heaven, I think my love as rare, / As any she belied with false compare."

**Function.** "False compare" names the poem's argument: flattering comparisons are lies, and a love that refuses them is more honest, and so more "rare". Read with Sonnet 18, it shows that a poet can use comparison to praise, to criticize comparison, or both.

### 3. An extended metaphor: Dickinson's "Hope"

> Hope is the thing with feathers
> That perches in the soul,
> And sings the tune without the words,
> And never stops at all,

**Observe.** The vehicle is never named as "bird" in the first line, only "the thing with feathers". It perches, sings a wordless tune, never stops. Stanza 2 puts it in a gale; stanza 3 shifts to the speaker's own testimony: "I've heard it in the chillest land, / And on the strangest sea".

**Interpret.** Each detail of the bird is a claim about hope. Perching in the soul: hope lives inside us, lightly. A "tune without the words": hope is felt before it can be explained. "Sweetest in the gale": we hear hope most in hard times. The last line overturns the one thing a real bird would need: "Yet, never, in extremity, / It asked a crumb of me."

**Function.** The extended metaphor is the poem's entire argument. Its structure (definition, trial, testimony) moves from general truth to the speaker's experience, and its final line turns praise into gratitude. When you write about an extended metaphor, trace how the vehicle develops, not just that it exists.

### 4. Simile, refrain and a collective speaker: "The Negro Speaks of Rivers"

> My soul has grown deep like the rivers.

**Observe.** This simile appears twice, after the opening and as the last line. Between them, the speaker lists four rivers and his acts beside them: "I bathed in the Euphrates when dawns were young. / I built my hut near the Congo and it lulled me to sleep. / I looked upon the Nile and raised the pyramids above it."

**Interpret.** The ground of the simile is depth gained over time: rivers "ancient as the world" carve deep beds, and the speaker's soul has grown deep through a history that runs from the first civilizations through Africa to the Mississippi. The "I" is collective: no one person bathed in the ancient Euphrates and also heard the Mississippi "when Abe Lincoln went down to New Orleans".

**Function.** The repeated simile gives the speaker's identity a dignity and age equal to the rivers', and the refrain frames the historical journey with a single proud claim. The final image of the Mississippi, whose "muddy bosom turn[s] all golden in the sunset", uses metaphor to transform a river tied to slavery into one of beauty.

## From reading to writing: the comparison paragraph (7.A)

**Model paragraph (on Burns)**

> Burns's similes and hyperboles together present love as something both freshly new and impossibly lasting. **[claim]** The opening comparison, "O my Luve is like a red, red rose / That's newly sprung in June", stresses freshness and intensity; the doubled "red, red" and "newly sprung" make the feeling vivid and young. **[evidence + commentary]** Yet the poem refuses to let that freshness fade the way a rose does: the speaker will love "Till a' the seas gang dry" and "the rocks melt wi' the sun", impossible events that stretch his vow to the end of the world. **[evidence + commentary]** The tension between a June rose, which lasts weeks, and seas that never dry is the poem's point: the speaker asks for a love as new as spring and as old as the earth. **[link and complexity]**

**A weak version:** "Burns uses a simile when he says his love is like a red rose. He also uses hyperbole. This shows he really loves her." It identifies devices but never explains their ground or function, and its claim does not need defending.

## Common misreadings

- **Identifying without explaining.** "This is a metaphor" earns nothing. Explain the ground and the function.
- **Reading Sonnet 130 as an insult.** The couplet is the key: the speaker's love is "rare"; the false comparisons are the target.
- **Treating "this" in Sonnet 18 as the summer day.** "This" is the poem.
- **Importing qualities the poem does not use.** Roses have thorns, but Burns never mentions them; build your reading on the words the poet chose.
- **Assuming the "I" is always one person.** Hughes's speaker, like Dunbar's "we", speaks for a people.
