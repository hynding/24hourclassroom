## What you need to be able to do

- **4.D** Judge how far an account can be trusted and explain how that affects the story. A play has no narrator, so in drama the skill applies to characters who report offstage events, characters who interpret others, characters who present themselves to a listener they want to manage, and sources such as the Ghost.
- **1.C** Explain what contrasting characters do: what they share with the main character, how they differ, and what the difference makes the audience see or judge.

In plain terms, Act II is an act about information. Almost everyone is trying to find out what is wrong with Hamlet, and Hamlet is trying to find out whether the Ghost told the truth. Your job is to weigh every report and every comparison.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Reliability (in drama) | How far the audience should trust what a character reports or claims, judged by access (could they know?), motive (do they want something?) and inference (do they go beyond the evidence?). |
| Reported action | An event that happens offstage and reaches us only through a character's account, like Ophelia's encounter with Hamlet in 2.1. |
| Unreliable interpreter | A character who reports accurately but explains wrongly; Polonius is the type. |
| Foil | A character whose similarity to the protagonist makes the differences stand out. |
| Prose mask | Hamlet's quick, punning prose in front of spies, the language of the "antic disposition." |
| Play within the play | A performance staged inside the play; Hamlet prepares one at the end of this act. |
| Chiasmus | A crossed repetition (A-B, B-A): "What’s Hecuba to him, or he to Hecuba." |

## The text in context

Act II begins some weeks after Act I. Polonius sends his servant Reynaldo to Paris to spy on Laertes, teaching him to "By indirections find directions out." Ophelia then runs in to describe a terrifying visit from Hamlet. Polonius decides at once that Hamlet is mad for love and hurries to the King.

The long second scene is a sequence of interviews. Claudius and Gertrude welcome Rosencrantz and Guildenstern, Hamlet's schoolfellows, and ask them to discover what afflicts him. Ambassadors report that the King of Norway has stopped young Fortinbras's attack on Denmark and redirected it against Poland. Polonius, at tedious length, presents his theory and a plan: he will "loose" his daughter to Hamlet while he and the King watch. Hamlet then takes on each visitor in turn: he baits Polonius ("Words, words, words"), unmasks the schoolfellows ("Were you not sent for?"), and welcomes the players, asking for a speech about Pyrrhus and Priam. Alone at last, he condemns himself, doubts the Ghost, and plans a play to "catch the conscience of the king."

The theatre was never far from Shakespeare's mind. The players' arrival lets him write about acting, audiences and what a performance can make people feel or confess, inside a play performed at the Globe.

## Close reading

### 1. A report we cannot check (2.1.87-111)

> My lord, as I was sewing in my chamber,
> Lord Hamlet,—with his doublet all unbrac’d;
> No hat upon his head; his stockings foul’d,
> [...]
> Pale as his shirt; his knees knocking each other;
> And with a look so piteous in purport
> As if he had been loosed out of hell
> To speak of horrors,—he comes before me.

**Observe.** The account is exact: unfastened doublet, no hat, fallen stockings, pale face, knocking knees. Later he holds her wrist, studies her face "As he would draw it," sighs, and leaves without taking his eyes off her. He says nothing.

**Interpret.** As testimony, Ophelia is reliable. She has seen it, she is frightened, she has nothing to gain. As interpretation she claims nothing: asked "Mad for thy love?" she answers, "I do not know; / But truly I do fear it." The imagery she uses, of a figure "loosed out of hell / To speak of horrors," unknowingly makes Hamlet a ghost, which only the audience, having seen 1.5, can understand.

**Function (4.D).** Because the scene is reported, Hamlet's state stays ambiguous. One scene earlier he told us he might "put an antic disposition on." Is this the performance beginning, real anguish after the Ghost, or grief at losing Ophelia? Shakespeare will not let us see for ourselves. Then he gives the interpretation to the least reliable reader on stage: Polonius decides it is "the very ecstasy of love," a theory that flatters his own judgment, since he ordered Ophelia to reject Hamlet.

### 2. Hamlet reporting himself (2.2)

To his schoolfellows, once they admit they were sent for, Hamlet says:

> I have of late,—but wherefore I know not,—lost all my mirth, forgone all custom of exercises; and indeed, it goes so heavily with my disposition that this goodly frame, the earth, seems to me a sterile promontory

and goes on to call man, for all his reason and beauty, a "quintessence of dust."

**Observe.** It is prose, beautifully shaped, and its picture of a dead world matches the first soliloquy's "weary, stale, flat, and unprofitable."

**Interpret.** "Wherefore I know not" is the giveaway. Hamlet knows exactly why: the Ghost. He is telling the King's spies a true symptom and a false cause.

**Function (4.D).** A speaker can be unreliable on purpose. The audience, who knows what the schoolfellows do not, hears the speech as both sincere and strategic, the "antic disposition" at its most sophisticated. Compare "I am but mad north-north-west," a confession of control dressed as a joke.

### 3. The Player and Pyrrhus as foils (2.2)

Hamlet asks the First Player for Aeneas's tale of Priam's death. In it Pyrrhus, avenging his father Achilles, stands over the old king with his sword raised:

> So, as a painted tyrant, Pyrrhus stood;
> And, like a neutral to his will and matter,
> Did nothing.

Then he strikes. The Player, reciting Hecuba's grief, turns pale and weeps.

**Observe.** Two figures mirror Hamlet. Pyrrhus is a son avenging a father, frozen for a moment between will and deed. The Player feels a fictional grief so fully that his body shows it.

**Interpret.** Pyrrhus pictures Hamlet's paralysis, and then does what Hamlet has not done. The Player shows what passion looks like when it is expressed, which is what Hamlet thinks he lacks.

**Function (1.C).** The foils provoke the soliloquy:

> What’s Hecuba to him, or he to Hecuba,
> That he should weep for her? What would he do,
> Had he the motive and the cue for passion
> That I have?

The contrast shames Hamlet, but notice what it also reveals. He is measuring his grief against an actor's display. The prince who told his mother he had "that within which passeth show" now reproaches himself for not showing enough. And Pyrrhus, the revenger who acts, is a butcher. The foils sharpen the question of delay without simply answering it.

### 4. Testing a source (end of 2.2)

> The spirit that I have seen
> May be the devil: and the devil hath power
> To assume a pleasing shape

**Observe.** For the first time Hamlet questions the Ghost. Many in Shakespeare's audience believed that apparitions might be devils exploiting the melancholy.

**Interpret.** The Ghost is the only witness to the murder. Hamlet wants "grounds / More relative than this," evidence closer to the fact.

**Function (4.D and plot).** Doubt about a source becomes a plan: "The play’s the thing / Wherein I’ll catch the conscience of the king." Reliability is not only a reading skill here; it is what drives the plot into Act III.

## From reading to writing

**Prompt-style task:** Explain how Shakespeare uses contrasting characters in Act II to develop the question of Hamlet's delay.

**Model paragraph:**

> Act II surrounds Hamlet with men who turn feeling into action, and each comparison sharpens the question of why he has not. The ambassadors report that Fortinbras, rebuked by his uncle, has turned his army against Poland, converting grievance into a campaign within a few lines. In the Player's speech, Pyrrhus, another son avenging a father, stands "like a neutral to his will and matter" and "Did nothing," a perfect image of Hamlet's paralysis, but then he strikes. Most painfully, the Player weeps for Hecuba, "all for nothing," while Hamlet, who has "the motive and the cue for passion," calls himself "A dull and muddy-mettled rascal." Yet the comparisons are less damning than Hamlet thinks. Fortinbras's speed is that of a man who does not reflect, Pyrrhus's vengeance is butchery, and the Player's tears are a technique. By placing Hamlet among these foils, Shakespeare makes delay look shameful and thought look necessary, and leaves the audience divided between the two judgments.

**Why it works.** The claim is arguable. Three foils are used, each with a quotation, and the commentary says what each contrast shows. The final sentences move from comparison to complexity: the foils condemn Hamlet and are themselves open to judgment.

**A weak version:** "Fortinbras, Pyrrhus and the Player are foils to Hamlet. They all do things and Hamlet does not. This shows Hamlet delays." It identifies foils but explains nothing about what the contrast reveals.

## Common misreadings

- **"Ophelia is lying, or imagining it."** Nothing supports this. Her report is precise and fearful. The uncertainty is about what Hamlet's behaviour means.
- **"Polonius is right that it is love-madness."** The play steadily undercuts him: his tedium, his spying, his eagerness to be proved right. He is an unreliable interpreter.
- **"Hamlet is simply mad now."** Polonius himself notices "method" in it, and Hamlet tells the spies he is mad only "north-north-west." The audience is meant to keep both possibilities open.
- **"The Player's speech is a digression."** It is a mirror. Pyrrhus and the Player are foils, and the speech triggers both the soliloquy and the plan.
- **"Hamlet doubts the Ghost because he is a coward."** The doubt was theologically serious. Testing it is a rational step, even if it is also a way of putting off action.
