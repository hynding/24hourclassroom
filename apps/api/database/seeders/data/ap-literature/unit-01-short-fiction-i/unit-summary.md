# Unit 1 Summary · Short Fiction I

Three short stories, three weeks, and the habits the rest of the year is built on: reading a character through details, telling the narrator apart from the author, reading setting as more than background, explaining why a plot puts events in the order it does, and writing a paragraph that argues instead of retelling. Short fiction carries the largest share of the multiple-choice section of the AP exam (about 42-49% across all short-fiction passages), and the Q2 essay is always a prose fiction passage, so these skills pay off directly in May.

## The skills, as this unit used them

| Skill | What it meant in Unit 1 |
|---|---|
| **1.A** Character details | Della counting her coins "Three times"; the boy's "chalice" fantasy; Mathilde, who "suffered ceaselessly." Read what a character does, says, owns and imagines, and say what it reveals about perspective and motive. |
| **2.A** Setting details | The "blind" street, the violet winter sky, the market "hostile to romance," the dark bazaar; Mathilde's bare walls against her imagined tapestries. A setting conveys a state of mind. |
| **3.A** Plot order | Chronological order, but uneven pacing: months of devotion in a paragraph and one Saturday night over pages; one night of a ball against ten years summarised. |
| **3.B** Function of a sequence | Triumph juxtaposed with loss; information withheld until the last line; delays piled up so the boy arrives as the lights go out. |
| **4.A** The narrator | O. Henry's intrusive outside storyteller; Joyce's adult looking back on his boyhood; Maupassant's cool, judging observer. |
| **4.B** Function of point of view | Staying with Della hides Jim's secret; the retrospective "I" lets us feel the boy's pain and see his self-deception; one judging clause gives ironic distance from Mathilde. |
| **7.A** Claim and evidence | A claim must need defending; evidence must prove the arguable part; commentary answers "so what?" |

## Week 1 · "The Gift of the Magi" (O. Henry, 1905)

**Key moves.** The narrator is outside the story but constantly present: he addresses "you," jokes ("sobs, sniffles, and smiles, with sniffles predominating"), and stage-manages the reader ("let us regard with discreet scrutiny some inconsequential object in the other direction"). The narration stays with Della, so Jim's sale of the watch is a surprise that completes the double irony: her chain for his watch, his combs for her hair. The last paragraph reverses the practical verdict ("two foolish children") into a moral one ("They are the magi").

**What to remember.** Identify a narrator with evidence (pronouns, participation, commentary), then explain what the narration does. Humour here is a form of respect: it keeps poverty from becoming pity.

## Week 2 · "Araby" (James Joyce, *Dubliners*, 1914)

**Key moves.** Setting reflects mind. The dead-end street with "brown imperturbable faces," the "feeble lanterns" under a violet sky, and the market where the boy imagines he bears "my chalice safely through a throng of foes" all chart a romantic imagination pressing against a drab world. The plot is ordered to arrive at one moment: delays (the uncle, the visitor, the slow train), a nearly dark bazaar with "a silence like that which pervades a church after a service," a trivial flirtation, and the light going out. The ending is an **epiphany**: "Gazing up into the darkness I saw myself as a creature driven and derided by vanity."

**What to remember.** The narrator is a man recalling his boyhood; words like "foolish blood" and "confused adoration" are his. Light belongs to the boy's idealised love, darkness to its end. The story stops at the recognition on purpose.

## Week 3 · "The Necklace" (Guy de Maupassant, 1884; early English translation "The Diamond Necklace")

**Key moves.** The story opens with desire, not action: a woman born "as if by a slip of fate, into a family of clerks," tortured by a home that "another woman of her rank would never even have been conscious" of. A causal chain follows: invitation, dress (her husband's four hundred francs), borrowed diamonds, triumph, loss, concealment, thirty-six thousand francs of debt, ten years of labour. Triumph is juxtaposed with loss; one night is told in detail while a decade is summarised; the truth that the necklace was imitation is withheld until the last line, which recasts the whole sacrifice as needless.

**What to remember.** The loss is chance; the ruin is a choice (to conceal rather than confess). Withholding information makes the reader live through the sacrifice as necessary before learning it was not.

## Vocabulary to own

Narrator, speaker, first-person, third-person, omniscient, limited, intrusive (editorial) narrator, retrospective narration, point of view, direct and indirect characterization, motive, tone and mood, setting, atmosphere, exposition, rising action, climax, resolution, pacing (scene and summary), causal chain, juxtaposition, reversal, withheld information, twist ending, epiphany, situational irony, allusion, claim, evidence, commentary, line of reasoning.

## Essay moves practised

- **The claim-evidence-commentary paragraph.** Claim first; short, embedded quotation; at least as much commentary as quotation; a closing link back to the claim.
- **Turning a fact into a claim.** "The Loisels replace the necklace" becomes "The Loisels' decision to replace the necklace, not its loss, is what ruins them."
- **The "so what?" test.** After each piece of evidence, explain what it reveals and how it supports the claim.
- **Comparing works.** Three gift-and-loss stories ending in irony give you ready material for a Q3 literary argument on sacrifice, illusion or revelation.

## Common traps

- Writing "the author says" when you mean the narrator.
- Labelling the point of view ("third person") without explaining its function.
- Treating "I" as proof of a first-person participant (O. Henry's storyteller says "I" but is not in the story).
- Reading setting as backdrop rather than as evidence about character.
- Calling the ending of "The Necklace" bad luck, or the ending of "Araby" unfinished.
- Writing plot summary and calling it commentary.

## What the unit exam looks like

Forty-four questions in about 80 minutes. Four passage sets of seven to ten questions each: new passages from the three stories, and one **unseen** passage, the complete sixth chapter of Kate Chopin's *The Awakening* (1899), in which Edna Pontellier's inner "light" begins to dawn and the sea calls to her soul. Expect AP-style stems ("The narrator's attitude toward X is best described as...", "The phrase X primarily serves to...", "Which best describes the function of..."), plus select-all, true/false and fill-in items. Ten questions range across the unit, including two short written responses (a sequence explanation and a comparative claim-and-evidence paragraph). The unseen passage ends with an AP-style **prose fiction analysis essay** scored on the 6-point rubric: thesis (0-1), evidence and commentary (0-4), sophistication (0-1).

To prepare: reread the passages quoted in the three guides, practise describing each narrator in one sentence and its function in another, rehearse the plot chain of "The Necklace," and write one timed paragraph on an unfamiliar passage, starting from the narrator.
