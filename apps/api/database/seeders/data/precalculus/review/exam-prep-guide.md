# Exam Prep Guide · Precalculus

**Use it:** in week 33, before the practice final in week 34
**Covers:** all four units; the AP Precalculus exam tests Units 1–3 only
**Framework:** AP Precalculus Course and Exam Description; exam format from AP Central, May 2027 exam

This guide does three jobs. It explains the shape of the AP Precalculus exam as it will be given in May 2027, it shows how the course's practice final is built to rehearse that exam, and it collects, unit by unit, the facts and procedures you should be able to use without looking anything up. It ends with a two-week review plan. If you are taking Precalculus as a regular course rather than an AP course, the whole practice final is your final exam; the format notes still tell you how it is organised.

## The AP exam in May 2027

The exam is on **Tuesday, May 11, 2027**, in the first session of the day. Multiple-choice questions are answered on a device in the Bluebook app; free-response answers are handwritten in a paper booklet. The College Board updated the number of multiple-choice questions and the timing for 2027; the course content did not change.

| Section | Part | Questions | Time | Calculator | Share of score |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I: Multiple choice | A | 29 | 65 minutes | not allowed | 43.75% |
| I: Multiple choice | B | 13 | 40 minutes | graphing calculator required | 18.75% |
| II: Free response | A | 2 (questions 1 and 2) | 35 minutes | graphing calculator required | 18.75% |
| II: Free response | B | 2 (questions 3 and 4) | 35 minutes | not allowed | 18.75% |

Multiple choice is 62.5% of the score and free response 37.5%. There is no penalty for a wrong answer, so never leave a multiple-choice question blank.

The four free-response questions always have the same jobs:

1. **Function Concepts** (calculator). Work with functions given as formulas, tables or graphs: composition, inverses, values, zeros, limits and end behaviour, and rates of change. Expect to justify a claim about increasing, decreasing or concavity using average rates of change.
2. **Modeling a Non-Periodic Context** (calculator). A situation and data that call for a linear, quadratic, polynomial, rational, exponential or logarithmic model. You build the model, use it, interpret its numbers with units, and comment on its assumptions or limitations.
3. **Modeling a Periodic Context** (no calculator). A sinusoidal situation such as a wheel, a tide or a spring. You find the amplitude, period, phase shift and midline from a description or table, write the function, and reason about where it increases, decreases and changes concavity.
4. **Symbolic Manipulations** (no calculator). Rewrite and solve: exponential and logarithmic expressions and equations, trigonometric identities and equations, and domain questions that the rewriting raises.

## How the practice final maps onto it

The practice final has 70 questions. **Questions 1–46 are a full mock AP exam** on Units 1–3:

- Questions 1–29: Section I, Part A. No calculator. Units 1, 2 and 3 in proportion (11, 9 and 9 questions).
- Questions 30–42: Section I, Part B. Graphing calculator. These are marked "(Calculator.)".
- Questions 43–46: Section II, the four free-response types in AP order. Each is scored out of 6 here with the scoring notes your teacher uses.

Sit questions 1–46 in one block with the AP timings above (3 hours 5 minutes, plus a 10-minute break after question 42), then score yourself. The College Board sets the score cut points each year and does not publish them in advance, so don't try to convert your total into a 1–5 score; use the topic of every lost point to decide where to spend your review time.

**Questions 47–70** are not AP format. Fourteen of them cover Unit 4 (parametric functions, conic sections, vectors and matrices), which the AP exam leaves out but a full precalculus course includes, and the rest practise the item types the AP exam doesn't use: select-all, true/false, fill-in-the-blank, numeric entry and short answers. Do them on another day, in about 40 minutes.

## Know these cold

### Unit 1

- Average rate of change of f on [a, b]: (f(b) − f(a))/(b − a). Increasing average rates over consecutive equal intervals means concave up; decreasing means concave down.
- Linear functions have constant rates of change; quadratics have rates of change that change at a constant rate (constant second differences).
- A point of inflection is where the rate of change switches from increasing to decreasing, or the reverse.
- A polynomial of degree n has exactly n complex zeros counting multiplicity; non-real zeros of a real polynomial come in conjugate pairs a ± bi. Odd multiplicity crosses the x-axis, even multiplicity touches and turns.
- End behaviour: the leading term decides. Rational functions: higher degree on the bottom gives y = 0; equal degrees give the ratio of leading coefficients; one degree higher on top gives a slant asymptote found by division.
- Rational functions: a factor that cancels gives a hole (find its y-value from the simplified form); a denominator factor that does not cancel gives a vertical asymptote, written with one-sided limits such as lim (x → 2⁺) r(x) = ∞.
- Remainder theorem: the remainder on dividing p(x) by x − a is p(a). Binomial theorem coefficients come from Pascal's triangle.
- g(x) = a · f(b(x − h)) + k: vertical dilation by a (reflection if a < 0), horizontal dilation by 1/b, shift right h and up k.
- A model's answer needs units and a sentence of meaning; a model's domain is limited by its context.

### Unit 2

- Arithmetic sequences add a constant: aₙ = a₀ + dn. Geometric sequences multiply by a constant: gₙ = g₀ · rⁿ. Linear functions are their continuous version of adding; exponential functions of multiplying.
- f(x) = a · bˣ with a > 0: growth if b > 1, decay if 0 < b < 1, always concave up, horizontal asymptote y = 0.
- b^(x + k) = bᵏ · bˣ: a horizontal shift of an exponential is a vertical dilation. (bᵐ)ⁿ = b^(mn) lets you change the period of growth, for example 1.05 per year is 1.05^(1/12) per month.
- Growth by r percent per period: factor 1 + r. Doubling time T: f(t) = a · 2^(t/T). Half-life H: f(t) = a · (1/2)^(t/H). Continuous growth: a · e^(kt).
- Residual = actual − predicted. A pattern in the residuals means the model type is wrong.
- f(g(x)) applies g first; its domain is the inputs where g is defined and g(x) is in the domain of f. Inverses swap inputs and outputs and reflect the graph in y = x; check with f(f⁻¹(x)) = x.
- log_b c = a means bᵃ = c. log_b(mn) = log_b m + log_b n; log_b(m/n) = log_b m − log_b n; log_b(mᵏ) = k log_b m; log_b x = ln x / ln b.
- Log equations can create extraneous solutions: check every answer in the original equation's arguments.
- On a semi-log plot (log y against x), exponential data lie on a line: log y = (log b)x + log a.

### Unit 3

- Radians: s = rθ; π radians = 180°. On the unit circle the point at angle θ is (cos θ, sin θ), and tan θ = sin θ / cos θ is the slope of the terminal ray.
- Exact values at π/6, π/4, π/3 and their reference angles in all four quadrants, with signs.
- f(θ) = a sin(b(θ + c)) + d: amplitude |a|, period 2π/|b|, phase shift −c, midline y = d. Tangent has period π/|b|.
- Ranges of the inverse functions: sin⁻¹ and tan⁻¹ give angles in [−π/2, π/2] (open interval for tan⁻¹); cos⁻¹ gives angles in [0, π].
- Solve trig equations in [0, 2π) first, using reference angles and quadrant signs; add 2πn (or πn for tangent) for all solutions.
- sin²θ + cos²θ = 1, 1 + tan²θ = sec²θ, 1 + cot²θ = csc²θ. sin(α ± β) = sin α cos β ± cos α sin β; cos(α ± β) = cos α cos β ∓ sin α sin β; sin 2θ = 2 sin θ cos θ; cos 2θ = cos²θ − sin²θ = 1 − 2sin²θ = 2cos²θ − 1.
- Polar: x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ, r² = x² + y². a + bi = r(cos θ + i sin θ).
- Polar rates: when r > 0 and increasing, the point moves away from the origin; when r < 0 and decreasing, |r| grows, so it also moves away.

### Unit 4

- Parametric motion: position (x(t), y(t)); rates of change of x and y with respect to t; slope of the path over an interval Δy/Δx.
- Circle: x = h + r cos t, y = k + r sin t. Segment from P to Q: P + t(Q − P), 0 ≤ t ≤ 1.
- Ellipse (x − h)²/a² + (y − k)²/b² = 1; hyperbola with a minus sign; parabola x² = 4py with focus (0, p). Complete the square to identify a conic.
- Vectors: |⟨a, b⟩| = √(a² + b²); unit vector v/|v|; u · v = u₁v₁ + u₂v₂ = |u||v| cos θ.
- Matrices: multiply row by column; AB ≠ BA in general; det [[a, b], [c, d]] = ad − bc; the inverse is (1/det)[[d, −b], [−c, a]] when det ≠ 0.
- A transition matrix moves a state vector forward one step; a steady state is unchanged by it.

## Showing work on free response

Free-response scoring rewards specific things, and the scoring notes in this course follow the same habits:

- **Write the setup, not just the answer.** "f(4) = 11/3, so g(f(4)) = 3 ln(11/3) + 1 ≈ 4.898" earns the point; "4.898" alone may not.
- **Decimals:** round final answers to three decimal places unless told otherwise, and don't round in the middle of a calculation.
- **Use limit notation when asked for end behaviour or asymptote behaviour.** Words alone ("it goes to 2") usually lose the point.
- **Justify with the right evidence.** Concavity from data needs average rates of change over consecutive equal intervals, and the sentence "these are decreasing, so the graph is concave down".
- **Units and context.** A rate of change of −2.8 needs "degrees Celsius per minute" and a sentence about what is happening.
- **Exact answers in Part B of Section II.** Without a calculator, leave 25 − 10√2 and 8/3 exact.
- **Don't erase work you are unsure of; cross it out.** Crossed-out work is not scored, but an unfinished correct start can still earn a point.

## Calculator checklist

Before the exam, make sure you can do each of these on your own graphing calculator in under a minute: graph a function in a sensible window; find zeros, maximums, minimums and intersections; build a table of values; run linear, quadratic, cubic, exponential, logarithmic and sinusoidal regressions and read the coefficients; and switch between radian and degree mode. **Leave it in radian mode.** A daylight model evaluated in degree mode is one of the most common ways to lose a Part B point, and one of the practice final's distractors is built from exactly that mistake.

## A two-week review plan

**Week 33 (this week): rebuild the toolkit.**

- Day 1: Unit 1 summary sheet, then redo the Unit 1 exam's free-response items without looking at your first attempt.
- Day 2: Unit 2 summary sheet; drill logarithm rules and solving exponential and logarithmic equations for 30 minutes.
- Day 3: Unit 3 summary sheet; write the unit circle from memory twice, then solve ten trig equations on [0, 2π).
- Day 4: Flashcards for every week you marked weak, then the Unit 3 exam's periodic-modelling question.
- Day 5: Calculator day: the checklist above, then the Part B questions from the three unit exams.

**Week 34: the practice final.**

- Day 1: Sit questions 1–46 under AP timing, in one sitting.
- Day 2: Score it with the explanations and scoring notes. For every miss, write one line: what the question tested and what you will do differently.
- Day 3: Re-teach yourself the two topics that cost you the most points, using their weekly guides.
- Day 4: Questions 47–70 (Unit 4 and mixed types), timed at 40 minutes.
- Day 5: Redo every free-response question you scored under 4 out of 6, then stop. Rest matters more than one more set.

In the weeks between the practice final and the exam, alternate one timed multiple-choice block with one free-response question every other day, and keep the flashcards going in short sessions.

## Test-day reminders

- Bring a charged, approved graphing calculator and a spare battery or charger, and a pencil and pen for the booklet.
- In Section I, mark any question that takes longer than two minutes and come back to it. Answer everything.
- In Section II, read all parts of a question before you start; later parts often reuse an earlier answer, so a wrong earlier answer used correctly can still earn later points.
- Check that every answer to a context question has units and a sentence of meaning.
