# Unit 4 · Period 4: 1800–1848 · The night-before sheet

**Exam weight:** 10–17% of the AP exam · **CED topics:** 4.1 to 4.14 · **Themes:** PCE, WXT, NAT, SOC, ARC, MIG, WOR
**Built from:** Jefferson's First Inaugural (1801) and letters to Livingston (1802) and Holmes (1820); Marbury (1803), McCulloch (1819), Gibbons (1824) and Worcester (1832); the Missouri Compromise (1820); the Monroe Doctrine (1823); Jackson's 1830 message, Bank Veto and Nullification Proclamation (1832); Tocqueville (1835); Emerson (1837); Douglass (1845); the Declaration of Sentiments (1848)

## The ideas that generate every question

1. **Democracy expanded for white men and narrowed for others.** Property tests fell, mass parties rose, turnout climbed to about 80% in 1840, and politicians spoke for "the farmers, mechanics, and laborers." At the same time free Black men lost the vote in several states, women stayed excluded, and Native nations were removed.
2. **The market revolution knit the nation together and pulled the regions apart.** Canals, steamboats, factories and the telegraph tied the North and West; cotton tied the South to slavery and to world markets.
3. **The fight over federal power never stopped.** Strict versus loose construction, the bank, the tariff, internal improvements and nullification ran from Jefferson to Jackson; the Marshall Court built federal and judicial power throughout.
4. **Religion and reform were partners.** The Second Great Awakening's free will and perfectionism produced temperance, schools, asylums, utopias, abolition and women's rights.
5. **Slavery grew and its defence hardened.** The enslaved population more than tripled from 1790 to 1840; after 1830 the South answered abolitionism and revolt with the positive-good argument, harsher codes and the gag rule.
6. **The U.S. secured the continent.** Louisiana, the War of 1812, Adams–Onís and the Monroe Doctrine made the U.S. the dominant power in North America and set up Manifest Destiny.

## Chains to know

- **Causation:** cotton gin → profitable short-staple cotton → expansion into the Deep South → Indian removal and domestic slave trade → slavery entrenched → sectional conflict over territories.
- **Causation:** Second Great Awakening → perfectionism → reform societies → abolition → women in abolition → exclusion in 1840 → Seneca Falls (1848).
- **Causation:** Embargo → New England hardship → opposition to the war → Hartford Convention → Federalist collapse → Era of Good Feelings → Missouri crisis and Panic of 1819 → second party system.
- **Continuity and change:** the bank debate 1791 → 1811 → 1816 → 1819 (McCulloch) → 1832 (veto) → 1836 (end).
- **Continuity and change:** Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions (1798) → Hartford Convention (1814) → nullification (1832) → secession (1860).
- **Comparison:** Democrats (limited government, expansion, small farmers, immigrants, the South) versus Whigs (American System, moral reform, commerce, New England); both bisectional, so both dodged slavery.
- **Comparison:** North (free labour, factories, cities, immigrants, reform) versus South (enslaved labour, cotton, few cities, proslavery defence).

## Thesis-ready arguments

- "Although Jacksonian democracy opened politics to nearly all white men, it was built on racial and gender exclusion, so democracy expanded in participation more than in membership."
- "The market revolution was the most important cause of change in Period 4, because it produced the middle class that drove reform, the wage labour that fed democratic politics, and the cotton economy that entrenched slavery."
- "The Democratic-Republicans largely abandoned strict construction in office: the Louisiana Purchase, the Embargo and the postwar American System show broad federal power used by the party of limited government."
- "Andrew Jackson strengthened the presidency and the Union while weakening federal economic power and Native sovereignty."
- "The Second Great Awakening, more than Revolutionary ideals or economic change, explains the timing and intensity of reform between 1820 and 1848."

## Traps the exam sets

- **Dates that sound alike.** The Tariff of 1816 is nationalist and protective; the Tariff of 1828 is the "Abominations" that provoked nullification; the Compromise Tariff of 1833 ended the crisis.
- **Two Marshall cases about the Cherokee.** Cherokee Nation v. Georgia (1831) called them domestic dependent nations that could not sue as foreign states; Worcester v. Georgia (1832) ruled Georgia's laws had no force in their territory.
- **Jackson's two faces.** He defended the Union against South Carolina but sided with Georgia against the Cherokee; he expanded the presidency but shrank federal economic power.
- **Abolition was a minority cause.** Before 1848 most northerners were not abolitionists; antislavery became a majority position only as opposition to slavery's expansion after the Mexican War.
- **Most white southerners held no one in slavery,** yet most defended it.
- **The War of 1812 changed no borders,** but it ended Native military resistance east of the Mississippi and the Federalist Party.
- **Most Americans still farmed in 1848,** even in the North; the market revolution changed what and for whom they farmed.

## The unit exam

Forty-five items: six source-based multiple-choice sets (the Missouri Compromise, the Monroe Doctrine, the Bank Veto, Tocqueville, Douglass, and an interpretation of the market revolution), single items across every topic and about a fifth review of Periods 1–3, select-all, true/false and fill-in items, three SAQs (comparing interpretations of Jacksonian democracy, sourcing Jackson's removal message, and a causation chart) and a seven-point DBQ: evaluate the extent to which American democracy expanded from 1820 to 1848. For the DBQ, plan before writing: sort the six documents into "expanded" and "limited," pick two to source with HIPP, and have one outside example ready (1840 turnout, Pennsylvania's 1838 constitution, the Trail of Tears or the Dorr Rebellion).
