# Unit 2 · Period 2: 1607–1754 · The night-before sheet

**Exam weight:** 6–8% of the AP exam · **CED topics:** 2.1 to 2.8 · **Themes:** WOR, MIG, GEO, WXT, SOC, PCE, ARC
**Built from:** the First Charter of Virginia (1606), the Mayflower Compact (1620), the Fundamental Orders of Connecticut (1639), the Maryland Toleration Act (1649), the Navigation Act (1660), Penn's Charter of Privileges (1701) and Equiano's *Narrative* (1789); weeks 3–5 of this course

## The ideas that generate every question

1. **Goals decide everything.** Spain wanted converts and labor, France wanted furs, the Dutch wanted trade, England wanted land. Goals set the number of colonists, and numbers set relations with American Indians: coercion and later accommodation (Spain), alliance and intermarriage (France), displacement and war (England).
2. **Environment plus motive makes a region.** New England (Puritan families, towns, mixed farming and trade), the Middle Colonies (diversity, grain), the Chesapeake (tobacco, servants then slaves), the Lower South and West Indies (rice, sugar, African majorities).
3. **Slavery was built, not inherited.** Planters chose enslaved Africans over servants from about 1680, and laws (1662, 1705) made slavery racial and hereditary. It was legal everywhere and tied every region to the Atlantic economy.
4. **Native nations shaped the outcome.** They resisted (Powhatan, Pequot, Metacom, Pueblo Revolt, Yamasee), accommodated, and played empires off against one another (Haudenosaunee after 1701).
5. **The empire was regulated on paper and loose in practice.** Mercantilism and the Navigation Acts set the rules; the Dominion of New England failed; salutary neglect let smuggling and strong colonial assemblies flourish.
6. **Colonists became more British and more diverse at once.** Anglicization, the Enlightenment and the Great Awakening created shared culture, while German, Scots-Irish and African peoples made the colonies unlike Britain.

## The Atlantic economy (2.4)

| Item | What to know |
|---|---|
| Mercantilism | Colonies supply raw materials and buy the mother country's manufactures |
| Navigation Acts (1651, 1660, 1663, 1696) | English/colonial ships and crews; enumerated goods only to England; European goods through England |
| Enumerated goods | Sugar, tobacco, cotton, indigo and more |
| Dominion of New England (1686–1689) | Andros; assemblies abolished; overthrown after the Glorious Revolution |
| Salutary neglect | Walpole era; lax enforcement; smuggling; strong assemblies |
| Molasses Act (1733) | Tax on foreign molasses; evaded |
| Wool (1699), Hat (1732), Iron (1750) Acts | Limits on colonial manufacturing |
| Triangular trade | A simplification of a web linking rum, enslaved people, sugar and molasses |
| Consumer revolution | British goods on credit; Anglicization |

Who gained: British manufacturers and merchants; New England shipbuilders (colonial ships counted as English). Who lost: tobacco planters (no Dutch buyers).

## Chains to know

**Causation (Chesapeake labor):** tobacco → labor demand → servants → freed servants want land → Bacon's Rebellion → fewer servants, more Africans after 1698, longer lives → slavery → slave codes.

**Causation (imperial control):** Navigation Acts → Dominion of New England → Glorious Revolution → Andros overthrown → salutary neglect → smuggling and strong assemblies → shock of enforcement after 1763 (Unit 3).

**Comparison (empires):** goal → number of colonists → relations with American Indians → type of society (mixed, allied or settler).

**Comparison (regions):** environment + migrants' motives + labor system → economy and society.

**Continuity and change:** continuity of Spanish labor demands from Period 1; change in that Spain accommodated after 1680; continuity of European rivalry; change in that English settlers came to dominate the coast.

## Thesis-ready arguments

- "Although all European colonizers shared religious and economic motives, their methods differed because Spain sought labor, France furs and England land, so English colonization was the most displacing."
- "The shift to slavery in the Chesapeake was driven by economic demand but decided by political choice, as planters responded to Bacon's Rebellion and a falling supply of servants."
- "By 1754 British colonists were both more British (in goods, politics and identity) and more diverse than Britain, a tension that shaped their later response to imperial reform."
- "Native nations shaped colonization as much as Europeans did: they forced Spanish accommodation in New Mexico and held the balance of power between Britain and France."

## Traps to avoid

- Pilgrims (Separatists, Plymouth 1620) are not Puritans (reformers, Massachusetts Bay 1630).
- Jamestown was a business, not a religious refuge.
- Slavery was legal in every colony, and northern merchants profited from it.
- The Great Awakening (emotion, faith) is not the Enlightenment (reason, science).
- Toleration in 1649 meant toleration of Christians only; Penn's 1701 charter went further.
- Native peoples were not passive: the Pueblos won concessions and the Haudenosaunee held the balance of power.

## The unit exam

Weeks 3 to 5, every topic 2.1 to 2.8, with about a fifth of the items reviewing Period 1; 45 items in about 75 minutes. Expect stimulus sets on a compact, a trade law and two historians' interpretations written for this course; select-all, true/false and fill-in-the-blank items; a short-answer question on a map of colonial regions; and a long essay on the causes of the growth of slavery. For the SAQ, answer each part in two or three sentences with a specific example. For the LEQ, weigh the causes: say which mattered most and why, then show how they interacted.
