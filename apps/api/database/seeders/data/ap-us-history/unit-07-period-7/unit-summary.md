# Unit 7 · Period 7: 1890–1945 · The night-before sheet

**Exam weight:** 10–17% of the AP exam · **CED topics:** 7.1 to 7.15 · **Themes:** WOR, PCE, NAT, SOC, MIG, WXT, ARC, GEO
**Built from:** Theodore Roosevelt's Annual Message (1904); the Anti-Imperialist League platform (1899); W. E. B. Du Bois, *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903); Woodrow Wilson's war message and Fourteen Points (1917–1918); *Schenck* (1919); Calvin Coolidge (1925); Langston Hughes (1926); Franklin Roosevelt's inaugurals, Four Freedoms and Executive Orders 8802 and 9066; *Korematsu* (1944); Harry Truman on Hiroshima (1945)

## The ideas that generate every question

1. **The United States became a world power, and argued about it every step of the way.** Expansionists against anti-imperialists (1898), Wilson against Lodge (1919), Roosevelt against America First (1941). The trend runs from regional power to global leadership, but the debate never stopped.
2. **The federal government grew in three surges: Progressive regulation, wartime mobilization and the New Deal.** Each surge built on the last (the War Industries Board was the NRA's model), and each provoked a reaction (normalcy in the 1920s; the Liberty League and the Court in the 1930s).
3. **War repeatedly narrowed civil liberties.** The Espionage and Sedition Acts and *Schenck* (1917–1919), the Red Scare (1919–1920), and Japanese American incarceration and *Korematsu* (1942–1944). Courts deferred to the government during wartime.
4. **Migration remade the nation, and so did the backlash against it.** New immigrants and the Great Migration filled cities; nativists answered with quotas (1924), the Klan and the Red Scare; wartime labor needs pulled Mexican workers in (1910s, Bracero Program) while the Depression pushed them out (repatriation).
5. **Modern mass culture and traditionalism collided.** Cars, radio, movies and credit in the 1920s against Prohibition, fundamentalism and immigration restriction.
6. **Reform always had limits of race and gender.** Progressives segregated federal offices, the New Deal excluded farm and domestic workers from Social Security, and the wartime military stayed segregated; African Americans, women and other groups organised to push past those limits.

## Chains to have ready

- **Causation, U.S. entry in 1917:** blockade → trade and loans to the Allies → U-boats → *Lusitania* → Sussex pledge → unrestricted submarine warfare + Zimmermann Telegram + Russian Revolution → war message.
- **Causation, the Depression:** 1920s inequality and debt + farm crisis + speculation → crash → bank failures (no insurance) → falling spending and money supply → Smoot–Hawley and world trade collapse → 25% unemployment.
- **Continuity and change, the federal role:** Progressive regulation → wartime coordination → 1920s retreat → New Deal provision and regulation → wartime command economy.
- **Continuity, nativism:** Chinese Exclusion (1882) → Red Scare (1919) → quotas and Asian exclusion (1924) → Mexican repatriation (1930s) → Japanese American incarceration (1942).
- **Comparison, the two peaces:** 1919 (Article X, Wilson refuses compromise, Senate rejects) vs 1945 (bipartisan planning, the veto, Senate approves 89–2).
- **Comparison, the two home fronts:** both expanded federal power and curtailed liberties; the Second was larger, more compulsory, and targeted a whole ethnic group.

## Thesis-ready arguments

- "Between 1898 and 1945 the United States moved from regional to global power, but the moments of greatest change (1898, 1917, 1941) each came through a crisis, and each was followed by a debate that limited how far the change went until the Second World War made it permanent."
- "The New Deal changed the federal government's relationship with ordinary Americans more than any earlier reform, turning Washington into a provider of jobs, security and labor rights, but it left racial and gender hierarchies largely intact."
- "The cultural conflicts of the 1920s were a reaction to modernity: as mass culture, cities and migration reshaped the nation, traditionalists used Prohibition, quotas and anti-evolution laws to defend an older idea of America."
- "Both world wars expanded federal power and narrowed civil liberties; what differed was the scale of mobilization and the nation's willingness to stay engaged after victory."

## The unit exam

Forty-five questions covering all fifteen topics, with about a fifth reviewing Periods 3 to 6. Six multiple-choice sets on sources (Beveridge, Du Bois, the Fourteen Points, Harding, the Kellogg–Briand Pact and the Platt Amendment), two SAQs (a secondary interpretation of Progressivism and the New Deal, and Executive Order 8802), and a seven-document DBQ on how far the New Deal changed the relationship between the federal government and the American people. For the DBQ, plan before writing: a thesis that weighs change against its limits, a context paragraph that begins before 1929, at least four documents used as evidence, sourcing for two (Hoover's 1928 audience, the unanimous Court in *Schechter*, how the unemployment series was defined), and one piece of evidence beyond the documents.
