## A changing economy

Three forces remade American work after 1980. **Technology** came first. Personal computers moved into offices in the 1980s; the Internet, which grew from a Defense Department research network, became a public and commercial medium in the 1990s. Automation also cut the number of workers needed in factories. **Globalization** came second. Container shipping, cheaper communication and lower trade barriers let companies make goods wherever costs were lowest. NAFTA (1994), negotiated under George H. W. Bush and passed under Bill Clinton with votes from both parties, and the World Trade Organization (1995) lowered barriers further. **The shift to services** followed from both: by the 1990s most Americans worked in health care, retail, finance, education and technology rather than in factories.

The effects were uneven. Consumers gained cheaper goods; new industries created wealth, especially on the coasts and in the Sun Belt, and the late-1990s boom helped produce federal budget surpluses from 1998 to 2001. But the Rust Belt lost unionized factory jobs that had carried workers into the middle class after 1945. Union membership fell from 20.1 percent of wage and salary workers in 1983 to 13.5 percent in 2000, and to 9 percent in the private sector. Income inequality widened. Economists and historians still argue about the weight of each cause: technology rewarding education, trade, the decline of unions, and tax and regulatory policy.

Supporters of free trade and deregulation argued that they made the economy more productive and lifted living standards here and abroad. Critics argued that the gains went to investors and highly educated workers while communities that depended on manufacturing paid the costs. Both sides cite real evidence.

The new economy also proved volatile. The dot-com bubble burst in 2000–2001. A housing bubble built on risky mortgage lending collapsed in 2007–2008, producing the Great Recession, the worst downturn since the 1930s, and a federal rescue of the banking system. Those events fall at the edge of the course's coverage; know them briefly as part of the continuing debate over government's role in the economy.

## Immigration and a changing population

The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 replaced the 1920s national-origins quotas with a system based on family ties and skills. Its sponsors did not expect large changes, but over the following decades immigration grew and its sources shifted. The Census Bureau counted 9.6 million foreign-born residents in 1970 (4.7 percent of the population) and 31.1 million in 2000 (11.1 percent); by 2009, over 80 percent of the foreign born had been born in Latin America or Asia, whereas in 1960 three-quarters had been born in Europe. By the early 2000s Latinos had become the nation's largest minority group. Internal migration also continued, as Americans moved toward the Sun Belt.

Policy swung between openness and enforcement. The Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986, signed by Reagan, combined sanctions on employers who knowingly hired unauthorized workers with legal status for about 2.7 million long-term residents. The Immigration Act of 1990 raised legal immigration. In 1994 California voters approved Proposition 187 to deny public services to undocumented immigrants, though courts blocked it, and the 1996 federal enforcement law expanded deportation and border enforcement.

The arguments recur. Those who favored restriction and enforcement stressed the rule of law, border security, the costs of public services and competition for low-wage jobs. Those who favored openness or legalization stressed immigrants' contributions to the economy, family unity, humanitarian concerns and the nation's history as a land of immigrants. Compare the Gilded Age: then, too, Americans debated assimilation, labor competition and restriction, though the newcomers came mainly from Southern and Eastern Europe and Asia.

## The post-Cold War world and September 11

With the Soviet Union gone, the United States was the sole superpower, but its purpose was less clear. When Iraq invaded Kuwait in 1990, George H. W. Bush spoke of "a new world order" of nations cooperating against aggression; a UN-authorized coalition expelled Iraq in early 1991. In the 1990s the U.S. intervened for humanitarian reasons in Somalia, Bosnia and Kosovo, did not intervene in the genocide in Rwanda, and debated when force was justified without a direct threat. At home, the Contract with America brought Republicans control of Congress in 1995, and Clinton, a New Democrat, signed welfare reform in 1996. The disputed election of 2000, decided after the Supreme Court halted the Florida recount in Bush v. Gore, showed how evenly divided the country had become.

On September 11, 2001, al-Qaeda terrorists hijacked four airliners and killed nearly 3,000 people. The government responded abroad and at home. U.S. forces invaded Afghanistan in October 2001 to destroy al-Qaeda's bases and overthrow the Taliban. In March 2003 the U.S. invaded Iraq, citing weapons of mass destruction that were not found; the war and occupation divided Americans. At home, the USA PATRIOT Act expanded surveillance, Congress created the Department of Homeland Security, and suspects were held at Guantánamo Bay. Supporters argued these steps prevented further attacks; critics argued they endangered civil liberties. The Supreme Court ruled in cases such as Hamdi v. Rumsfeld (2004) that detainees had some right to challenge their detention.

Other challenges carried into the new century: debates over climate change and energy (the Kyoto Protocol was signed but never sent to the Senate), an aging population that strained Social Security and Medicare, and continuing arguments over the role of government. Later events, such as the 2008 election of Barack Obama as the first African American president and the Affordable Care Act (2010), lie at the edge of the course's scope.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986, as codified at 8 U.S.C. § 1324a.**

> "It is unlawful for a person or other entity— (A) to hire, or to recruit or refer for a fee, for employment in the United States an alien knowing the alien is an unauthorized alien ..."

*Purpose:* to reduce unauthorized immigration by removing the job magnet. *Historical situation:* rising immigration after 1965 and a bipartisan compromise that paired this enforcement with legalization. Why it matters: the word "knowing" made the law hard to enforce, one reason the debate returned in the 1990s.

**2. Bill Clinton, State of the Union Address, January 23, 1996.**

> "The era of big government is over. But we cannot go back to the time when our citizens were left to fend for themselves."

*Point of view:* a centrist Democrat facing a Republican Congress after the 1994 elections and the budget shutdowns. Why it matters: it shows conservatism's influence on the center and the limits of that influence in one breath.

**3. George W. Bush, address to a joint session of Congress, September 20, 2001; and the 9/11 Commission Report, 2004.**

> "Every nation, in every region, now has a decision to make: Either you are with us, or you are with the terrorists."

> "The 9/11 attacks were a shock, but they should not have come as a surprise."

*Compare purposes:* the first rallies the nation and warns foreign governments nine days after the attacks; the second, from a bipartisan commission, evaluates failures after investigation and recommends reforms. Why it matters: reading them together shows how audience and purpose shape what each source stresses.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: reading a Census table.** Foreign born: 9.6 million (4.7%) in 1970; 31.1 million (11.1%) in 2000.

- *Trend:* the number more than tripled; the share more than doubled.
- *Trap:* the 2000 share was not a record; it was nearly 15 percent around 1890–1910. Distinguish numbers from shares.
- *Claim:* "Immigration after 1970 grew faster than the population and changed in origin, from European to mainly Latin American and Asian."

**Worked example 2: a causation chain.** Immigration and Nationality Act (1965) ends quotas → family-reunification chains grow from Latin America and Asia, alongside economic pull factors and conflict in Central America and Southeast Asia → foreign-born population triples by 2000 → enforcement and legalization debates: IRCA (1986), Proposition 187 (1994), the 1996 enforcement law. To earn complexity, add a qualifier: the 1965 law opened the door, but economic conditions on both sides of the border set how many came, and policy shortfalls (IRCA's weak enforcement) caused the next round of debate.

**Worked example 3: an SAQ answered part by part.** Source: union membership fell from 20.1 percent (1983) to 13.5 percent (2000), 9.0 percent in the private sector.

- (a) *Describe a pattern:* "Membership fell by about a third, and by 2000 unions were concentrated among government workers."
- (b) *Explain an economic cause:* "Deindustrialization: union jobs in steel and autos were lost to automation, imports and relocation, while new service jobs were rarely unionized."
- (c) *Explain a political cause:* "Reagan's firing of striking air traffic controllers in 1981 encouraged employers to replace strikers." Each answer names a specific development and states the link.

## Common misconceptions

- *"Globalization was caused by trade deals alone."* Technology and business decisions mattered as much as policy.
- *"Immigration in 2000 was at a record share."* The record share was around 1890–1910.
- *"NAFTA was a one-party policy."* It was negotiated by a Republican president and passed under a Democratic one with votes from both parties, and opposed by members of both.
- *"After 1991 the U.S. stopped using military force."* The 1990s saw the Gulf War and interventions in Somalia, Bosnia and Kosovo.
- *"The debate over security and liberty began after 9/11."* It echoes the Alien and Sedition Acts, the Espionage Act and Japanese American internment.
