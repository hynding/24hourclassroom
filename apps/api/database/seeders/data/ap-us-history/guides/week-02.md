## Why Europeans sailed (1.3)

The motives were the ones you met in week 1: **new sources of wealth** (spices, gold, trade routes), **the spread of Christianity**, and **military and economic advantage** for rival monarchs. What changed was capacity: caravels, compass and astrolabe, printed maps and news, and monarchies strong enough to finance voyages.

**Columbus** persuaded Ferdinand and Isabella to back a westward voyage to Asia. On **October 12, 1492**, he reached an island in the Bahamas and went on to Cuba and Hispaniola, where he met the Taíno. His letter of 1493, printed and translated within months, broadcast the news across Europe. Portugal, already sailing around Africa, protested, and the two crowns agreed in the **Treaty of Tordesillas (1494)** to a line 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands. When Cabral reached Brazil in 1500 it fell on Portugal's side. Other powers were not bound: **John Cabot** sailed for England in 1497, **Verrazzano** (1524) and **Jacques Cartier** (1534–1542) for France. Through the 1500s, though, only Spain built a large American empire; French and English colonies came after 1600.

## Conquest and the Columbian Exchange (1.4)

**The Caribbean first.** Hispaniola became Spain's base. The Taíno population collapsed within a generation from disease, forced labor, violence and hunger.

**Mexico.** In 1519 **Hernán Cortés** landed with about five hundred men. He won tens of thousands of Native allies, above all the **Tlaxcalans**, enemies of the Mexica, and relied on the interpreter **Malintzin**. The Mexica drove the Spaniards out of Tenochtitlan in 1520, but a **smallpox** epidemic then killed many in the city, including the emperor Cuitláhuac. After a long siege the city fell in August 1521.

**Peru.** Disease reached the Andes ahead of the Spanish, killing the Inca ruler and setting off a civil war between his sons. In 1532 **Francisco Pizarro** captured the victor, **Atahualpa**, at Cajamarca, took a vast ransom and executed him in 1533.

**North of Mexico.** Expeditions searched for another Mexico: Ponce de León (Florida, 1513), **Cabeza de Vaca** (Texas to Mexico, 1528–1536), **Hernando de Soto** (the Southeast, 1539–1542) and **Coronado** (the Southwest and Plains, 1540–1542). They found farming towns, not golden cities, and spread violence and disease. Spain settled **St. Augustine** (1565) to guard its treasure fleets, and **Juan de Oñate** took colonists into New Mexico in 1598; when **Acoma Pueblo** resisted, his forces destroyed it.

**The Columbian Exchange.** Contact joined two hemispheres that had been separated for thousands of years.

| From the Americas | From Europe, Africa and Asia |
|---|---|
| Maize, potatoes, sweet potatoes, tomatoes, beans, squash, chili peppers, cacao, peanuts, tobacco | Wheat, rice, sugarcane, bananas, coffee |
| Silver and gold | Horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, chickens |
| | Smallpox, measles, influenza; later malaria and yellow fever |

**In the Americas**, epidemics struck people with no acquired immunity; by most estimates many regions lost 80 to 90 percent or more of their population within a century, though exact figures are debated. Livestock trampled Native fields, and horses would later transform Plains societies. **In Europe, Africa and Asia**, American crops, especially maize and potatoes, later supported population growth. **In the world economy**, silver from **Potosí** (1545) and Zacatecas made Spain the richest power in Europe, contributed to inflation, financed wars and paid for trade with China through the Manila galleons. Historians tie it to Europe's shift toward commercial capitalism.

## Labor, slavery and caste (1.5)

Conquest created a labor problem: colonists wanted mines and estates worked while the Native population was falling. Spain's answers came in stages.

- **Encomienda.** The Crown granted colonists the labor and tribute of Native communities, in return for protection and Christian instruction. In practice it often meant forced labor and abuse.
- **Reform and its limits.** Dominican friars protested from 1511, and the **New Laws of 1542** banned Native slavery and limited encomiendas. Colonists resisted so fiercely that parts were relaxed in 1545; over time labor drafts such as the **repartimiento** and, in the Andes, the **mita**, replaced the encomienda while keeping the coercion.
- **African slavery.** As Native populations collapsed and the Crown restricted Native slavery, colonists turned to enslaved Africans, first to Hispaniola early in the 1500s and then to sugar plantations in the Caribbean and Brazil. This began the transatlantic slave trade that would carry millions across the ocean.
- **The casta system.** Spanish migrants were mostly men, and many formed unions with Native and African women. The Spanish recognised and ranked the resulting population: **peninsulares** (born in Spain) at the top, then **criollos** (Spanish ancestry, born in America), then **mestizos**, **mulattoes** and others of mixed descent, then Native people and enslaved Africans. Ancestry shaped taxes, offices and status.

## Encounters, arguments and adaptation (1.6)

**European justifications.** Europeans' belief in their cultural and religious superiority justified conquest. Spanish captains were required to read the **Requerimiento** (1513), a legal demand, read in Spanish, that Native people accept the Pope and the Spanish Crown or face war and enslavement (paraphrased here).

**European critics.** Spaniards argued with each other. **Bartolomé de las Casas** denounced the conquest, and at the **Valladolid debate (1550–1551)** he argued that Native people were rational human beings to be converted peacefully, while **Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda** argued, drawing on Aristotle's idea of natural slavery, that they could be justly conquered. No verdict was declared; the morality of empire was contested from the start. Las Casas's book was later used by Spain's Protestant rivals to brand Spain uniquely cruel, the so-called **Black Legend**.

**Native responses.** Native peoples resisted by force (Acoma, the Calusa of Florida, many peoples who fought de Soto), allied with Europeans against their own rivals (the Tlaxcalans), and adapted selectively, taking up metal tools, livestock and parts of Christianity on their own terms. **Syncretism** blended Catholic saints and festivals with older beliefs; devotion to the Virgin of Guadalupe, said by tradition to have appeared near Mexico City in 1531, became a shared symbol.

**African responses.** Enslaved Africans brought languages, religions, music and farming knowledge, and kept and blended them under slavery. Some escaped to form free communities in the hills and forests of the Caribbean and Mexico.

**England's first attempt.** Walter Raleigh's **Roanoke** colony (1585–1590) failed twice; the 1587 settlers had vanished by 1590. Harriot's report records both English curiosity and the epidemic that followed the English from town to town.

## Causation in Period 1 (1.7)

The period's causal chain, ready for any essay: **motives and means** → **voyages** → **Columbian Exchange** and **epidemics** → **conquest**, aided by Native allies → demand for labor → **encomienda**, then **African slavery** and the **casta** system → American **silver and sugar** → Spanish power, European inflation and commercial growth → **rivals** (England, France, the Netherlands) seeking their own colonies after 1600.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Columbus, letter on the first voyage (1493), translated by Cecil Jane (1930).**

> "They have no iron or steel or weapons, nor are they fitted to use them. This is not because they are not well built and of handsome stature, but because they are very marvellously timorous. It is true that, after they have been reassured and have lost this fear, they are so guileless and so generous with all that they possess, that no one would believe it who has not seen it."

*HIPP:* Written in 1493 to Luis de Santángel, a court official who had helped finance the voyage, and soon printed. To secure further backing it presents the Taíno as gentle, unarmed and easy to convert and govern. Later in the same letter Columbus promises the monarchs gold, spices and "slaves, as many as they shall order to be shipped". *Why it matters:* the image of a docile people became a justification for the encomienda.

**2. Treaty of Tordesillas (1494).**

> ". . . a boundary or straight line be determined and drawn north and south, from pole to pole, on the said ocean sea, from the Arctic to the Antarctic pole. This boundary or line shall be drawn straight, as aforesaid, at a distance of three hundred and seventy leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands . . ."

*HIPP:* Two Catholic crowns divide lands they had barely seen; no Native people were consulted. *Why it matters:* it explains Portuguese Brazil, and excluded powers became the rivals of Period 2.

**3. Bartolomé de las Casas, *A Brief Account of the Destruction of the Indies* (written 1542, published 1552; English translation 1689).**

> "The Spaniards first assaulted the innocent Sheep, so qualified by the Almighty, as is premention'd, like most cruel Tygers, Wolves and Lions hunger-starv'd, studying nothing, for the space of Forty Years, after their first landing, but the Massacre of these Wretches . . ."

*HIPP:* Las Casas had held an encomienda before becoming a Dominican friar; he wrote to persuade the Spanish Crown to protect Native people, and he succeeded in part (the New Laws). His purpose led him to dramatic imagery and large numbers that historians treat with caution. The 1689 English translation served a Protestant audience eager to condemn Spain, a reminder that sources are reused for new purposes.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: an SAQ answered part by part.** *(a) Briefly describe one cause of the decline of the Native population of the Caribbean after 1492. (b) Briefly explain one effect of that decline on the Spanish colonial labor system. (c) Briefly explain one way Spanish writers disagreed about the treatment of Native people.*
- (a) "Smallpox and other European diseases, to which Native people had no acquired immunity, killed large numbers, and forced labor under the encomienda made communities more vulnerable."
- (b) "As Native workers died, Spanish colonists began importing enslaved Africans to work Caribbean sugar plantations and mines, starting the transatlantic slave trade to the Americas."
- (c) "At the Valladolid debate of 1550–1551, Las Casas argued that Native people were rational and should be converted peacefully, while Sepúlveda argued that they were natural inferiors who could be conquered."

**Worked example 2: a causation chain on silver.** Potosí silver (1545) → Spain pays for armies and imports → silver floods Europe and contributes to inflation → rival merchants grow rich while Spain's manufacturing lags → England and the Netherlands seek colonies of their own. Each arrow is a "because" you must be able to explain.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A handful of Spaniards defeated the Aztecs with guns."** Native allies and smallpox were decisive.
- **"Native people simply died out from disease."** Disease worked together with war, forced labor and famine, and Native peoples survived, resisted and adapted.
- **"The Spanish all agreed about conquest."** Las Casas, Dominican reformers and the New Laws show deep disagreement.
