## A nation on the move: the context of Period 5

Period 5 runs from 1844, when an election turned on expansion, to 1877, when the last federal troops stopped protecting Reconstruction governments. The College Board frames it as a story of growth that sharpened division. Three forces were at work at once.

First, **territorial growth**. In four years (1845–1848) the United States added Texas, the Oregon Country south of the 49th parallel and the Mexican Cession, about 1.2 million square miles. Every new acre raised the question the Missouri Compromise had answered only for the Louisiana Purchase: would slavery be allowed there?

Second, **migration from abroad**. The Irish potato famine sent more than a million people across the Atlantic between 1845 and the mid-1850s, mostly poor Catholics who crowded into Boston, New York and Philadelphia and did the heaviest unskilled work. German immigrants, many with savings and skills and some fleeing the failed revolutions of 1848, bought farms in the Midwest or settled in Milwaukee, Cincinnati and St. Louis. In California, Chinese miners arrived after 1849. Native-born Protestants reacted with **nativism**: anti-Catholic riots in the 1840s and, by 1854, the American or "Know-Nothing" Party, which wanted a 21-year wait for citizenship.

Third, **an economy pulling in two directions**: Northern free labor and Southern cotton, which needed fresh land. Each section wanted the West shaped in its own image.

## Manifest destiny and the move west

In 1845 John L. O'Sullivan, a Democratic editor, wrote that it was the nation's "manifest destiny" to overspread the continent (paraphrased here; his essays appeared in the *United States Magazine and Democratic Review*). The phrase bundled several beliefs: that Providence favoured the United States, that its republican institutions should spread, and that Anglo-Americans were superior to Native peoples and Mexicans, who would give way.

Ideas alone did not move families 2,000 miles. **Overland migrants**, mostly Midwestern farm families, took the Oregon Trail from the early 1840s, drawn by fertile land and pushed by the hard times after the Panic of 1837. The **Mormons** went west for a different reason: after mob violence and the murder of Joseph Smith in Illinois in 1844, Brigham Young led the Latter-day Saints to the Great Salt Lake in 1847 to build a community where they could practise their faith. **Gold** at Sutter's Mill in January 1848 brought the largest rush of all; California's population exploded in 1849, and the new Californians wrote a constitution banning slavery and asked to enter the Union at once.

Polk, elected in 1844 over Henry Clay on an openly expansionist platform, pursued Oregon and California together. With Britain he talked tough ("Fifty-four forty or fight!" was a campaign cry, not his policy) and then compromised at the **49th parallel** in 1846, because by then he was at war with Mexico.

**Who paid the cost.** In California, miners and militias drove Native people from their land and killed thousands; scholarly estimates put the Native population at roughly 150,000 in 1848 and about 30,000 by 1870. On the Plains, the Treaty of Fort Laramie (1851) began a pattern of treaties made and broken that Unit 6 picks up.

## The Mexican–American War

**Causes.** Mexico never recognised Texan independence (1836), and when the United States annexed Texas in 1845 Mexico broke off relations. The two countries also disagreed about where Texas ended: Mexico said the **Nueces River**; Texas and the United States said the **Rio Grande**, about 150 miles further. Polk sent John Slidell to Mexico City to settle the border and buy California and New Mexico; the Mexican government, which could not survive selling national territory, refused to receive him. Polk then ordered General Zachary Taylor to the Rio Grande. In April 1846 Mexican cavalry attacked a U.S. patrol in the disputed strip, and Polk, who had already been drafting a war message, told Congress that Mexico "has invaded our territory and shed American blood upon the American soil."

**The war.** Congress voted for war by large margins in May 1846. While Taylor fought in northern Mexico and U.S. forces seized New Mexico and California, Winfield Scott landed at Veracruz in 1847 and fought his way to Mexico City, which fell in September.

**Dissent.** The war was popular in the South and West and contested in the North. Whigs charged that Polk had provoked it; a young Whig congressman, Abraham Lincoln, introduced the **Spot Resolutions** demanding the exact spot where blood had been shed. Abolitionists saw a plot to add slave states. Henry David Thoreau went to jail rather than pay a tax to support the war and slavery, and wrote the essay later called "Civil Disobedience."

**The peace.** The **Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo** (1848) fixed the Rio Grande border, transferred the Mexican Cession for $15 million plus the assumption of American citizens' claims against Mexico, and promised that Mexicans in the ceded land could stay, keep their property and become citizens. The promise was not kept in practice: the California Land Act of 1851 forced grant holders to prove their titles in years of costly litigation, and in Texas violence and fraud did the same work.

## Why the war reopened the slavery question

Within three months of the war message, Pennsylvania Democrat David Wilmot moved that slavery be barred from any land taken from Mexico. The **Wilmot Proviso** passed the House, where the free states had a majority, and failed in the Senate, where the sections were equal. Northern Democrats and Whigs voted for it, Southern Democrats and Whigs against: section was beginning to count for more than party.

Four positions now competed:

1. **Free soil** (the Proviso): Congress must bar slavery from the territories; the Free-Soil Party ran Martin Van Buren on it in 1848.
2. **Extend the Missouri line** to the Pacific at 36°30'.
3. **Popular sovereignty** (Lewis Cass, 1848): let the settlers decide.
4. **Calhoun's position**: the territories belong to all the states, so Congress cannot bar slaveholders from them.

Next week these four positions collide in the Compromise of 1850.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Polk's war message, May 11, 1846.**

> "But now, after reiterated menaces, Mexico has passed the boundary of the United States, has invaded our territory and shed American blood upon the American soil. She has proclaimed that hostilities have commenced, and that the two nations are now at war."

*HIPP.* Historical situation: the boundary was disputed, and Polk had moved the army into the strip Mexico claimed. Intended audience: Congress and the public. Purpose: to get a declaration and money quickly by presenting war as a fact Mexico had created. Point of view: an expansionist Democrat who wanted California. Why it matters: the phrase "the boundary of the United States" assumes the very thing in dispute.

**2. Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, Article VIII (1848).**

> "Mexicans now established in territories previously belonging to Mexico, and which remain for the future within the limits of the United States ... shall be free to continue where they now reside ... retaining the property which they possess in the said territories ..."

*HIPP.* A treaty is a negotiated legal promise; its audience is two governments and the people living in the ceded land. Its purpose here was to protect Mexican residents. Why it matters: comparing the promise with what happened (land lost through courts and force) is a classic continuity-and-change question.

**3. Henry David Thoreau, "Resistance to Civil Government" (1849).**

> "Witness the present Mexican war, the work of comparatively a few individuals using the standing government as their tool; for, in the outset, the people would not have consented to this measure."

*HIPP.* A Massachusetts Transcendentalist justifying, to a reform-minded audience, refusal to obey unjust laws. Why it matters: it links Period 4 reform (week 13) to dissent over the war.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: a contextualization paragraph.** Prompt: *Evaluate the extent to which the Mexican–American War changed American politics.* A strong context paragraph sets the scene before 1846 and connects it to the argument:

> In the decades before 1846, the market revolution tied the Northwest to the Northeast, while the cotton boom pushed slavery into the Gulf states and Texas. The Missouri Compromise of 1820 had settled the status of slavery only in the Louisiana Purchase, and the second party system of Whigs and Democrats deliberately kept slavery out of national debate so that both could win votes in both sections. Expansion into land outside the Missouri line threatened that arrangement, which is why the war's political effects were so large.

**Worked example 2: sourcing Polk's message.** Question: *How does Polk's purpose shape the message?* Step 1, name the purpose: to secure a declaration of war and funds. Step 2, find the language that serves it: "war exists," "by the act of Mexico herself," "American soil." Step 3, explain the effect: if war already exists, Congress is not choosing war but defending troops already under fire, so opposition looks unpatriotic.

**Worked example 3: a causation chain.** From the treaty to the crisis of 1850:

1. 1848: the treaty adds the Mexican Cession with no rule on slavery.
2. Because the Missouri line does not apply, the four rival positions (Proviso, 36°30', popular sovereignty, Calhoun) all claim the land.
3. Because of the Gold Rush, California fills up and asks to enter as a free state in 1849, skipping the territorial stage.
4. Because the Senate was balanced 15–15, admitting California would give the free states a majority.
5. Therefore Southern leaders threatened secession, and Congress had to find a new compromise in 1850.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The war was universally popular."** Whigs, abolitionists and many New Englanders opposed it.
- **"The Wilmot Proviso became law."** It never passed the Senate. Its importance was the sectional voting pattern.
- **"Free-Soilers were abolitionists."** Most wanted to keep slavery, and often Black people, out of the West to protect white labor; they did not propose ending slavery in the South.
- **"Mexicans in the Cession were immigrants."** They did not cross the border; the border crossed them, and the treaty made them eligible for citizenship.
