## The long boom

Between 1945 and 1973 the American economy grew almost without interruption, and median family income roughly doubled. Five causes stand out:

1. **Pent-up demand.** Americans had saved during the war, when consumer goods were rationed. After 1945 they spent on cars, houses and appliances.
2. **Federal policy.** The GI Bill sent millions of veterans to college and helped them buy homes. FHA and VA mortgage insurance made thirty-year, low-down-payment loans standard. The 1956 Federal-Aid Highway Act built 41,000 miles of interstate.
3. **Cold War spending.** Defense budgets stayed high after Korea. Aerospace, electronics and research flowed especially to California, Texas, Florida and Georgia: a "military Keynesianism" no one planned as such.
4. **Population.** The baby boom created demand for homes, schools and goods.
5. **Global position.** With Europe and Japan rebuilding, American firms faced little competition, and oil was cheap.

**Where people lived.** Suburbs absorbed most of the growth. William Levitt's assembly-line houses on Long Island sold for under $8,000 and came with federally backed mortgages. Home ownership rose from about 44% of households in 1940 to about 62% in 1960. But the same federal underwriting rules marked Black neighbourhoods as risky, and Levittown's deeds barred Black buyers. Highways and urban renewal often cut through Black neighbourhoods. The postwar metropolis was therefore both more prosperous and more segregated.

**The Sun Belt.** Defense plants, military bases, air conditioning and cheap land drew people and firms south and west. Right-to-work laws, made possible by the **Taft-Hartley Act** (1947), kept unions weak there. Over the next decades congressional seats, and political power, followed the population.

**Labour's bargain.** After the great strike wave of 1946, unions settled into a bargain with large firms. The 1950 contract between the United Auto Workers and General Motors traded labour peace for wages tied to productivity, pensions and health insurance. Union density peaked at about a third of nonfarm workers in the mid-1950s, and the AFL and CIO merged in 1955. Unionised workers joined the middle class, but benefits tied to employers replaced the push for national health insurance that Truman's Fair Deal had failed to win.

**Who was left out.** Rural Southerners, Appalachia, inner-city residents, many African Americans and Mexican Americans, and American Indians moved by federal relocation programs to cities all saw little of the boom. Mexican farm workers came under the Bracero Program while a 1954 federal campaign deported large numbers of undocumented Mexicans. The writer Michael Harrington's 1962 study of an invisible poor would later shock readers who assumed poverty had vanished.

**Politics of consensus.** Eisenhower called his approach Modern Republicanism. He kept and expanded the New Deal (Social Security, the minimum wage, a new Department of Health, Education and Welfare) while trying to balance budgets. Historians call this broad agreement on a mixed economy and anti-communism the **liberal consensus**.

## Culture after 1945

**Mass culture.** Television spread from a small minority of homes in 1950 to most homes by 1960. It created a shared national culture, sold consumer goods and changed politics. Advertising, credit cards and shopping centres fed a consumer culture that many presented as proof of capitalism's superiority over communism.

**Family and gender.** Americans married younger and had more children. Magazines and television celebrated the breadwinner father and the full-time housewife. Yet the share of married women in paid work rose throughout the decade, in offices, shops and schools. That gap between ideal and reality became a central grievance of 1960s feminism.

**Religion.** Church membership grew. Billy Graham's crusades filled stadiums. Congress added "under God" to the Pledge of Allegiance in 1954 and adopted "In God We Trust" as the national motto in 1956, defining America against "godless" communism.

**Youth.** Teenagers had money to spend. Rock and roll, rooted in Black rhythm and blues and carried by stars such as Elvis Presley and Chuck Berry, alarmed adults who linked it to delinquency and racial mixing.

**Critics.** Sociologists warned that corporate and suburban life produced conformity; economists criticised private affluence alongside public neglect; Beat writers rejected materialism outright. Few in number, they planted the ideas of the 1960s.

## Early civil rights, 1940s–1950s

**Wartime roots.** World War II's fight against a racist enemy, the Double V campaign, Executive Order 8802 and the Great Migration all built Black political power. Black veterans came home determined not to accept Jim Crow, and some were attacked for it.

**Truman's executive action.** In 1946 Truman appointed a President's Committee on Civil Rights, whose 1947 report, *To Secure These Rights*, called for federal action against lynching, disenfranchisement and segregation. Congress, where Southern Democrats chaired key committees, refused. Truman then used executive power: **Executive Order 9981** (1948) ordered equal treatment in the armed forces, and integration was largely complete during the Korean War. His party's 1948 civil rights plank provoked the **Dixiecrat** walkout and Strom Thurmond's States' Rights candidacy, the first crack in the Solid South.

**Breaking barriers.** Jackie Robinson integrated Major League Baseball in 1947, a public symbol of change.

**The legal campaign.** The NAACP Legal Defense Fund, under Charles Hamilton Houston and Thurgood Marshall, attacked "separate but equal" first in graduate schools, then in public schools. In **Brown v. Board of Education** (1954) a unanimous Court under Earl Warren held that segregated schools were inherently unequal. A year later, Brown II ordered desegregation without a deadline. Mexican American litigants won parallel victories: Mendez v. Westminster (1947) in California and Hernandez v. Texas (1954), which extended equal protection to jury selection.

**Massive resistance.** In 1956, 101 Southern members of Congress signed the **Southern Manifesto** against Brown. States passed pupil-placement laws; Virginia closed schools rather than integrate. In 1957 Arkansas Governor Orval Faubus used the National Guard to block nine Black students from Little Rock's Central High School. Eisenhower, no enthusiast for Brown, sent the 101st Airborne to enforce the courts' authority.

**Grassroots action.** The 1955 murder of Emmett Till in Mississippi, and his mother's insistence on an open casket, outraged Black America. Months later Rosa Parks, an experienced NAACP activist, was arrested in Montgomery. The Women's Political Council and local ministers organised a boycott led by the young Martin Luther King Jr.; it lasted 381 days until the Supreme Court struck down bus segregation in Browder v. Gayle (1956). King and other ministers founded the **SCLC** in 1957. Congress passed the **Civil Rights Act of 1957**, the first since Reconstruction, though its voting provisions were weak.

**Perspectives.** Many white Southerners framed resistance as defence of states' rights and local control; Northern liberals were often cautious; Cold War leaders worried about America's image abroad; Black activists disagreed among themselves over litigation versus direct action. By 1960 the law had changed far faster than practice.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Executive Order 9981, July 26, 1948**

> "It is hereby declared to be the policy of the President that there shall be equality of treatment and opportunity for all persons in the armed services without regard to race, color, religion or national origin."

*HIPP:* Issued months before a close election, after A. Philip Randolph threatened a campaign of draft resistance and with Congress unwilling to act. *Why it matters:* executive action could move where legislation could not.

**2. Brown v. Board of Education, May 17, 1954**

> "We conclude that, in the field of public education, the doctrine of "separate but equal" has no place. Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal."

*HIPP:* Chief Justice Warren worked to make the decision unanimous so that it would carry maximum authority in the South. *Why it matters:* it destroyed the legal basis of Jim Crow but left enforcement to others.

**3. The Southern Manifesto, March 12, 1956**

> "We regard the decisions of the Supreme Court in the school cases as a clear abuse of judicial power."

*HIPP:* Signed by 101 Southern members of Congress, many facing segregationist voters; the purpose was to legitimise resistance through constitutional language. *Why it matters:* it shows massive resistance as a respectable political strategy, not only mob action.

**4. Dwight D. Eisenhower, address on Little Rock, September 24, 1957**

> "Mob rule cannot be allowed to override the decisions of our courts."

*HIPP:* Spoken to a national audience including white Southerners; Eisenhower stressed law and Cold War prestige rather than racial justice. *Why it matters:* the first use of federal troops to protect Black citizens' rights since Reconstruction.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: reading a data table.** Births were about 2.6 million in 1940, 3.4 million in 1946 and 4.3 million in 1957, then fell to 3.1 million by 1975. A strong answer names the pattern (a rise of two thirds in seventeen years, then a decline), explains a cause (postwar prosperity, earlier marriage, suburban housing after Depression and war), and links an effect (school building in the 1950s, a huge youth generation in the 1960s).

**Worked example 2: an SAQ part.** "Briefly explain ONE limitation of Brown v. Board."

> Brown declared school segregation unconstitutional, but the Court's 1955 implementation order set no deadline, and Southern states used massive resistance (pupil-placement laws, school closures and the defiance at Little Rock) so that very few Black children in the Deep South attended integrated schools until after the Civil Rights Act of 1964 tied federal money to desegregation.

**Worked example 3: from prompt to thesis.** Prompt: "Evaluate the extent to which postwar prosperity was shared by all Americans, 1945–1960."

- Weak: "Some Americans benefited and some did not." (no line of reasoning)
- Strong: "Postwar prosperity reached a broad majority through rising wages, the GI Bill and home ownership, but because federal housing and veterans' policies were administered in discriminatory ways, its benefits flowed disproportionately to white suburban families, leaving many African Americans, Mexican Americans and the rural poor outside the new middle class."

## Common misconceptions

- **"Brown desegregated Southern schools."** It changed the law; real desegregation came after 1964.
- **"Rosa Parks was simply tired."** She was a trained activist, and the boycott was organised by an existing network.
- **"Women left the workforce in the 1950s."** Many were pushed out of industry in 1945–1946, but women's overall employment rose during the decade.
- **"Eisenhower dismantled the New Deal."** He expanded Social Security and built the interstates.
