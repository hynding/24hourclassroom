## Between the wars (7.11)

The 1920s were never truly isolationist. The United States hosted the **Washington Naval Conference** (1921–1922), which limited battleships; arranged the **Dawes Plan** (1924) to ease German reparations with American loans; and signed the **Kellogg–Briand Pact** (1928), in which the signers declared that they "condemn recourse to war for the solution of international controversies, and renounce it, as an instrument of national policy." What Americans refused was binding commitment: no League, no alliances, no guarantees.

The Depression and the rise of aggressive regimes tested that stance. When Japan seized Manchuria (1931), the **Stimson Doctrine** refused to recognise the conquest but imposed no sanctions. In Latin America, Roosevelt's **Good Neighbor policy** renounced armed intervention, withdrew the Marines from Haiti and abrogated the Platt Amendment (1934). Meanwhile the **Nye Committee** (1934–1936) suggested that bankers and arms makers had dragged the nation into the First World War, and Congress passed **Neutrality Acts** (1935–1937) that banned arms sales and loans to all nations at war and kept Americans off belligerents' ships.

As Italy conquered Ethiopia, Germany rearmed and Japan invaded China (1937), Roosevelt tested opinion with his **Quarantine speech**: "War is a contagion, whether it be declared or undeclared." The backlash showed how strong isolationist feeling remained.

## From neutrality to war (7.11)

When Germany invaded Poland in September 1939, Roosevelt won **cash-and-carry**, which favoured Britain and France because they controlled the sea. After France fell in June 1940, he traded fifty old destroyers for British bases, Congress passed the first peacetime **draft**, and Roosevelt won an unprecedented third term. In January 1941 he described a future founded on **Four Freedoms** (of speech and of worship, from want and from fear) and asked for **Lend-Lease**, which made the United States "the arsenal of democracy." In August he and Churchill issued the **Atlantic Charter**, and U.S. warships were soon escorting convoys and exchanging fire with U-boats. The **America First Committee**, with Charles Lindbergh as its star, fought each step.

War came from the Pacific. Japan's push into French Indochina led the United States to freeze Japanese assets and cut off oil in the summer of 1941. Facing the loss of the fuel its navy needed, Japan decided to seize the oil and rubber of Southeast Asia and to cripple the U.S. Pacific Fleet first. On **December 7, 1941**, its carriers struck **Pearl Harbor**. Congress declared war on Japan; Germany and Italy declared war on the United States.

## The home front (7.12)

**The economy.** The **War Production Board** converted factories to tanks, planes and ships; output roughly doubled; unemployment, still about 15 percent in 1940, fell below 2 percent by 1944. The **Office of Price Administration** set price ceilings and issued ration books. The federal budget, the national debt and the income tax (now withheld from paychecks and paid by most workers) all grew enormously. Huge cost-plus contracts bound large corporations to the government.

**Women.** Millions of women took jobs in shipyards, aircraft plants and offices, symbolised by "Rosie the Riveter", and some 350,000 served in uniform. Most were pushed out of industrial work when the war ended, but the experience changed expectations.

**African Americans.** Facing exclusion from defense jobs, **A. Philip Randolph** threatened a march on Washington; Roosevelt answered with **Executive Order 8802** (1941), banning "discrimination in the employment of workers in defense industries or government because of race, creed, color, or national origin," and the Fair Employment Practices Committee. About a million African Americans served, in segregated units such as the **Tuskegee Airmen**. The *Pittsburgh Courier*'s **Double V** campaign demanded victory over racism at home; NAACP membership grew several-fold. A second Great Migration took Black workers to the North and West, and racial violence followed (Detroit, 1943).

**Mexican Americans.** The **Bracero Program** (1942) brought Mexican contract workers to farms and railroads. In Los Angeles, servicemen attacked young Mexican Americans in the **Zoot Suit Riots** (1943). Hundreds of thousands of Mexican Americans served in the armed forces.

**American Indians.** More than 40,000 served, including the Navajo **code talkers**; many others left reservations for war work.

**Japanese Americans.** **Executive Order 9066** (February 1942) named no group, but it was used to remove about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry, roughly two-thirds of them citizens, from the West Coast to inland camps. In *Korematsu v. United States* (1944) the Court upheld exclusion; Justice Murphy, dissenting, said it fell "into the ugly abyss of racism." Thousands of Japanese American men nonetheless served, many in the much-decorated 442nd Regimental Combat Team. In 1988 Congress apologised and paid reparations to survivors.

## Winning the war (7.13)

The Allies agreed to defeat **Germany first**. Americans fought in North Africa (1942), Sicily and Italy (1943), and landed in Normandy on **D-Day** (June 6, 1944), while the Soviet Union, which bore the heaviest losses, drove from the east. Strategic bombing devastated German cities. Germany surrendered on May 8, 1945. As Allied troops reached the camps, the full scale of the **Holocaust**, the murder of six million Jews and millions of others, became undeniable; the United States had admitted few refugees in the 1930s and created a War Refugee Board only in 1944.

In the Pacific, the U.S. Navy broke Japan's offensive at **Midway** (June 1942), then **island-hopped** toward Japan (Guadalcanal, the Marianas, Iwo Jima, Okinawa), seizing bases for bombers. Fire-bombing destroyed much of Tokyo in March 1945. The **Manhattan Project** produced atomic bombs; Truman, president after Roosevelt's death in April, approved their use on **Hiroshima** (August 6) and **Nagasaki** (August 9). The Soviet Union declared war on Japan on August 8, and Japan announced its surrender on August 15. Historians continue to debate the decision: whether the bomb was needed to avoid a costly invasion, whether clearer terms for the emperor might have ended the war, and how far concern about the Soviet Union shaped it.

## Planning the peace (7.14)

The **Atlantic Charter** set principles; conferences turned them into plans. At **Tehran** (1943) the Big Three agreed on the Normandy invasion. At **Yalta** (February 1945) they agreed on occupation zones in Germany, Soviet entry into the Pacific war, and a United Nations; Stalin promised free elections in Eastern Europe, a promise soon broken. At **Potsdam** (July 1945), Truman, now aware of the bomb's success, met a Stalin whose armies held Eastern Europe. The **United Nations** was founded at San Francisco in 1945. Roosevelt, determined not to repeat Wilson's mistakes, had involved leading Republicans, and the Security Council veto protected U.S. sovereignty; the Senate approved the Charter 89–2.

## Comparison in Period 7 (7.15)

Use the two wars to practise comparison. **Similar:** both began with U.S. neutrality and aid to Britain; both multiplied federal economic power (War Industries Board, War Production Board); both used propaganda and restricted civil liberties (*Schenck*, *Korematsu*); both drew Black and Mexican migrants to new jobs and sharpened demands for equality. **Different:** the Second World War began with a direct attack rather than submarine warfare; mobilization was larger and more compulsory; repression targeted a whole ethnic group; and the peace ended not in retreat but in permanent engagement.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Franklin D. Roosevelt, Quarantine speech, October 5, 1937**

> "When an epidemic of physical disease starts to spread, the community approves and joins in a quarantine of the patients in order to protect the health of the community against the spread of the disease."

*HIPP.* Delivered in Chicago, heartland of isolationism, three months after Japan invaded China. Purpose: to test whether Americans would accept collective action against aggressors. *Why it matters:* the hostile reaction shows the limits Roosevelt worked within until 1941.

**2. Executive Order 9066, February 19, 1942**

> "... to prescribe military areas in such places and of such extent as he or the appropriate Military Commander may determine, from which any or all persons may be excluded ..."

*HIPP.* Signed ten weeks after Pearl Harbor amid fear of invasion and pressure from West Coast politicians and interest groups. *Why it matters:* neutral language, targeted use; read it with *Korematsu* and the 1988 apology.

**3. Harry S. Truman, statement on Hiroshima, August 6, 1945**

> "It is an atomic bomb. It is a harnessing of the basic power of the universe. The force from which the sun draws its power has been loosed against those who brought war to the Far East."

*HIPP.* Issued to the American public and the world, and aimed at Japan's leaders, warning of "a rain of ruin from the air." *Why it matters:* the opening of the atomic age, and a source for the debate over the decision.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: a comparison paragraph.** *Compare the peace settlements after the First and Second World Wars.* "Both settlements aimed to create an international organization to keep the peace, and both reflected Wilsonian principles of self-determination. They differed in outcome: in 1919–1920 the Senate rejected the Treaty of Versailles because of fears that Article X would commit the nation to war, whereas in 1945 the Senate approved the UN Charter 89–2. The difference came from Roosevelt's bipartisan planning, the Security Council veto, and the experience of a second, larger war that convinced most Americans that isolation had failed."

**Worked example 2: an SAQ on incarceration.** *Briefly explain ONE reason for the incarceration of Japanese Americans.* "Decades of anti-Asian prejudice on the West Coast, visible in the Chinese Exclusion Act and the 1924 exclusion of Japanese immigrants, combined with fear after Pearl Harbor, led military and political leaders to treat all people of Japanese ancestry as potential enemies, even though no evidence of sabotage existed."

**Worked example 3: a thesis on the bomb.** Prompt: *Evaluate the relative importance of the reasons for using the atomic bomb.* Thesis: "The most important reason for using the atomic bomb was the desire to end the war quickly and avoid an invasion that planners expected to cost very heavily after Iwo Jima and Okinawa, but the decision was also shaped by the momentum of a $2 billion project, the logic of a war that had already made cities targets, and the hope of strengthening the U.S. position against the Soviet Union."

## Common misconceptions

- **"The United States was neutral until Pearl Harbor."** By late 1941 it was supplying Britain and the Soviet Union and its navy was fighting U-boats in the Atlantic.
- **"Executive Order 9066 named Japanese Americans."** It named no group; it was applied almost entirely to them.
- **"The United States defeated Germany largely alone."** The Soviet Union did most of the land fighting against Germany; Britain and the Commonwealth fought from 1939.
