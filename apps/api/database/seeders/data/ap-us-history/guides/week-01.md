## What you need to be able to do

- **1.1** Explain the context in which Europeans began to explore and colonize the Americas: the state of Native societies, of Western Europe, and of West Africa around 1491.
- **1.2** Explain how and why different Native American societies developed before European contact, above all how the spread of maize and the demands of different environments produced very different economies, settlement patterns and social structures.

In plain language: be able to describe North America in 1491 region by region, explain *why* each region's peoples lived as they did, and explain what was happening in Europe and Africa that would soon bring the three continents together.

## North America in 1491: one continent, many worlds

Start with scale. Native peoples had lived in the Americas for many thousands of years, spreading from the far north to the tip of South America. By 1491 the land north of Mexico held several million people, possibly many more (estimates vary widely because they are reconstructed from archaeology and later records), who spoke hundreds of languages. To the south, the Mexica (Aztec) capital of Tenochtitlan was one of the largest cities in the world, and the Inca ruled an Andean empire thousands of miles long. Nothing about this was a "new" world except to Europeans.

The second thing is diversity. The CED's central idea is that peoples developed very different societies by **adapting to and transforming** their environments. Two forces did most of the shaping: the resources each environment offered, and the spread of maize.

**Maize and the spread of farming.** Maize was domesticated in Mexico and moved north and east over centuries, carried by trade and migration and bred into local varieties. Where it took hold it changed everything: a storable, high-yield crop allowed larger populations, permanent villages, specialised leaders and priests, and the labor to reshape land. In the arid **Southwest**, Ancestral Puebloans at Chaco Canyon built great houses of stone and a network of roads, and caught rain with ditches, dams and terraces; their descendants, the Pueblo peoples, lived in multistorey towns surrounded by irrigated fields of maize, beans, squash and cotton. In the **Mississippi Valley**, fertile floodplains fed **Cahokia**, a city of perhaps 10,000 to 20,000 at its height, with a central mound larger at its base than the Great Pyramid of Giza. Mississippian chiefdoms collected tribute, traded copper and shells over long distances and built mounds across the Southeast. Many had declined before 1492: Native history had its own rises and falls.

**Mobile peoples of the dry interior.** In the **Great Basin** and on the **Great Plains**, scarce and unpredictable rain made large-scale farming hard. Most peoples lived in small bands that moved with the seasons, gathering seeds and roots and hunting. Plains hunters pursued bison on foot, driving herds into corrals or over cliffs, with dogs pulling loads on travois. The horse, and the mounted Plains cultures of popular imagination, came later with the Spanish. Some Plains peoples farmed river valleys and lived in earth lodges: environment set possibilities, not rules.

**Mixed economies of the East.** In the **Northeast** and along the **Atlantic seaboard**, peoples combined farming with hunting, fishing and gathering. Women usually worked the fields of maize, beans and squash (the Three Sisters), and men hunted, fished and cleared land. Villages were permanent but moved every decade or so as soils wore out and firewood ran short. Thomas Harriot's 1588 description of Carolina Algonquian farming, quoted below, shows this system precisely.

**Settled peoples without farming.** On the **Pacific Northwest** coast, salmon runs, shellfish and great cedar forests were rich enough that peoples such as the Chinook lived in large permanent villages of plank houses without growing crops. Wealth was displayed and redistributed in feasts such as the potlatch. **California** held dozens of distinct peoples who lived by fishing, hunting and gathering, above all acorns, which they leached and stored; it was one of the most densely settled regions north of Mexico. These cases break the common assumption that only farming supports permanence.

## Social and political structures

Economies shaped social structures (SOC), but did not dictate them. Mississippian societies were **ranked chiefdoms** with hereditary elites. The **Haudenosaunee** of present-day New York lived in **longhouses** shared by related families of a clan traced through the mother's line; clan mothers chose and could remove the men who served as chiefs. Five nations (Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca) formed a league to keep peace among themselves and to decide common matters in council; historians date its founding anywhere from the 1100s to the 1500s.

Three generalisations hold across most of the continent, and you will need them for the rest of the course:

1. **Land was held in common for use**, not owned outright as a commodity by individuals. A family might farm a field, and a village might claim a hunting territory, but land was not bought and sold the way English law allowed.
2. **Gender roles were complementary and often gave women real power**, especially in farming societies where women controlled crops and households.
3. **Native peoples shaped their environments.** They burned forests to improve hunting, irrigated deserts and bred plants. The "untouched wilderness" is a later European myth, later used to justify taking land settlers called unused.

## The other side of the Atlantic: Europe and West Africa

**Western Europe** in the late 1400s had stronger monarchies, growing commercial cities and a hunger for Asian spices and silks. The Ottoman capture of Constantinople (1453) left a powerful Muslim empire astride the eastern routes, and Italian merchants controlled what reached the West. A sea route to Asia promised to cut out the middlemen. **Portugal** led the way. Through the 1400s its sailors and royal sponsors mapped the West African coast, developed the **caravel**, used the **astrolabe** and **magnetic compass**, and learned the wind patterns of the Atlantic. Dias rounded the Cape of Good Hope in 1488. **Spain** came later but with force: in January 1492 Ferdinand and Isabella conquered Granada, completing the **Reconquista**, and the same year they expelled Spain's Jews and agreed to fund Columbus. Europeans' motives are often summed up as **God, gold and glory**: spreading Christianity, new sources of wealth, and the quest for military and economic advantage.

**West Africa** was not a blank either. Empires such as **Mali** and **Songhai** had grown rich on trans-Saharan trade in gold and salt; coastal kingdoms such as **Benin** and **Kongo** were powerful states. Portugal's first relations with them were trade on African terms: at **Elmina** (1482) the Portuguese bought gold from local rulers. Slavery existed in many African societies, but in different forms. On the Atlantic islands of **Madeira** and **São Tomé**, however, Portugal combined sugar, plantations and enslaved African laborers into a new and brutal system, a rehearsal for what would cross the ocean.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Thomas Harriot on Algonquian farming (1588).**

> "Then their setting or sowing is after this maner. First for their corne, beginning in one corner of the plot, with a pecker they make a hole, wherein they put foure graines with that care they touch not one another, (about an inch asunder) and couer them with the moulde againe . . . By this meanes there is a yarde spare ground betwene euery hole: where according to discretion here and there, they set as many Beanes and Peaze . . ."

*Historical situation:* Harriot, an English mathematician, spent 1585–86 with Walter Raleigh's expedition at Roanoke. *Audience and purpose:* English investors and would-be colonists, whom he wanted to persuade that Virginia was fertile and worth settling. *Point of view:* an educated outsider, curious and careful, judging by English standards. *Why it matters:* although written after contact, it is a precise eyewitness account of a farming system that long predated the English, maize and beans in the same plot, the Three Sisters at work.

**2. Harriot on Algonquian towns.**

> "Their townes are but small, & neere the sea coast but few, some containing but 10. or 12. houses: some 20. the greatest that we haue seene haue bene but of 30. houses: if they be walled it is only done with barks of trees made fast to stakes, or els with poles onely fixed vpright and close one by another."

*Sourcing note:* A promoter had reason to make the Native population seem small and unthreatening, and Harriot saw only coastal towns. Use it as evidence of settled, palisaded villages, but do not use it to estimate population.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: sourcing with HIPP.** Prompt: *Explain one way Harriot's purpose affects his account of Algonquian towns.* Strong answer: "Harriot wrote to win support for an English colony, so he had reason to describe Native towns as small and lightly defended; the description may understate the population and strength of Algonquian communities." Name the purpose, then say *what* it makes the author emphasise or omit. "He was biased" earns nothing.

**Worked example 2: a contextualization paragraph.** For an essay on European exploration, a contextualization sentence set should reach beyond the prompt: "In the late 1400s, Western European monarchies were consolidating power and competing for trade. The Ottoman capture of Constantinople in 1453 strengthened Muslim and Italian control of the routes to Asian goods, while Portugal's voyages down the African coast had produced new ships, navigational tools and a model of sugar plantations worked by enslaved Africans. Spain, newly united by the conquest of Granada in 1492, was eager to compete." Specific, and *before* the thesis.

**Worked example 3: from prompt to thesis.** Prompt: *Evaluate the extent to which the natural environment shaped Native American societies before 1492.*
- Weak: "The environment shaped Native societies in many ways." (Restates the prompt.)
- Strong: "The environment largely determined how Native peoples made a living, from mobile bison hunters on the Plains to settled salmon fishers on the Northwest coast, but the spread of maize and peoples' own political and cultural choices, such as the Haudenosaunee league, meant that societies in similar environments could still differ greatly." (Takes a position on extent and sets up a line of reasoning.)

## Common misconceptions

- **"Native Americans were all the same."** Hundreds of languages, and economies from irrigated farming to salmon fishing. Diversity is the main idea.
- **"Plains Indians always rode horses."** Not before the Spanish; horse cultures developed in the 1600s and 1700s.
- **"Only farmers could live in permanent villages."** Northwest and California peoples did so without farming.
- **"The land was empty wilderness."** It was peopled and shaped by burning, farming and irrigation.
