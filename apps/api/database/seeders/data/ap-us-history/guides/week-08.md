In plain language: the first national government was weak on purpose, and that weakness nearly wrecked the new nation. In one secret summer a group of nationalists wrote a stronger government, then had to persuade a suspicious public to accept it, and the price of acceptance was a bill of rights.

## A government weak by design (3.7)

The Second Continental Congress drafted the **Articles of Confederation** in 1777, but they did not take effect until 1781. Maryland, which had no western land claims, refused to ratify until states such as Virginia ceded their claims to the national government. That cession turned the West into a national domain, which proved to be the Confederation's best opportunity.

The Articles were a reaction against Parliament and the crown. They created a "firm league of friendship" among states that each kept its "sovereignty, freedom and independence". Congress had one house, each state one vote; major measures required nine states and amendments required all thirteen. There was no executive and no national judiciary. Above all, Congress **could not tax**; it could only request money from the states, and it **could not regulate commerce**.

**Achievements.** The Confederation won the war and negotiated the Treaty of Paris. Its **Land Ordinance of 1785** imposed a grid on the Northwest: townships six miles square, sections of 640 acres sold at auction, one section in each township reserved for schools. The **Northwest Ordinance of 1787** laid out how territories would be governed and become states on an equal footing with the original thirteen, protected civil liberties, encouraged education, banned slavery north of the Ohio River and promised "good faith" toward American Indians. Those promises collided at once with settlers moving into land the Native nations had never ceded.

**Failures.** The war had been financed by loans, IOUs and paper money, and Congress could not repay any of it. Britain closed its West Indian ports to American ships and kept forts in the Northwest; Spain closed the lower Mississippi in 1784; Barbary corsairs seized American ships. States taxed one another's goods. Rhode Island alone blocked an amendment to give Congress a 5 percent import duty.

The crisis came in **Massachusetts**. To pay its war debt, the state levied heavy taxes payable in hard money. Farmers in the west, many of them veterans, faced foreclosure and debtors' prison. In 1786 armed crowds, later associated with Captain **Daniel Shays**, closed county courts. Congress could not help; the governor raised a militia paid for by Boston merchants, which dispersed the rebels in early 1787. To nationalists, **Shays' Rebellion** proved that the Articles could not protect property or order. Meanwhile, a meeting on commerce at **Annapolis** (1786) had drawn only five states; Hamilton and Madison used it to call a general convention in Philadelphia.

## The Constitutional Convention (3.8)

Fifty-five delegates, mostly wealthy and experienced, met in secret in Philadelphia from May to September 1787, with Washington presiding, and quickly decided to write a new constitution rather than revise the Articles.

**Large states versus small.** Madison's **Virginia Plan** proposed a strong national government with a two-house legislature apportioned by population. William Paterson's **New Jersey Plan** kept one house with equal votes per state. The **Great Compromise**, proposed by Connecticut's delegates, created a House of Representatives apportioned by population and elected by the people, where money bills must originate, and a Senate with two members per state chosen by state legislatures.

**North versus South over slavery.** Southern delegates wanted enslaved people counted fully for representation; many northerners wanted them not counted. The **three-fifths clause** counted three-fifths of "all other Persons" for representation and direct taxes. South Carolina and Georgia threatened to leave unless the slave trade was protected, so Congress was barred from banning it before **1808**. A **fugitive slave clause** required the return of people who escaped. The word "slave" appears nowhere, but slavery was protected in at least three places, and the three-fifths clause gave slaveholding states extra seats and electoral votes for decades.

**The executive.** Fearing both a king and a weak executive, the delegates created a single president with a four-year term, chosen by an **Electoral College**, commander in chief, with a veto that two-thirds of Congress could override. They knew Washington would be the first president, and that eased their fears.

## The Constitution's design (3.9)

The Constitution answered the Articles point by point: Congress may tax, borrow, regulate interstate and foreign commerce, raise armies and make all laws "necessary and proper" to carry out its powers; national law is supreme; states may not coin money or impair contracts. The new government acts directly on individuals, not only through the states.

It also limits that power. **Federalism** divides authority between nation and states. **Separation of powers** puts legislative, executive and judicial power in different branches, and **checks and balances** let each restrain the others: the veto and the override, Senate approval of treaties and appointments, impeachment, life tenure for judges. Madison explained the logic in **Federalist No. 51**: "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition", because if men were angels no government would be necessary. Amendment requires broad supermajorities but is possible, unlike under the Articles.

## Ratification and the Bill of Rights (3.8, 3.9)

Ratification required conventions in nine states. **Federalists**, strongest among merchants, creditors, coastal towns and nationalist leaders, argued that only a stronger government could pay debts, protect commerce, keep order and win respect abroad. **Anti-Federalists**, strongest among small farmers, the backcountry and state-centred leaders such as Patrick Henry, George Mason and Mercy Otis Warren, feared a distant government dominated by the wealthy, a standing army, a powerful executive and the swallowing of the states. Many believed, following Montesquieu, that a republic could survive only in a small territory. Their sharpest point was practical: there was **no bill of rights**.

Hamilton, Madison and John Jay answered in **The Federalist**, 85 newspaper essays aimed at New York. In **Federalist No. 10**, Madison turned the small-republic argument upside down: factions are inevitable, most often arising from the unequal distribution of property, but in a large republic so many interests compete that no single majority faction can easily oppress the rest.

Delaware ratified first (December 1787). Massachusetts ratified narrowly after Federalists agreed to recommend amendments, a pattern repeated in Virginia and New York. New Hampshire became the ninth state in June 1788, and Virginia and New York followed narrowly that summer; North Carolina and Rhode Island joined later.

Madison, once sceptical of a bill of rights, drafted amendments in the First Congress to honour the Federalists' promise. Ten were ratified in **1791**: freedoms of religion, speech, press and assembly; the right to bear arms; protections for people accused of crimes; and the **Tenth Amendment**, reserving undelegated powers to the states or the people.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Articles of Confederation, Article IX** (National Archives)

> ". . . nor coin money, nor regulate the value thereof . . . nor borrow money on the credit of the united states, nor appropriate money . . . nor appoint a commander in chief of the army or navy, unless nine states assent to the same . . ."

*HIPP.* Written in 1777 by a Congress at war against centralised power, for states jealous of their independence. Purpose: to make sure no national decision of consequence could be taken without broad agreement. Why it matters: a sensible safeguard in principle became paralysis in practice.

**2. George Washington to James Madison, 5 November 1786** (Founders Online)

> "No Morn ever dawned more favourable than ours did—and no day was ever more clouded than the present! . . . We are fast verging to anarchy & confusion!"

*HIPP.* A private letter from the most respected man in America, a planter and creditor, to the leading advocate of reform, written as news of Shays' Rebellion spread. Why it matters: Washington's alarm, and his willingness to attend the convention, gave the reform movement credibility.

**3. James Madison, The Federalist No. 10, November 1787** (Avalon Project)

> "Extend the sphere, and you take in a greater variety of parties and interests; you make it less probable that a majority of the whole will have a common motive to invade the rights of other citizens . . ."

*HIPP.* A New York newspaper essay signed Publius, aimed at convention delegates and voters weighing ratification. Purpose: to rebut the claim that republics must be small. Point of view: a Virginia nationalist alarmed by debtor-friendly state legislatures. Why it matters: the clearest theory of why the framers thought a large, complex republic would protect rights.

**4. Northwest Ordinance, Article 6, 1787** (National Archives)

> "There shall be neither slavery nor involuntary servitude in the said territory, otherwise than in the punishment of crimes whereof the party shall have been duly convicted . . ."

*Why it matters:* adopted the same summer as the Constitution's protections for slavery, it shows the founders' ambivalence.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: a comparison chart.** Comparison questions reward matching each problem to its solution.

| Problem under the Articles | The Constitution's answer |
|---|---|
| No power to tax | Congress may lay and collect taxes, duties and excises |
| No power over commerce | Congress regulates interstate and foreign commerce |
| No executive | President, commander in chief, with veto |
| No national courts | Supreme Court and federal judiciary |
| One state, one vote | House by population, Senate equal by state |
| Unanimity to amend | Two-thirds of Congress and three-quarters of states |
| States issue paper money | States barred from coining money or impairing contracts |

Note similarities too: both were federal and both left slavery to the states.

**Worked example 2: sourcing Federalist No. 10.** A sourcing point must explain how the circumstance shaped the content. Weak: "Madison wrote this to get the Constitution ratified." Strong: "Because Madison was writing to New York voters, many of whom accepted Montesquieu's view that only small republics last, he built his argument around the size of the republic, claiming that a large republic would better protect the rights of minorities, such as creditors, threatened by majorities in state legislatures."

**Worked example 3: an LEQ thesis.** Prompt: *Evaluate the extent to which the Constitution resolved the weaknesses of the Articles.* Thesis: "The Constitution resolved the Articles' central weakness, the lack of power to tax and enforce national law, but by creating a strong central government it opened new conflicts over the limits of that power and left slavery protected and unresolved." The claim answers *to what extent* and sets up three body paragraphs: resolved (finance and commerce), resolved (order and structure), unresolved or new (federal power, slavery).

## Common misconceptions

- **"The Articles achieved nothing."** The land ordinances were lasting and admired.
- **"The three-fifths clause said enslaved people were three-fifths human."** It was a formula for apportioning representation and taxes, a political bargain over power; it gave enslaved people no rights and enlarged their enslavers' power.
- **"The Bill of Rights was in the original Constitution."** It was added in 1791 to fulfil promises made during ratification.
- **"Anti-Federalists opposed any national government."** Most wanted a stronger union than the Articles, but with stronger limits and a bill of rights.
