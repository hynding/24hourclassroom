## The Second Great Awakening

Beginning around 1800 at frontier camp meetings such as Cane Ridge, Kentucky (1801), and peaking in the 1820s and 1830s, waves of revival swept the country. The message broke with the Calvinist predestination of the colonial churches: sin was a choice, and anyone could choose salvation. Charles Grandison Finney's revivals in western New York, the **burned-over district** along the Erie Canal, introduced "new measures": meetings lasting days, an "anxious bench" for those close to conversion, and women praying aloud. Methodists and Baptists, whose preachers needed no seminary, became the largest denominations.

**Why then?** Disestablishment made churches compete for members; the market revolution unsettled communities and made people receptive to a message of personal choice and self-discipline; and democratic politics made appeals to ordinary people the norm everywhere.

**Effects.** (1) Women, most of the converts, ran benevolent, missionary and reform societies. (2) Black Christians built their own churches, such as Richard Allen's African Methodist Episcopal Church (1816). (3) New faiths appeared: Joseph Smith founded the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints in 1830; after persecution and Smith's murder (1844), Brigham Young led the Saints to Utah. (4) **Perfectionism**: if individuals could be perfected, so could society, and quickly.

## An age of reform

**Temperance** was the largest movement. Heavy drinking was common, and reformers linked it to poverty, violence and wasted wages; the American Temperance Society (1826) urged abstinence, consumption fell sharply, and Maine adopted prohibition in 1851.

**Institutions and schools.** Reformers believed the right environment could remake people. Dorothea Dix exposed the jailing of the mentally ill and won state asylums; penitentiaries aimed at rehabilitation; Horace Mann built tax-supported **common schools** in Massachusetts to produce disciplined citizens, a model other northern states followed. Many Protestant reformers also hoped schools would Americanise Catholic immigrants, a source of conflict.

**Utopias.** Some withdrew to build perfect societies: Brook Farm (Transcendentalist), New Harmony (Robert Owen's socialism), Oneida (shared property and "complex marriage") and the Shakers (celibacy, sexual equality). Most were short-lived.

**Abolition.** Early antislavery favoured gradual emancipation and colonization through the American Colonization Society (1816), which founded Liberia. Free Black Americans rejected colonization; David Walker's *Appeal* (1829) called for resistance. In 1831 William Lloyd Garrison's *Liberator* demanded **immediate** emancipation, and the American Anti-Slavery Society followed in 1833. Abolitionists were a small and often hated minority: mobs attacked them in the North, and the editor Elijah Lovejoy was killed in Illinois in 1837. Black abolitionists, above all Frederick Douglass, were the movement's most powerful witnesses. The movement split in 1840 over women's role and political action; the Liberty Party ran candidates in 1840 and 1844.

**Women's rights.** Women in abolition met limits to their own freedom. The Grimké sisters were attacked for lecturing to mixed audiences, and women delegates were refused seats at the 1840 World Anti-Slavery Convention in London, where Elizabeth Cady Stanton met Lucretia Mott. In July 1848 they convened at **Seneca Falls**, New York. The **Declaration of Sentiments** rewrote 1776: "all men and women are created equal." It listed grievances on property, marriage, education and divorce, and, most controversially, said "He has never permitted her to exercise her inalienable right to the elective franchise." The suffrage resolution passed narrowly, with Douglass's support.

## African Americans in the early republic

By 1840 about 2.5 million people were enslaved and about 390,000 African Americans were free. Free Black communities in northern cities such as Philadelphia, New York and Boston, and in southern cities such as Baltimore, built churches, schools, mutual aid societies and newspapers, and met from 1830 in national conventions. They faced segregation, exclusion from the vote in most states (week 12) and mob violence.

Enslaved people resisted in many ways: slowing work, breaking tools, running away, keeping families and faith alive against sale and separation, and, rarely, organised revolt. Gabriel's conspiracy (Virginia, 1800) and Denmark Vesey's (Charleston, 1822) were betrayed before they began. In 1831 Nat Turner led a revolt in Southampton County, Virginia, that killed about 55 to 60 white people before militias crushed it and killed many Black people in reprisal. Virginia's legislature debated gradual emancipation in 1831–1832 and chose stricter control instead.

## The society of the South

The cotton gin and fertile lands taken from Native nations made the Deep South the world's leading cotton producer, and by the 1830s cotton was the largest U.S. export. The international slave trade ended in 1808, but natural increase and the **domestic slave trade** expanded slavery: perhaps a million people were forcibly moved from the Upper South to the cotton states between 1790 and 1860, many sold away from their families.

White southern society was a pyramid. A small planter elite owned most enslaved people and dominated politics. Most white families owned none; they were yeoman farmers, many of whom still defended slavery out of racial solidarity, ties of kinship and trade with planters, and the hope of owning enslaved people themselves. The South built fewer cities, factories and schools than the North; its capital was invested in enslaved people and land.

Southern defences of slavery hardened after 1830. Against Garrison, Walker and Turner, John C. Calhoun and others called slavery a **positive good**. States tightened slave codes, banned abolitionist mail, and southern congressmen imposed the **gag rule** (1836–1844) on antislavery petitions. Southern Methodists and Baptists split from their northern brethren in 1844–1845.

## Causation in Period 4

Four causes drove the period's changes, and they interacted:

1. **The market revolution** linked the North and West, created a middle class and wage labour, and, through cotton, tied the South more tightly to slavery.
2. **Democratisation** gave white men the vote and built mass parties, while hardening racial lines.
3. **The Second Great Awakening** turned individual faith into collective reform.
4. **Westward expansion**, from Louisiana to Indian removal, kept reopening the question of slavery's spread.

The result by 1848 was a more democratic, more prosperous and more divided nation.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Declaration of Sentiments, Seneca Falls, July 1848**

> "The history of mankind is a history of repeated injuries and usurpations on the part of man toward woman, having in direct object the establishment of an absolute tyranny over her."

*HIPP.* Situation: abolitionist women excluded from leadership, and a wave of reform in western New York. Audience: the convention, the press and the public. Purpose: to frame women's rights as the logical extension of American principles. Point of view: middle-class, mostly white reformers; the document addresses the legal disabilities of married women more than the conditions of working or enslaved women. **Why it matters:** it began the organised women's suffrage movement that ended with the Nineteenth Amendment (1920).

**2. Frederick Douglass, Narrative (1845)**

> "I now understood what had been to me a most perplexing difficulty—to wit, the white man's power to enslave the black man. It was a grand achievement, and I prized it highly. From that moment, I understood the pathway from slavery to freedom."

*HIPP.* Situation: a fugitive, still legally enslaved, publishing under his own name in 1845. Audience: northern readers. Purpose: to prove slavery's cruelty and Black intellectual equality. **Why it matters:** slave narratives were among abolition's most persuasive weapons, and literacy is a recurring theme of Black freedom.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: HIPP sourcing of the Declaration of Sentiments.** "Explain how the purpose of the document affects its argument." *Model:* Because the authors wanted to persuade a skeptical public that women's rights were not a dangerous novelty, they copied the structure and language of the Declaration of Independence, presenting their demands as the fulfilment of principles every American already claimed to hold.

**Worked example 2: reading the census.** Enslaved population: about 700,000 (1790), 1.19 million (1810), 2.01 million (1830), 2.49 million (1840). (1) Describe: more than tripled in fifty years. (2) Explain: imports ended in 1808, so growth after 1810 came from natural increase, while cotton made enslaved labour valuable and the domestic trade moved people south and west. (3) Connect: the growth explains why the South defended slavery more aggressively after 1830 and why its expansion into new territory became the central political question of Period 5.

**Worked example 3: a causation thesis.** Prompt: "Evaluate the relative importance of the causes of reform, 1820–1848."

- Weak: "Religion, economics and ideals all caused reform."
- Strong: "Although the market revolution created the middle class that filled reform societies and Revolutionary ideals supplied reformers' language, the Second Great Awakening was the most important cause, because its doctrine that sin was a choice made immediate personal and social perfection seem possible, turning converts into activists."

The strong version ranks causes and gives the reason for the ranking.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Most northerners were abolitionists."** Abolitionists were a small, often persecuted minority before 1848; most northerners opposed slavery's expansion later, not slavery itself.
- **"Most white southerners owned enslaved people."** Most did not; a planter minority held most enslaved people.
- **"Slavery was dying out before the Civil War."** The enslaved population grew rapidly and slavery was spreading west.
- **"Seneca Falls was only about the vote."** The vote was the most controversial demand, but the Declaration also attacked laws on property, marriage, divorce and education.
- **"Reform was purely liberal."** Some reformers also sought to impose Protestant morality on immigrants.

## Where this goes next

Period 5 opens in 1844 with Texas and Manifest Destiny. The abolitionist minority becomes a free-soil majority in the North when the Mexican Cession raises the old Missouri question again; the gag rule and the proslavery argument turn into fights over the Wilmot Proviso, the Fugitive Slave Act and Kansas. Women's rights activists will press for the vote again during Reconstruction.
