## From neutral trade to war, 1803–1815

When Britain and Napoleonic France went back to war in 1803, American merchants profited by carrying goods for both sides, and both sides seized American ships. Britain also **impressed** sailors, and in 1807 HMS *Leopard* fired on the U.S. frigate *Chesapeake* and took four men. Jefferson chose economic pressure over war: the **Embargo Act** (1807) banned all exports. It hurt Americans far more than Europe. New England ports idled, smuggling boomed, and the Federalists revived. Its replacements, the Non-Intercourse Act (1809) and Macon's Bill No. 2 (1810), failed too.

In the West, the grievance was different. Tecumseh and his brother Tenskwatawa were building a confederacy of Native nations to stop land cessions north of the Ohio, and Americans blamed British agents in Canada for arming them. William Henry Harrison's victory at **Tippecanoe** (1811) did not end the movement. The **War Hawks** in Congress, most from the South and West, demanded war for national honour, free seas and security on the frontier, and some hoped to take Canada. Madison asked for war in June 1812; New England's representatives voted against it.

The war went badly at first: invasions of Canada failed, and in 1814 the British burned Washington. But American naval victories on Lake Erie and Lake Champlain, Harrison's victory at the Thames (where Tecumseh died) and Jackson's defeat of the Red Stick Creeks at Horseshoe Bend (1814) changed the picture. The **Treaty of Ghent** (December 1814) restored prewar boundaries. Two weeks later, before news crossed the Atlantic, Jackson routed the British at **New Orleans**. Americans remembered the war as a victory.

**Effects.** (1) Nationalism and a new hero, Jackson. (2) The **Hartford Convention**'s demands reached Washington amid victory celebrations, and the Federalist Party collapsed. (3) Native nations east of the Mississippi lost their British ally and their last chance at a united defence. (4) Cut off from British goods, Americans had built more factories, which the Tariff of 1816 then protected.

## Diplomacy after 1815 and the Monroe Doctrine

With Britain, the **Rush–Bagot Agreement** (1817) demilitarised the Great Lakes and the **Convention of 1818** set the border at the 49th parallel to the Rockies and joint occupation of Oregon. With Spain, Secretary of State **John Quincy Adams** used Jackson's 1818 raid into Florida, made in pursuit of Seminoles and the people who had escaped slavery and lived among them, to win the **Adams–Onís Treaty** (1819): Florida, and a boundary to the Pacific.

Meanwhile Spain's American empire collapsed. Fearing that Europe's restored monarchies would help Spain take it back, and that Russia would push south from Alaska, Monroe told Congress in 1823 that the Americas were "henceforth not to be considered as subjects for future colonization by any European powers," and that European intervention would be considered "dangerous to our peace and safety." In return, the U.S. would stay out of Europe's wars. Britain's navy, not American power, made the doctrine credible; Latin Americans noticed that it protected U.S. interests more than theirs. It became the foundation of American policy in the hemisphere for a century.

## The market revolution: how a national market was built

**Transportation.** Before 1815, hauling goods a few dozen miles inland by wagon could cost as much as carrying them across the Atlantic by ship. Then came turnpikes and the federal **National Road**; Robert Fulton's steamboat (1807), which made the Ohio and Mississippi two-way rivers; and the **Erie Canal** (1825), built by New York State, which cut freight costs from Buffalo to New York City by about 90% and made New York the nation's greatest port. A canal boom followed, and by the 1840s railroads were overtaking canals. Samuel Morse's **telegraph** (1844) let prices and news travel faster than goods.

**Law.** Marshall's Court supplied the legal frame. **McCulloch v. Maryland** (1819) upheld the Second Bank: "Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the Constitution, and all means which are appropriate ... are Constitutional," and "the power to tax involves the power to destroy." **Gibbons v. Ogden** (1824) struck down New York's steamboat monopoly: "Commerce, undoubtedly, is traffic, but it is something more: it is intercourse." Competition opened the rivers.

**Manufacturing.** Samuel Slater's Rhode Island mill (1793) spun cotton by water power. The Boston Associates' mills at **Lowell** (from the 1820s) did everything under one roof. Interchangeable parts spread from federal armouries to clocks and tools. Most factories were in the Northeast, near water power, capital and labour.

**Agriculture.** The cotton gin (1793) made the Deep South a cotton empire worked by enslaved people; the steel plough and the mechanical reaper made the Midwest a grain region. Regions specialised: the South grew cotton, the Northeast made goods, the West sold food. Specialisation tied them together, and in time pulled the Northwest's trade east toward New York rather than south toward New Orleans.

## The market revolution in society

**Work.** Apprentices and journeymen who once expected to own shops became wage earners. Workers formed unions and pressed for a ten-hour day; *Commonwealth v. Hunt* (1842) ruled unions were not illegal conspiracies. The Lowell women struck in 1834 and 1836 against wage cuts and higher board.

**Class and family.** A **middle class** of clerks, merchants and professionals grew, with values of self-discipline, education and sobriety. As paid work left the home, the ideal of **separate spheres** assigned men to the market and women to a moral, domestic world. That ideal confined women but also gave them moral authority to carry into reform (week 13). Families had fewer children.

**Immigration.** Arrivals rose from about 130,000 in the 1820s to about 1.4 million in the 1840s (DHS figures). The Irish, fleeing famine after 1845, were mostly poor Catholics who stayed in eastern cities and dug canals and railroads. Germans more often had some capital and bought Midwestern farms. **Nativists** attacked Catholic institutions, burning a convent near Boston in 1834, and by the 1850s formed the Know-Nothing Party.

**Region.** The North and West grew more urban and more alike; the South grew richer but stayed rural, with less industry and fewer immigrants. Different economies would mean different interests on tariffs, banks, land and, above all, slavery.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. James Monroe, annual message, December 2, 1823**

> "... we should consider any attempt on their part to extend their system to any portion of this hemisphere as dangerous to our peace and safety."

*HIPP.* Situation: Latin American independence, the Holy Alliance, Russian claims. Audience: Congress, but really Europe. Purpose: to warn off European powers while avoiding a joint statement with Britain. Point of view: John Quincy Adams's continental nationalism. **Why it matters:** it is the starting point for every later U.S. claim in the hemisphere.

**2. McCulloch v. Maryland, 1819**

> "That the power to tax involves the power to destroy; that the power to destroy may defeat and render useless the power to create ..."

*HIPP.* Situation: states taxing the unpopular Second Bank during the Panic of 1819. Purpose: to protect federal institutions from state control. **Why it matters:** it writes Hamilton's implied-powers argument into constitutional law and sets up Jackson's veto in 1832.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: a causation chain.** Link the Embargo to the end of the Federalists:

Embargo Act (1807) → New England shipping collapses → Federalists revive in New England → New England opposes the War of 1812 → Hartford Convention (1814–1815) → convention's demands arrive with news of Ghent and New Orleans → Federalists look disloyal → party disappears by 1820 → one-party Era of Good Feelings.

Each arrow should be a "because" you could defend in a sentence. A secondary chain runs from the Embargo to manufacturing: no imports → domestic factories → demand for the Tariff of 1816.

**Worked example 2: reading a table.** The immigration figures (about 130,000 in the 1820s, 540,000 in the 1830s, 1.4 million in the 1840s) roughly tripled each decade. A strong answer (1) describes the trend precisely, (2) explains it with push factors (the Irish famine, German upheavals) and pull factors (canal, railroad and factory jobs, cheap land), and (3) connects it to an effect (nativism, urban poverty, a cheaper factory workforce).

**Worked example 3: an SAQ on Lowell.** "(a) Briefly describe ONE feature of the Lowell system. (b) Briefly explain ONE way it changed women's lives. (c) Briefly explain ONE limit to that change."

- (a) Lowell mills hired young unmarried New England farm women, housed them in supervised boardinghouses and paid cash wages.
- (b) Wages gave many young women their first independent income and a period away from family control, and some used it to fund education or a dowry.
- (c) Work was long and closely supervised, and when owners cut wages in 1834 and 1836 the women's strikes failed; by the late 1840s Irish immigrants willing to accept lower wages were replacing them.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The U.S. won the War of 1812."** It was a draw; the Treaty of Ghent changed no borders. The clear losers were Native nations.
- **"The Monroe Doctrine was enforced by the U.S. Navy in 1823."** The U.S. lacked the power; Britain's navy did the work.
- **"The market revolution meant the North industrialised and the South did not change."** The South's cotton economy was a central part of the market revolution and grew with it.
- **"Most Northerners were factory workers by 1848."** Most still farmed, but for markets.
- **"The federal government built the canals."** States and private companies built most of them.
