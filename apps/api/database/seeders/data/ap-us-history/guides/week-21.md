## Reformers and critics

Industrial America's problems (slums, child labor, poverty amid wealth, corruption) produced a generation of reformers, many from the new middle class and many of them women.

**Religious reform.** The **Social Gospel**, preached by ministers such as Washington Gladden, taught that Christians must change the conditions that produced poverty, not merely urge the poor to virtue.

**Settlement houses.** Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr opened **Hull House** in Chicago in 1889. Its college-educated residents ran kindergartens, clubs, classes and a public kitchen, studied the neighborhood's sweatshops and sanitation, and lobbied for factory and child-labor laws. Settlement work gave educated women a public role at a time when most professions and the vote were closed to them.

**Temperance and suffrage.** The **Woman's Christian Temperance Union** (1874) became, under Frances Willard, the largest women's organization in the country; its 'Do Everything' policy linked temperance to suffrage, labor and prison reform. The two wings of the suffrage movement, divided since 1869, united in 1890 as the **National American Woman Suffrage Association**. Western states led: Wyoming entered the Union with woman suffrage in 1890, followed by Colorado (1893) and Utah and Idaho (1896). African American women organized the National Association of Colored Women (1896), joining suffrage to the fight against lynching and segregation.

**Radical critics.** Henry George's *Progress and Poverty* (1879) asked why poverty deepened as progress advanced and proposed a 'single tax' on land values. Edward Bellamy's *Looking Backward* (1888) imagined a cooperative society in the year 2000 and inspired 'Nationalist' clubs. Socialists, though few, gained a foothold, especially after the Pullman strike turned Eugene Debs toward socialism.

## The role of government: a contested question

**The case for laissez-faire.** Social Darwinists such as William Graham Sumner argued that government help for one class robbed another, the 'forgotten man' who paid. President Grover Cleveland, vetoing $10,000 for seed for drought-stricken Texas farmers in 1887, declared that the people should support the government and not the reverse. Courts treated many regulations as violations of property rights and liberty of contract.

**The contradiction.** The same governments that refused relief actively helped business: land grants and loans to railroads, high protective tariffs, troops and injunctions against strikers, and a gold standard that favored creditors. The real question was not *whether* government would act, but *for whom*.

**The case for regulation.** Farmers organized through the **Grange** won state laws regulating railroad and grain-elevator rates, upheld in **Munn v. Illinois** (1877). But in **Wabash v. Illinois** (1886) the Court barred states from regulating interstate rates, so Congress passed the **Interstate Commerce Act** (1887), creating the first federal regulatory agency, and the **Sherman Antitrust Act** (1890). Both were weakened by the courts in the 1890s; *E. C. Knight* (1895) narrowed the Sherman Act and *Pollock* (1895) struck down a federal income tax.

## Gilded Age politics

From 1876 to 1896 the two parties were almost perfectly balanced. Presidential elections were decided by tiny margins; in 1876 and 1888 the winner lost the popular vote. Turnout was very high, often around 80 percent of eligible voters outside the South, because party loyalty was bound up with region, religion, ethnicity and memories of the Civil War. **Republicans**, strong in the Northeast and Midwest among Protestants and Union veterans, defended high tariffs and sound money and 'waved the bloody shirt'. **Democrats**, solid in the white South and strong among Catholic immigrants in Northern cities, favored lower tariffs and limited government.

Three issues dominated:

- **Patronage.** Party machines ran on government jobs. When a disappointed office seeker assassinated President James Garfield in 1881, Congress passed the **Pendleton Civil Service Act** (1883), requiring competitive exams for a share of federal jobs.
- **The tariff.** Republicans claimed protection raised wages; Democrats and farmers said it raised prices. The **McKinley Tariff** (1890) pushed rates to new highs and cost Republicans in 1890 and 1892.
- **Money.** The Union's wartime greenbacks were gradually redeemed in gold after 1879. Debtors wanted inflation: first greenbacks (the Greenback Party of the 1870s), then the free coinage of silver. The Coinage Act of 1873, which silverites called the 'Crime of '73', dropped the silver dollar; the Bland-Allison Act (1878) and the Sherman Silver Purchase Act (1890) offered limited compromises.

## The Populist revolt and the election of 1896

Farmers' problems (falling crop prices, debt, railroad rates, crop liens) went unaddressed by both parties. In the 1880s the **Farmers' Alliances** organized cooperatives and sent lecturers across the South and the Plains; the Colored Farmers' Alliance organized Black farmers. When the major parties ignored the Alliances' **subtreasury plan** for federal crop loans, they formed the **People's Party**. Its **Omaha Platform** (1892) demanded free silver, a graduated income tax, government ownership of railroads, telegraphs and telephones, and postal savings banks, and backed the direct election of senators and the secret ballot. Its candidate, James B. Weaver, won over a million votes and 22 electoral votes. In Georgia, Tom Watson urged Black and white farmers to unite on their economic interests; Democrats answered with fraud, violence and race-baiting.

The **Panic of 1893** brought the worst depression the country had yet seen. Cleveland's defense of gold, including a loan arranged by J. P. Morgan's bankers, split the Democrats; Coxey's Army of the unemployed marched on Washington in 1894; the Pullman Strike was crushed. In 1896 the Democrats nominated **William Jennings Bryan** after his 'Cross of Gold' speech, and the Populists endorsed him, giving up their independence. Republican **William McKinley**, backed by Mark Hanna's record fund-raising, won the industrial Northeast and Midwest, including many urban workers who feared free silver would raise prices. The election realigned politics: Republicans dominated until 1932, the Populists faded, and the Gold Standard Act (1900) settled the money question. Yet much of the Omaha Platform became law in the Progressive Era.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Munn v. Illinois (1877).**

> "Property does become clothed with a public interest when used in a manner to make it of public consequence and affect the community at large. When, therefore, one devotes his property to a use in which the public has an interest, he, in effect, grants to the public an interest in that use, and must submit to be controlled by the public for the common good, to the extent of the interest he has thus created."

*Sourcing:* Chief Justice Morrison Waite for the Supreme Court, upholding an Illinois Granger law. *Why it matters:* the constitutional foundation for regulating private business, soon narrowed but never abandoned.

**2. Cleveland's veto of the Texas Seed Bill (1887).**

> "A prevalent tendency to disregard the limited mission of this power and duty should, I think, be steadfastly resisted, to the end that the lesson should be constantly enforced that though the people support the Government the Government should not support the people."

*Sourcing:* a Democratic president, committed to limited government and the gold standard, writing to Congress. Audience: Congress and the public. *Why it matters:* the clearest statement of Gilded Age laissez-faire; set it against the Omaha Platform.

**3. The Omaha Platform (1892).**

> "We believe that the power of government—in other words, of the people—should be expanded (as in the case of the postal service) as rapidly and as far as the good sense of an intelligent people and the teachings of experience shall justify, to the end that oppression, injustice and poverty, shall eventually cease in the land."

*Sourcing:* a national convention of farmers' alliance members, labor and reform delegates, meeting on the Fourth of July; partly a manifesto, partly a campaign document. *Why it matters:* the first major party program to claim that an active federal government could end poverty.

**4. Bryan (1896).**

> "You shall not press down upon the brow of labor this crown of thorns. You shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold."

*Sourcing:* a 36-year-old former congressman from Nebraska addressing the Democratic convention; the purpose was to win the nomination and commit the party to silver. Its religious imagery spoke to evangelical rural voters, and limited its appeal to urban Catholics.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: sourcing Cleveland's veto for a DBQ.**
"Cleveland's veto reflects his point of view as a Bourbon Democrat committed to limited government and sound money; because he addressed Congress during a period of rising agrarian protest, his warning against 'paternal care' was also meant to resist growing demands from farmers' organizations for federal aid." It earns sourcing by explaining how point of view and situation shape the argument.

**Worked example 2: a thesis on the Populists.**
Prompt: "Evaluate the extent to which the Populist movement succeeded." Thesis: "Although the People's Party failed as a party, collapsing after it fused with the Democrats in 1896, it succeeded to a considerable extent as a movement, because the income tax, the direct election of senators and federal regulation of railroads that it demanded became law within two decades."

**Worked example 3: a continuity-and-change paragraph for 6.14.**
"Between 1865 and 1898 the American economy changed from a collection of regional markets into a national industrial system dominated by corporations, and the federal government took its first steps toward regulating it. But the period's politics showed striking continuity: the two parties remained evenly balanced around Civil War loyalties, and the dominant belief in limited government, voiced by Cleveland in 1887, held firm until the depression of the 1890s and the Populist challenge forced the question of what government owed its citizens onto the national agenda."

## Common misconceptions

- **"Gilded Age government did nothing."** It subsidized, protected and policed on behalf of business; it regulated little.
- **"The Populists were only about silver."** Silver dominated in 1896, but the Omaha Platform was a broad program.
- **"The Populists failed completely."** Their party died; much of their program became law.
- **"Bryan lost because of fraud."** He lost because his coalition could not win industrial workers and the cities.
- **"Woman suffrage began in the East."** The first full-suffrage states were in the West.
