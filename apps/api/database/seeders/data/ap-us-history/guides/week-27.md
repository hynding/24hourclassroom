## Contextualizing Period 8: a superpower and its anxieties

Period 8 runs from 1945 to 1980, and its opening is unlike any earlier moment in American history. The United States came out of World War II with its cities untouched, its factories producing roughly half of the world's manufactured goods, a monopoly on the atomic bomb and a leading role in the new United Nations, the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund. Isolationism, which had shaped policy in the 1930s, was discredited: most leaders concluded that staying out of world affairs had let fascism grow.

Two other threads run through the period: a long boom that built a mass middle class (week 28), and rights movements that demanded the country live up to its ideals (weeks 28–30). By the 1970s Vietnam, Watergate and an energy crisis eroded confidence in government. Almost every Period 8 question touches the Cold War, prosperity or rights.

## From allies to adversaries, 1945–1947

The wartime alliance with the Soviet Union rested on a common enemy. Once Germany was defeated, the two powers' goals collided. Stalin, whose country had lost something like 27 million people, wanted a belt of friendly governments in Eastern Europe and a weak Germany. The United States wanted self-determination, free elections and an open world economy. At Yalta (February 1945) Stalin had promised free elections in liberated Europe; by 1946 Soviet-backed Communists controlled Poland, Romania and Bulgaria, and the elections were not free.

Each side read the other's actions as aggressive: Americans saw Soviet pressure on Iran, Turkey and Greece; Soviet leaders saw the end of Lend-Lease and the atomic monopoly. In February 1946 George Kennan, a diplomat in Moscow, sent the Long Telegram, arguing that Soviet hostility came from deep insecurity and ideology and could be contained only by steady counter-pressure. A month later Churchill, speaking at Fulton, Missouri, described an iron curtain dividing Europe.

## The tools of containment

**Economic first (1947–1948).** In February 1947 Britain told the United States it could no longer support Greece and Turkey. Truman went before Congress and asked for $400 million, framing the request as part of a world struggle between two ways of life. The **Truman Doctrine** committed the United States, in principle, to support free peoples anywhere. Three months later Secretary of State George Marshall proposed what became the **European Recovery Program**: about $13 billion over four years to rebuild Western Europe. It aimed to remove the poverty that made communism attractive and to restore trading partners for American exports. The offer was open to the Soviet bloc, but Stalin refused it and kept his satellites out.

**Confrontation over Germany (1948–1949).** When the Western powers moved to merge their occupation zones and introduce a new currency, Stalin blockaded West Berlin. Truman chose neither retreat nor force: for about eleven months American and British aircraft supplied the city. The Soviets lifted the blockade in May 1949, and Germany was split into two states.

**Alliances and the security state (1947–1949).** The **National Security Act** (1947) created the CIA and the National Security Council. In 1949 the United States joined **NATO**, its first peacetime alliance since 1778, a direct reversal of the Neutrality Acts tradition. The Soviets answered with the Warsaw Pact in 1955.

**Militarisation (1949–1953).** Two shocks arrived in 1949: the Soviet atomic test in August and Mao Zedong's Communist victory in China in October. Truman approved a hydrogen bomb program, and the National Security Council drafted **NSC-68**, which described the Soviet Union as bent on world domination and called for a vast, permanent military buildup. It was expensive, and Truman hesitated until North Korea invaded South Korea in June 1950. The **Korean War** made NSC-68 policy. A U.S.-led United Nations force pushed north; China intervened in late 1950 and the war stalemated near the 38th parallel. Truman fired MacArthur (April 1951) for demanding a wider war, upholding civilian control and **limited war**. The 1953 armistice left Korea divided, defense spending had roughly tripled, and containment now covered Asia.

**Eisenhower's New Look (1953–1961).** To hold down costs, Eisenhower and Dulles relied on nuclear deterrence (**massive retaliation**), alliances and covert action. Sputnik (1957) led to NASA and the National Defense Education Act (1958). Week 29 continues the story.

**Perspectives on origins.** An "orthodox" view holds that the United States responded defensively to Soviet expansion; "revisionists" stress American economic aims and pressure on a devastated Soviet Union; "post-revisionists" argue that each side misread the other. You need not name the schools, but you must show how evidence supports more than one reading.

## The Second Red Scare

Fear had a real basis: Soviet agents had penetrated the Manhattan Project and government agencies, as decoded cables (Venona) later confirmed. But the response reached far beyond spies.

- **Loyalty testing.** Executive Order 9835 (March 1947) ordered loyalty investigations of all federal employees and applicants. Membership in any organisation the Attorney General labelled subversive could count against a worker. Millions were screened; several thousand resigned or were dismissed, often without being told the evidence against them.
- **Congressional investigations.** HUAC's 1947 hearings on Hollywood produced the jailing of the Hollywood Ten for contempt and a blacklist that ruined careers. In 1948 the committee heard Whittaker Chambers accuse Alger Hiss; Hiss's perjury conviction in 1950 made Richard Nixon's name.
- **The shocks of 1949–1950.** The Soviet bomb, the "loss" of China, the arrest of the physicist Klaus Fuchs and then the Rosenbergs made betrayal seem a plausible explanation for setbacks abroad.
- **McCarthy.** In February 1950 Senator Joseph McCarthy claimed to hold a list of Communists in the State Department. He never proved it, but his accusations dominated politics for four years.
- **Laws and courts.** The McCarran Internal Security Act (1950) passed over Truman's veto; in Dennis v. United States (1951) the Supreme Court upheld Smith Act convictions of Communist leaders.
- **Wider reach.** Teachers' loyalty oaths; the CIO's expulsion of left-led unions; the Lavender Scare (Executive Order 10450, 1953).

The scare ebbed after the televised Army–McCarthy hearings and McCarthy's censure (1954), though the security bureaucracy lasted much longer.

**Perspectives.** Many liberals supported loyalty programs as protection against real espionage; critics such as Margaret Chase Smith argued that smears destroyed the freedoms they claimed to protect.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Harry S. Truman, address to Congress, March 12, 1947**

> "I believe that it must be the policy of the United States to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures."

*HIPP:* Historical situation: Britain's withdrawal from Greece and Turkey. Intended audience: a Republican Congress keen to cut spending, and the public. Purpose: win $400 million in aid by framing it as a universal principle. Point of view: a president who saw Soviet pressure as part of a global pattern. *Why it matters:* the open-ended wording ("free peoples," not "Greece and Turkey") is what made the doctrine a template for later commitments, including Korea and Vietnam.

**2. George C. Marshall, Harvard speech, June 5, 1947**

> "Our policy is directed not against any country or doctrine but against hunger, poverty, desperation, and chaos."

*HIPP:* A commencement speech whose real audience was European governments, invited to plan their own recovery with American money. *Why it matters:* read alongside Truman's, it shows containment combining generosity with strategy.

**3. Executive Order 9835, March 21, 1947**

> "There shall be a loyalty investigation of every person entering the civilian employment of any department or agency of the executive branch of the Federal Government."

*HIPP:* Issued by Truman partly to meet Republican charges that his administration was soft on communism. *Why it matters:* the loyalty program predates McCarthy by three years; McCarthyism grew in ground the government had already prepared.

**4. Margaret Chase Smith, Declaration of Conscience, June 1, 1950**

> "But I do not want to see the Republican party ride to political victory on the Four Horsemen of Calumny—Fear, Ignorance, Bigotry, and Smear."

*HIPP:* A Republican senator speaking against tactics that were helping her party in an election year; the Congressional Record carried it to a national audience. *Why it matters:* evidence that resistance to McCarthyism existed early and inside his own party.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: a contextualization paragraph.** Prompt: "Evaluate the extent to which the United States changed its foreign policy after World War II."

> Before 1941 the United States had avoided permanent alliances: the Senate rejected the League of Nations, and the Neutrality Acts of the 1930s tried to keep the country out of European wars. World War II convinced most American leaders that isolation had allowed aggression to grow, and the war left the United States as the world's dominant economic power and sole atomic power, while the Soviet Union occupied Eastern Europe. That combination of new power and a new rival set the stage for a fundamental change in policy.

Why it works: it describes the situation before the period and links it to the prompt.

**Worked example 2: an SAQ part answered.** "Briefly explain ONE way the Korean War changed U.S. foreign policy."

> The Korean War turned containment into a global military commitment. Before 1950 Truman had hesitated to fund the large buildup NSC-68 recommended; after North Korea's invasion, defense spending roughly tripled and the United States committed to defending Taiwan and aiding the French in Indochina, extending containment to Asia.

Why it works: a specific change, a specific mechanism and specific evidence, in three sentences.

**Worked example 3: a comparison chain (two Red Scares).** Similarity: both followed a world war and curbed civil liberties in the name of security. Differences: 1919–1920 aimed at immigrant radicals and faded in two years; 1947–1954 reached officials, teachers, entertainers and gay federal workers and was built into executive orders, committees and statute. Thesis: "Both Red Scares used fear of communism to restrict dissent, but the second was longer and more institutionalised because the Cold War made the threat appear permanent."

## Common misconceptions

- **"McCarthy started the Red Scare."** Truman's loyalty program (1947), HUAC's Hollywood hearings (1947) and the Hiss case (1948) came first.
- **"Containment meant freeing Eastern Europe."** It meant stopping further expansion. The United States did not intervene in Hungary in 1956.
- **"The Marshall Plan was only charity."** It was also strategy and economics: stable democracies and markets for American goods.
- **"There was no espionage."** There was real Soviet espionage; the problem was the scale and recklessness of the response.
