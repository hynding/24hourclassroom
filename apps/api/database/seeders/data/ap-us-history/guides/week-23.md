## From neutrality to war (7.5)

When Europe went to war in August 1914, Wilson asked Americans to be "impartial in thought as well as in action." The request made sense for a nation of immigrants, but neutrality in trade was impossible. Britain's navy blockaded Germany, so American goods and loans flowed almost entirely to Britain and France. By 1917 the U.S. economy was deeply tied to an Allied victory.

Germany's answer to the blockade was the submarine, which could not follow the old rules of stopping a ship and saving its crew without risking its own destruction. The sinking of the *Lusitania* in 1915 killed 128 Americans. Wilson protested so firmly that his pacifist Secretary of State, William Jennings Bryan, resigned. After another attack in 1916, Germany promised (the Sussex pledge) to stop sinking merchant ships without warning, and Wilson won re-election that November on the slogan "He kept us out of war."

In January 1917 Germany gambled. It announced unrestricted submarine warfare from February 1, betting it could starve Britain before American troops could matter. The same month its foreign secretary, Arthur Zimmermann, cabled Mexico proposing an alliance: "We intend to begin on the first of February unrestricted submarine warfare. We shall endeavor in spite of this to keep the United States of America neutral." If that failed, Mexico was to reconquer "the lost territory in Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona." British intelligence intercepted the telegram and gave it to Washington, which published it in March. U-boats sank American ships that month; the Russian Revolution removed the tsar, making the Allies look more democratic. On April 2 Wilson asked for war, and Congress declared it on April 6.

## Fighting the war and making the peace (7.5)

The United States was not ready. The Selective Service Act drafted nearly three million men; African Americans served in segregated units, mostly as laborers, though the 369th Infantry fought with distinction under French command. General John J. Pershing insisted the American Expeditionary Forces fight as a separate army. U.S. troops arrived in force in 1918, helped stop Germany's spring offensives and fought the Meuse-Argonne offensive that autumn. Germany signed an armistice on November 11, 1918. About 116,000 Americans died, more than half from disease, much of it the influenza pandemic that killed some 675,000 people at home.

Wilson's **Fourteen Points** (January 1918) proposed a new kind of peace: open diplomacy, freedom of the seas, lower trade barriers, arms reduction, self-determination for the peoples of the Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman empires, and "a general association of nations." At Paris in 1919 Britain and France wanted security and compensation. Wilson accepted a war-guilt clause, reparations and colonial mandates in return for the **League of Nations**.

At home, Republicans controlled the Senate. Henry Cabot Lodge offered to ratify the treaty with **reservations**, above all to make clear that **Article X** could not commit the United States to war without a vote of Congress. A group of Irreconcilables opposed it in any form. Wilson refused to compromise, went on a national speaking tour and suffered a severe stroke in October 1919. He told Democrats to vote against the Lodge version; with neither version able to win two-thirds, the treaty failed. The United States never joined the League.

## The home front (7.6)

**Mobilizing the economy.** The war multiplied the federal government. The **War Industries Board** under Bernard Baruch set production priorities and prices in partnership with business; the Railroad Administration ran the railroads; Herbert Hoover's **Food Administration** urged "meatless" and "wheatless" days. The **National War Labor Board** pushed employers toward the eight-hour day and union recognition in return for no-strike pledges, and union membership grew. Taxes, including a steeply graduated income tax, and Liberty Bonds paid for it all.

**Shaping opinion and punishing dissent.** George Creel's **Committee on Public Information** sold the war with posters, films and the "Four-Minute Men." Suspicion of anything German followed: states banned German-language teaching, sauerkraut became "liberty cabbage", and a German-born miner, Robert Prager, was lynched in Illinois in 1918. The **Espionage Act** (1917) and **Sedition Act** (1918) led to some 2,000 prosecutions, mostly of socialists, pacifists and radical unionists; Eugene Debs received ten years for an antiwar speech. In *Schenck v. United States* (1919) Justice Holmes upheld such convictions, reasoning that speech creating "a clear and present danger" could be punished, especially in wartime.

**Migration.** War stopped European immigration just as factories needed workers. About half a million African Americans left the South between 1915 and 1920, pushed by Jim Crow, lynching and the boll weevil, pulled by jobs and encouraged by the *Chicago Defender*. Mexicans fleeing the Mexican Revolution moved north to southwestern farms and railroads. Migrants found work and more freedom, and also hostility: white mobs attacked Black neighbourhoods in East St. Louis (1917) and in more than two dozen cities during the "Red Summer" of 1919, including Chicago, where 38 people died.

**Women.** Women took jobs in munitions plants, offices and transport, and served overseas as nurses and telephone operators. Carrie Chapman Catt's National American Woman Suffrage Association supported the war and lobbied; Alice Paul's National Woman's Party picketed the White House, asking how the nation could fight for democracy abroad while denying it to women. Pickets were jailed and force-fed during hunger strikes. Wilson endorsed suffrage in 1918, and the **Nineteenth Amendment** was ratified in August 1920. The war also helped Prohibition: the **Eighteenth Amendment** was ratified in 1919.

**1919.** Peace brought turmoil. Inflation and the end of wartime labor controls produced some 3,600 strikes, among them the Seattle general strike, the Boston police strike and a national steel strike, which employers and newspapers called Bolshevik. Anarchist bombs, including one at Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer's home, set off the **Red Scare**: the **Palmer Raids** arrested thousands, mostly immigrants, without warrants, and hundreds were deported. The fear faded by late 1920 but fed the nativism of the next decade.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Woodrow Wilson, address to Congress, April 2, 1917**

> "The world must be made safe for democracy. Its peace must be planted upon the tested foundations of political liberty. We have no selfish ends to serve. We desire no conquest, no dominion."

*HIPP.* Historical situation: unrestricted submarine warfare, the Zimmermann Telegram, the fall of the tsar. Audience: Congress, the American public and the world. Purpose: to win a declaration of war from a nation that had re-elected him for keeping out of it, by presenting the war as a crusade rather than a defence of trade. *Why it matters:* it is the founding statement of "Wilsonian" internationalism, and it set a standard of idealism the peace failed to meet.

**2. *Schenck v. United States*, 1919**

> "The question in every case is whether the words used are used in such circumstances and are of such a nature as to create a clear and present danger that they will bring about the substantive evils that Congress has a right to prevent."

*HIPP.* A unanimous Court, written by Justice Holmes after the armistice, for a nation still gripped by fear of radicalism. *Why it matters:* it set the first major test for free speech; Holmes himself began to narrow it in a dissent later that year (*Abrams v. United States*).

**3. Woodrow Wilson, Fourteen Points, January 8, 1918**

> "A general association of nations must be formed under specific covenants for the purpose of affording mutual guarantees of political independence and territorial integrity to great and small states alike."

*HIPP.* Delivered to Congress but aimed at the world, partly to answer the Bolsheviks, who had published the Allies' secret treaties. *Why it matters:* it became the League, and the Senate's objection to these "mutual guarantees" defeated it.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: a causation chain.** *Explain why the United States entered the war in 1917 and not in 1915.* Build the chain: British blockade → U.S. trade and loans flow to the Allies → Germany relies on submarines → *Lusitania* (1915), but Wilson protests and Germany retreats (Sussex pledge) → Germany decides in 1917 that starving Britain is worth the risk of U.S. entry → unrestricted submarine warfare plus the Zimmermann Telegram plus the Russian Revolution → war. The answer to "why 1917" is Germany's decision, which changed the cost of neutrality.

**Worked example 2: an SAQ part.** *Briefly explain ONE way the First World War expanded the power of the federal government.* "The War Industries Board, led by Bernard Baruch, set production priorities and prices for American industry, so for the first time federal officials directed much of the national economy, a model later used by the New Deal's National Recovery Administration." One development, one specific, one consequence.

**Worked example 3: comparison across periods.** *Compare the Sedition Act of 1798 with the Sedition Act of 1918.* Similarity: both were passed during a war (the Quasi-War with France, the First World War) and punished criticism of the government, and both were used mainly against a political minority (Jeffersonian editors; socialists and radicals). Difference: the 1798 act expired in 1801 after a political backlash helped elect Jefferson; the 1918 act was upheld by the Supreme Court's reasoning in *Schenck* and *Debs* and was repealed in 1920 as the war ended. Lesson: wartime fear repeatedly narrows civil liberties; the remedy came through elections in 1800 and through slow judicial change after 1919.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The *Lusitania* brought the United States into the war."** It happened two years before U.S. entry. The trigger was unrestricted submarine warfare in 1917.
- **"The Senate rejected the treaty because Americans opposed all international involvement."** Lodge and most Republicans would have ratified it with reservations; Wilson's refusal to compromise was decisive.
- **"Women got the vote because of the war."** The war gave suffragists a powerful argument and a moment of leverage; decades of organizing and state victories, especially in the West, made it possible.
- **"Repression only targeted Germans."** Most prosecutions under the Espionage and Sedition Acts were of American socialists, pacifists and union radicals.
