## The Compromise of 1850

California's application to enter as a free state (week 14) threatened to give the free states a Senate majority. Henry Clay offered a package, Daniel Webster backed it as the price of preserving the Union, and John C. Calhoun, dying, demanded guarantees for the South. William Seward of New York said a higher law than the Constitution forbade slavery's extension. When Clay's single omnibus bill failed, Stephen A. Douglas passed each measure separately with shifting majorities.

What each side got: the **North** won a free California and the end of the slave trade (not slavery) in the District of Columbia. The **South** won a stronger Fugitive Slave Act and the chance that slavery might spread into New Mexico and Utah under popular sovereignty.

The compromise bought time but not peace, because of its most important part. The **Fugitive Slave Act** put federal power behind recapture. Commissioners heard cases without a jury, the accused could not testify, a commissioner earned $10 for sending a person south and $5 for releasing one, and bystanders could be commanded to help. Northern crowds rescued captives in Boston, Syracuse and Christiana, Pennsylvania; the federal government spent heavily to send Anthony Burns back to Virginia from Boston in 1854; Northern legislatures passed **personal liberty laws**. Harriet Beecher Stowe's *Uncle Tom's Cabin* (1852), written in anger at the law, sold about 300,000 copies in its first year in the United States. To white Southerners, Northern defiance proved that the North would not honour the Constitution's fugitive clause.

## Two societies: regional differences

By 1860 the North held about two-thirds of the population, most of the country's factories, railroad mileage and bank capital, and nearly all new immigrants. It was a society of family farms, growing cities and wage labor. The South had about four million enslaved people, almost a third of its population; it grew most of the world's cotton, and its wealth was concentrated in land and in enslaved people, the single largest financial asset in the national economy.

The sections were tied together (Northern banks and ships financed and carried cotton) and pulled apart by two ideologies:

- **Free labor** (Republicans): a man starts out working for wages, saves, and becomes an independent farmer or shopkeeper. Slavery degraded work and, if it spread west, would close the door of opportunity for white families. Most free-labor advocates opposed slavery's expansion, not slavery in the South, and many wanted the West for white settlers only.
- **The positive good** (Calhoun, George Fitzhugh): slavery was a benevolent, Bible-sanctioned order superior to Northern "wage slavery." James Henry Hammond's 1858 boast that cotton was king, and that no power would dare make war on it, expressed Southern confidence that the world could not do without it.

Each ideology defined the other section as a threat, and both believed the West would decide the nation's future.

## How compromise failed, 1854–1860

**The Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854).** Douglas wanted the Nebraska country organised so that a transcontinental railroad could run west from Chicago. To win Southern votes he applied popular sovereignty, which repealed the Missouri Compromise's ban on slavery north of 36°30'. The act declared its "true intent and meaning" was "not to legislate slavery into any Territory or State, nor to exclude it therefrom." Northerners saw a 34-year-old bargain broken. Three results followed:

1. **Party collapse.** The Whigs split by section and vanished. Antislavery Whigs, Free-Soil Democrats and many Know-Nothings formed the **Republican Party**, a purely Northern party. In 1856 its candidate, John C. Frémont, carried eleven free states.
2. **Bleeding Kansas.** Proslavery Missourians voted illegally and produced a proslavery legislature; free-state settlers formed their own. Proslavery forces sacked Lawrence in 1856; John Brown killed five proslavery settlers at Pottawatomie Creek. In 1857 a minority wrote the proslavery **Lecompton Constitution**; President Buchanan backed it, Douglas denounced it, and the Democratic Party began to divide.
3. **Violence in Congress.** Days after Charles Sumner's "Crime Against Kansas" speech, Representative Preston Brooks beat him unconscious at his Senate desk (May 1856).

**Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857).** Chief Justice Roger Taney ruled that Black people could not be citizens and that Congress had no power to bar slavery from the territories, so the Missouri Compromise had been unconstitutional all along. It adopted Calhoun's position and made the Republican platform unconstitutional.

**Lincoln and Douglas (1858).** In the Illinois Senate race, Lincoln warned that "this government cannot endure, permanently half slave and half free." At Freeport he asked how popular sovereignty survived Dred Scott; Douglas answered that settlers could keep slavery out by refusing to pass local laws protecting it. He won the seat but lost the South.

**John Brown (1859).** Brown's raid on the federal arsenal at Harpers Ferry, meant to arm enslaved people, failed; he was hanged. Some Northerners called him a martyr, and white Southerners concluded the North would welcome slave insurrection.

## The election of 1860 and secession

The Democrats split at their convention: Northern delegates nominated Douglas on popular sovereignty; Southern delegates nominated John C. Breckinridge on a demand for federal protection of slavery in all territories. Upper South moderates formed the Constitutional Union Party behind John Bell. The Republicans chose Lincoln, who promised to stop slavery's expansion and to leave it alone where it existed. Lincoln won 180 electoral votes with about 40% of the popular vote, all from free states.

South Carolina seceded on December 20, 1860. By February 1, 1861, Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana and Texas had followed; they formed the **Confederate States of America** at Montgomery under Jefferson Davis. The secession conventions explained themselves plainly: Northern hostility to slavery and the election of an antislavery president. The **Crittenden Compromise** failed because Lincoln would not accept the extension of slavery into the territories. In his inaugural address Lincoln repeated that he had no purpose to interfere with slavery in the states, but called the Union "perpetual." When he moved to resupply **Fort Sumter**, Confederate batteries opened fire on April 12, 1861. Lincoln called for 75,000 volunteers; Virginia, North Carolina, Tennessee and Arkansas seceded. The slave states of Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky and Missouri stayed in the Union.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Fugitive Slave Act (1850), section 6.**

> "In no trial or hearing under this act shall the testimony of such alleged fugitive be admitted in evidence ..."

*HIPP.* Written by Congress as the South's gain in the Compromise of 1850; its purpose was quick, certain recapture. Why it matters: denying the accused a voice turned an abstract constitutional clause into scenes on Northern streets.

**2. Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857), opinion of Chief Justice Taney.**

> "... so far inferior that they had no rights which the white man was bound to respect ..."

*HIPP.* Taney, a Marylander and former Jackson cabinet member, wrote for a Southern-majority Court, with Buchanan hoping a broad ruling would settle the territorial question. Why it matters: the attempt to end the debate radicalised it.

**3. Abraham Lincoln, House Divided speech (June 16, 1858).**

> "I believe this government cannot endure, permanently half slave and half free. I do not expect the Union to be dissolved — I do not expect the house to fall — but I do expect it will cease to be divided. It will become all one thing, or all the other."

*HIPP.* A Senate candidate addressing the Illinois Republican convention, separating his party from Douglas's popular sovereignty. Why it matters: Southern readers heard a threat to slavery itself.

**4. South Carolina, Declaration of the Immediate Causes (December 24, 1860).**

> "A geographical line has been drawn across the Union, and all the States north of that line have united in the election of a man to the high office of President of the United States, whose opinions and purposes are hostile to slavery."

*HIPP.* The secession convention addressed other slave states and the world to justify leaving. Why it matters: it states the cause of secession in its authors' own words, and that cause is the protection of slavery.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: reading the 1860 returns.** Lincoln had about 40% of the popular vote. Did he win only because his opponents were split? Check the arithmetic: he carried every free state but New Jersey (which he split), most with outright majorities, for 180 electoral votes, more than the 152 needed. Even against a single combined opponent he would have won the electoral college: the North alone could now elect a president.

**Worked example 2: turning a prompt into a thesis.** Prompt: *Evaluate the relative importance of the reasons compromise failed, 1850–1861.*

- Weak: "There were many reasons compromise failed, including the Fugitive Slave Act, Kansas and Dred Scott." (A list, no weighing.)
- Better: "The Kansas-Nebraska Act was the most important reason because it destroyed the Whig Party and created the Republicans."
- Strong: "Although the Fugitive Slave Act and Dred Scott inflamed opinion, the Kansas-Nebraska Act was the decisive failure, because by repealing the Missouri line it destroyed the last cross-sectional parties, leaving only a sectional Republican Party and a Democratic Party that split in 1860." (A claim, a weighing word, and a line of reasoning the essay can follow.)

**Worked example 3: an SAQ part (c) answered.** *Briefly explain one way the purpose of South Carolina's declaration shapes its argument.* "The convention wanted to convince other slave states that secession was a lawful defence of constitutional rights, not a revolution, so it stresses Northern violations of 'obligations', such as the fugitive slave clause, and the threat a sectional president posed to slavery." One specific feature of the source, linked to one feature of the argument.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The South seceded over states' rights in general."** The secession documents name slavery, and Southern leaders demanded more federal power (the Fugitive Slave Act, a territorial slave code) when it protected slavery.
- **"Lincoln ran on abolition."** He ran on stopping slavery's expansion and promised not to interfere where it existed.
- **"Republicans favoured racial equality."** Most opposed slavery's spread to protect free white labor; few supported equal rights for Black Americans in 1860.
- **"All the slave states seceded."** Four border slave states stayed in the Union.
