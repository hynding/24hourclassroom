In plain language: the Constitution was a blueprint, and the 1790s were the building. Within a decade Americans argued over what the blueprint meant, split into parties, nearly went to war with both Britain and France, fought a hard war in the Ohio country, and then, in 1800, handed power peacefully to the opposition.

## Building the government and the first parties (3.10)

Washington took office in 1789 with no precedents. Congress created executive departments, and Washington began meeting their heads as a **cabinet**: Jefferson at State, Hamilton at the Treasury, Knox at War, Randolph as Attorney General. The **Judiciary Act of 1789** built the federal courts. The First Congress proposed the Bill of Rights.

**Hamilton's program.** Hamilton believed the republic's survival depended on credit and on the loyalty of the wealthy. He proposed to **fund** the national debt at full value, **assume** the states' war debts, charter a **Bank of the United States**, and pay for it all with tariffs and an **excise tax on whiskey**; his *Report on Manufactures* (1791) also urged support for industry. Madison objected that paying speculators in full rewarded those who had bought soldiers' certificates cheaply, and Virginia, which had paid much of its debt, objected to assumption. The **Compromise of 1790** traded southern votes on assumption for a permanent capital on the Potomac.

The bank raised a constitutional question. Jefferson argued for **strict construction**: the Tenth Amendment reserves undelegated powers, and a bank is not "necessary". Hamilton argued for **loose construction**: the necessary and proper clause implies power to use any reasonable means toward an enumerated end. Washington sided with Hamilton.

**Foreign policy deepens the split.** When revolutionary France went to war with Britain in 1793, Washington issued a **Neutrality Proclamation** despite the 1778 alliance. The French minister **Edmond Genêt** tried to recruit Americans for France and was recalled. British seizures of American ships nearly caused war until **John Jay** negotiated a treaty (1794, ratified 1795): Britain would leave its northwest forts, but impressment and ship seizures went unresolved. Republicans called it a betrayal; Federalists called it the price of peace. **Pinckney's Treaty** with Spain (1795), opening the Mississippi, was popular with everyone.

By the mid-1790s two parties, unforeseen in the Constitution, had newspapers, candidates and loyal voters. **Federalists**, strongest in New England and among merchants and creditors, favoured a strong national government, commerce and friendship with Britain. **Democratic-Republicans**, strongest in the South and West, favoured states' rights, an agrarian republic and sympathy for France.

**Order.** In 1794 western Pennsylvania farmers attacked tax collectors in the **Whiskey Rebellion**. Washington called out about 13,000 militia, and the rebellion collapsed, a sharp contrast with the Confederation's helplessness during Shays' Rebellion.

**Washington's farewell and Adams's crisis.** Declining a third term, Washington published his **Farewell Address** (1796), warning against permanent foreign alliances and the "spirit of party". John Adams narrowly defeated Jefferson, who became vice president. When French officials demanded a bribe from American diplomats (the **XYZ Affair**), an undeclared naval **Quasi-War** with France followed. Federalists passed the **Alien and Sedition Acts** (1798): a longer naturalisation period, presidential power to deport aliens, and a crime of publishing "false, scandalous and malicious" writing against the government. About ten Republican editors and printers were convicted. Jefferson and Madison answered with the **Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions**, arguing that the states, as parties to a compact, could judge whether the federal government had exceeded its powers. Adams, to his party's fury, made peace with France in 1800. That year Jefferson won the presidency (after the House broke an electoral tie with Aaron Burr), and the Federalists handed over power peacefully.

## An American identity (3.11)

Independence raised a new question: what made someone an American? Writers answered in different ways. The French-born farmer **J. Hector St. John de Crèvecoeur** described the American as a "new man" formed by the mixing of European peoples and the rewards of free labour. **Noah Webster**'s speller promoted distinct American spellings and a common national language. Painters, poets and historians celebrated the Revolution and Washington.

Women's place in the republic was redefined through **Republican Motherhood**: mothers served the nation by raising virtuous sons. The idea kept women out of formal politics (coverture remained, and New Jersey's brief exception ended in 1807) but justified female academies and better education.

African Americans claimed the Revolution's principles. In 1791 the free Black mathematician **Benjamin Banneker** sent Thomas Jefferson his almanac and challenged him to reconcile the Declaration with slavery. In Philadelphia, **Richard Allen** and **Absalom Jones** founded independent Black churches, the beginnings of the African Methodist Episcopal Church.

**Regional identities** remained strong: New England commercial, Congregationalist and Federalist; the South agrarian, slaveholding and increasingly Republican; the West suspicious of eastern elites and focused on land and the Mississippi.

## Movement in the early republic (3.12)

**Into the West.** Settlers crossed the Appalachians in large numbers. Kentucky became a state in 1792 and Tennessee in 1796. Westerners needed three things from the government: access to the Mississippi (secured by Pinckney's Treaty), protection from Native resistance, and land.

**War in the Ohio country.** Native nations north of the Ohio had never accepted the Treaty of Paris. A **Western Confederacy** led by **Little Turtle** (Miami) and **Blue Jacket** (Shawnee) defeated General Josiah Harmar in 1790 and destroyed Arthur St. Clair's army in 1791. Congress funded a professional army under **Anthony Wayne**, which won at **Fallen Timbers** in 1794; British troops at a nearby fort refused to help. The **Treaty of Greenville** (1795) ceded most of present-day Ohio in return for annual payments. The pattern of war, defeat and cession would repeat for decades.

**Slavery moves too.** Some founders expected slavery to fade. Instead, Eli Whitney's **cotton gin** (1793) made short-staple cotton, which grows across the southern interior, highly profitable. Enslaved people were forced to move with cotton into the Georgia and Carolina upcountry and, soon, the Old Southwest. Meanwhile, northern gradual emancipation and southern manumissions created growing free Black communities in cities such as Philadelphia and Baltimore. Refugees from the Haitian Revolution, white and Black, also arrived in American ports in the 1790s.

## Continuity and change in Period 3 (3.13)

| | 1754 | 1800 |
|---|---|---|
| Political status | Subjects of the British crown | Citizens of an independent republic |
| Government | Colonial assemblies under royal governors and Parliament | A written Constitution, Bill of Rights and federal system |
| Politics | Factions within colonies | Two national parties; peaceful transfer of power |
| Foreign relations | Part of the British Empire against France | Independent; neutral between Britain and France |
| American Indians | Play-off diplomacy between rival empires | Facing one expanding republic; land lost by war and treaty |
| Slavery | Legal in all colonies | Ending gradually in the North; expanding in the South |
| Women | Coverture; no political rights | Coverture; no vote; new civic role as republican mothers |

**Changes:** independence; republican government; written constitutions and rights; parties. **Continuities:** slavery in the South; the exclusion of women from politics; settler pressure on Native land; debate over central versus local power, which ran from the Stamp Act to the Kentucky Resolutions.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Jefferson and Hamilton on the bank, February 1791** (Avalon Project)

> "To take a single step beyond the boundaries thus specially drawn around the powers of Congress, is to take possession of a boundless field of power, no longer susceptible of any definition." (Jefferson)

> ". . . every power vested in a government is in its nature sovereign, and includes, by force of the term, a right to employ all the means requisite and fairly applicable to the attainment of the ends of such power . . ." (Hamilton)

*HIPP.* Two cabinet secretaries writing for one reader, the president, who had to decide whether to sign the bank bill. Each wrote as an advocate for his own vision: Jefferson's agrarian, state-centred republic and Hamilton's commercial nation. Why it matters: the origin of strict and loose construction.

**2. George Washington, Farewell Address, 1796** (Avalon Project)

> "The great rule of conduct for us in regard to foreign nations is in extending our commercial relations, to have with them as little political connection as possible."

*HIPP.* Published in a Philadelphia newspaper, not delivered as a speech, near the end of a second term scarred by party conflict and the Jay's Treaty fight. Audience: the American public, and future leaders. Purpose: to guide the nation and defend his record. Why it matters: the foundation of a long tradition of avoiding permanent alliances.

**3. Crèvecoeur, Letters from an American Farmer, 1782** (Project Gutenberg)

> "What then is the American, this new man? He is either an European, or the descendant of an European, hence that strange mixture of blood, which you will find in no other country."

*HIPP.* A French-born immigrant writing for European readers, published in London. Purpose: to portray America as a land of opportunity. Why it matters: an early, influential definition of American identity, and a revealing one, since it leaves out Africans and American Indians.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: comparing two sources.** On the bank opinions, a comparison answer should name the shared question, then the difference, then the reason. *Both men answer whether Congress may charter a bank though the Constitution does not mention one. Jefferson says no, because powers not delegated are reserved; Hamilton says yes, because any sovereign power includes the means to achieve its ends. Their difference reflects rival visions: Jefferson feared a government serving northern finance; Hamilton thought a strong government and sound credit were the republic's best protection.*

**Worked example 2: sourcing the Farewell Address.** Weak: "Washington wanted to give advice." Strong: "Because Washington wrote after two years of fierce conflict over Jay's Treaty, in which Republicans accused him of favouring Britain, his warning against 'the spirit of party' and against foreign attachments served partly to defend his own neutral policy against his critics." The historical situation explains the content.

**Worked example 3: a continuity-and-change thesis.** Prompt: *Evaluate the extent to which American political life changed from 1754 to 1800.* Thesis: "American political life changed fundamentally as colonists became citizens of a republic with written constitutions, a bill of rights and competing parties that transferred power peacefully; yet the central conflict of the period, over how much power a distant central authority should hold over local communities, continued from the Stamp Act crisis to the Kentucky Resolutions."

## Common misconceptions

- **"The Constitution created political parties."** It did not mention them; the framers feared them.
- **"The Sedition Act was struck down by the Supreme Court."** The Court never ruled on it; it expired in 1801 and Jefferson pardoned those convicted.
- **"Jay's Treaty was a triumph."** It avoided war but left impressment unresolved and was bitterly divisive.
- **"Slavery was dying out by 1800."** The cotton gin did the opposite.
