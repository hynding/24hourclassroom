## Industrial work and the labor movement

Industrialization made most American workers wage earners who owned no tools, set none of the terms of their work and could be fired at will. Ten- or twelve-hour days and six-day weeks were normal; mines, mills and railroads killed and maimed tens of thousands a year with no compensation; wages fell in every depression. Mechanization broke skilled crafts into repetitive tasks that newcomers could learn quickly. Women worked in garment shops, textile mills and domestic service and increasingly as clerks; children worked in Southern mills, coal-mine breakers and city streets.

Workers organized. The **National Labor Union** (1866) pressed for the eight-hour day. The **Knights of Labor** (1869), led from 1879 by Terence Powderly, admitted skilled and unskilled workers, women and African Americans (but backed Chinese exclusion), and imagined cooperatives replacing the wage system; it grew to about 700,000 members by 1886. The **American Federation of Labor** (1886), led by Samuel Gompers, took a narrower path: a federation of skilled craft unions bargaining for higher wages, shorter hours and better conditions, accepting capitalism.

The great strikes show how the contest usually ended:

- **1877.** After repeated wage cuts, railroad workers struck across the country; President Hayes sent federal troops, and about 100 people died.
- **1886.** At Chicago's **Haymarket Square**, a bomb killed police at a rally for the eight-hour day. Eight anarchists were convicted with little evidence and four hanged. The public linked unions with violent radicalism; the Knights collapsed.
- **1892.** At Carnegie's **Homestead** works, manager Henry Clay Frick locked out the union; a battle with Pinkerton guards killed several on both sides, the state militia arrived, and the union was broken.
- **1894.** When the Pullman company cut wages but not rents in its company town, Eugene Debs's American Railway Union boycotted Pullman cars and stopped western rail traffic. A federal injunction and federal troops broke the strike; the Supreme Court upheld the injunction in *In re Debs* (1895). Debs, jailed, became a socialist.

Employers held most of the weapons: lockouts, blacklists, yellow-dog contracts, strikebreakers, private guards, and courts and governments that treated strikes as threats to order and property. Even in 1900 only a small fraction of American workers, well under one in ten, belonged to a union.

## The new immigration and the growth of cities

Between 1880 and 1920 more than 20 million immigrants arrived. Earlier arrivals had come mostly from Ireland, Germany, Britain and Scandinavia. The **new immigrants** came mostly from Italy, Austria-Hungary, Poland and the Russian Empire, many of them Catholic, Orthodox Christian or Jewish. Rural poverty and population growth pushed them; Jews also fled pogroms after 1881. Industrial jobs, cheap steamship passage and letters from relatives pulled them. On the West Coast, Chinese and then Japanese immigrants arrived; in the Southwest, Mexican migration grew toward the end of the century. Many immigrants, especially Italian and Greek men, planned to work a few years and return home, and many did.

Americans were on the move too. Farm children and African Americans from the rural South headed to cities for wages and schools. The urban share of the population rose from about a quarter in 1870 to about two-fifths in 1900. Chicago grew from about 300,000 people to about 1.7 million in thirty years. New technologies remade cities: electric streetcars let the middle class move to new suburbs; steel frames and elevators raised skyscrapers downtown. Meanwhile the poor crowded into tenements without light, air or plumbing, where disease spread fast.

Immigrants built their own worlds: ethnic neighborhoods, parishes and synagogues, mutual-aid and burial societies, foreign-language newspapers and fraternal lodges that helped newcomers find jobs and housing.

## Responses: machines, reformers and nativists

**Political machines** offered what government did not. In New York, Tammany Hall's ward bosses found immigrants jobs on city payrolls, brought coal in winter and helped with naturalization and the courts, in return for votes. Machines paid for this with graft from contracts and franchises. William 'Boss' Tweed's ring stole tens of millions of dollars before Thomas Nast's cartoons and *The New York Times* helped bring him down in the early 1870s; the machine outlived him.

**Reformers** responded differently. Settlement houses such as Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr's **Hull House** (Chicago, 1889) offered classes, child care and clubs while their residents studied neighborhood conditions and lobbied for laws. Jacob Riis's photographs and text in *How the Other Half Lives* (1890) brought tenement life to middle-class readers. Many reformers also promoted **Americanization**, teaching English and civics but often pressing immigrants to give up their own cultures.

**Nativists** wanted fewer newcomers. In California, Denis Kearney's Workingmen's Party campaigned against Chinese workers, and in 1885 white miners massacred Chinese miners at Rock Springs, Wyoming. Congress passed the **Chinese Exclusion Act** (1882), the first federal law to bar a group by nationality, and in the same year a general law barring convicts and those 'likely to become a public charge'. The anti-Catholic **American Protective Association** (1887) and the **Immigration Restriction League** (1894), which demanded a literacy test, aimed at Europeans. Nativists drew on the anti-Catholicism of the Know-Nothings of the 1850s and on new racial theories that ranked Europeans by 'stock'.

## The middle class and the culture of consumption

Corporations and cities created a new **middle class** of salaried managers, clerks, salesmen, engineers, teachers and professionals. Its members lived in streetcar suburbs, shopped in department stores such as Macy's and Marshall Field's or from Montgomery Ward catalogs, and spent leisure time at baseball games (the National League dates from 1876), vaudeville theaters, amusement parks and city parks such as Olmsted's Central Park. Middle-class women gained new opportunities: typing and office jobs, women's colleges (Vassar, Wellesley, Smith, Bryn Mawr) and women's clubs, while the Victorian ideal still assigned them the home. At the top, the very rich built mansions and displayed what Thorstein Veblen called **conspicuous consumption**; Mark Twain and Charles Dudley Warner's novel *The Gilded Age* (1873) gave the period its name: glittering on the surface, base metal underneath.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Chinese Exclusion Act (1882).**

> "Whereas in the opinion of the Government of the United States the coming of Chinese laborers to this country endangers the good order of certain localities within the territory thereof ..." Section 14: "That hereafter no State court or court of the United States shall admit Chinese to citizenship ..."

*Sourcing:* Congress, responding to Western voters and to both parties' competition for them after the depression of the 1870s. Purpose: to stop Chinese labor migration and keep Chinese immigrants permanently outside the political community. Note the vague justification, 'good order of certain localities'. *Why it matters:* the start of federal immigration restriction by race and nationality.

**2. Emma Lazarus, "The New Colossus" (1883).**

> "Give me your tired, your poor, / Your huddled masses yearning to breathe free, / The wretched refuse of your teeming shore."

*Sourcing:* a sonnet by a New York Jewish poet, written for an auction to fund the Statue of Liberty's pedestal, the year after Chinese exclusion and as Russian Jewish refugees were arriving. Purpose: to redefine the statue as a welcome to immigrants. *Why it matters:* it was little noticed at first and placed on the pedestal only in 1903; set beside the Exclusion Act, it shows two American answers to the same question.

**3. Jacob Riis, *How the Other Half Lives* (1890).**

> "Long ago it was said that 'one half of the world does not know how the other half lives.' That was true then. It did not know because it did not care."

*Sourcing:* a Danish immigrant turned police reporter, writing for middle-class readers with photographs taken using flash powder. Point of view: sympathetic to the poor, but often stereotyping the ethnic groups he described. *Why it matters:* it made housing a public issue and modeled the investigative journalism of the Progressive muckrakers.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: comparing the Knights and the AFL.**
Similarity: both sought the eight-hour day and better pay, and both backed Chinese exclusion. Difference: the Knights organized nearly everyone and wanted to replace wage labor with cooperatives; the AFL organized skilled crafts and wanted a bigger share within capitalism. Explanation: after Haymarket and the defeat of broad strikes, the AFL's narrow strategy survived because skilled workers were hard to replace, which gave them bargaining power that unskilled workers lacked.

**Worked example 2: a continuity-and-change paragraph.**
"Nativism in the 1880s and 1890s continued patterns from the 1850s, as anti-Catholic organizations such as the American Protective Association repeated the Know-Nothings' charges that Catholic immigrants were loyal to the pope. But it also changed: Congress, not just a political party, acted, and the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 introduced race and nationality as grounds for barring immigrants, a precedent for the quota laws of the 1920s."

**Worked example 3: sourcing Riis for an SAQ.**
Historical situation: New York's population was exploding with immigrants in the 1880s. Audience: middle-class readers. Purpose: reform of tenement housing. Point of view: a reformer's compassion mixed with ethnic stereotypes. A sentence that uses this: "Because Riis wrote to persuade comfortable readers, he emphasized squalor and danger, which made his book effective as reform but less reliable as a picture of immigrants' own community life."

## Common misconceptions

- **"Unions were strong in the Gilded Age."** Membership was small, and most major strikes were lost.
- **"All immigrants came through Ellis Island."** It opened only in 1892; Asian immigrants arrived on the West Coast and Mexicans across the southern border.
- **"Machines were just corruption."** They also delivered services; explain both sides.
- **"The middle class was the same as the rich."** It was salaried, suburban and often reform-minded, and distinct from both the very rich and wage workers.
- **"Immigrants were passive victims."** They built churches, unions, newspapers and mutual-aid societies, and many chose to return home.
