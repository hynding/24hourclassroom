## Expanding democracy, and its limits

Between 1800 and 1840 the United States became the first country where nearly all adult white men could vote. New western states entered without property requirements, and older states followed in conventions, such as New York in 1821. More offices became elective, and by 1832 every state but South Carolina chose presidential electors by popular vote. Turnout climbed from roughly a quarter of eligible voters in 1824 to about four in five in 1840.

**Why?** Western states competed for settlers; the market revolution produced wage earners without land who demanded a voice; and politicians found that organising new voters won elections.

**Who was left out?** The same conventions that widened white men's suffrage often narrowed Black men's. New Jersey ended voting by women and free Black men in 1807; New York in 1821 removed the property test for white men but set a $250 property requirement for Black men; Pennsylvania's 1838 constitution limited voting to white men. Women could not vote anywhere. Democracy in this era was explicitly white and male, and for many white Americans whiteness itself became the qualification for citizenship.

**Parties.** The **election of 1824** produced four candidates and no electoral majority. The House chose John Quincy Adams over Jackson, who had led the popular vote; when Adams named Henry Clay secretary of state, Jacksonians cried **corrupt bargain**. Martin Van Buren built a disciplined national **Democratic Party** around Jackson, who won in 1828. Parties now had conventions (from 1831–1832), newspapers, rallies and patronage (the **spoils system**). Jackson's opponents formed the **Whigs** in 1833–1834. In 1840 the Whigs beat the Democrats at their own game, running William Henry Harrison as a log-cabin candidate and driving turnout to its peak.

## Jackson and federal power

Jackson's presidency did not fit a simple "strong government" or "weak government" label. He strengthened the **presidency** and defended the **Union**, but he cut back the federal role in the **economy** and sided with **states** against Native nations.

**The Bank War.** Jackson saw the Second Bank as a privileged, unconstitutional monopoly controlled by eastern and foreign investors. When Clay and the bank's president, Nicholas Biddle, pushed for early recharter in 1832 to make it an election issue, Jackson vetoed it. His message attacked laws that "make the rich richer and the potent more powerful," spoke for "the farmers, mechanics, and laborers," and insisted that the president could judge constitutionality for himself, despite McCulloch. He won re-election, moved federal deposits to state "pet banks" in 1833 and let the bank's charter expire in 1836. Without its restraint, state banks lent freely; the **Specie Circular** (1836) and falling cotton prices helped bring the **Panic of 1837**.

**Nullification.** Southern planters exported cotton and bought manufactured goods, so the high **Tariff of 1828** felt like a tax on them. Vice President **John C. Calhoun** secretly wrote the South Carolina Exposition and Protest, arguing that a state convention could nullify an unconstitutional federal law, building on the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions. In 1832 South Carolina did so. Jackson, himself a southern slaveholder and states'-rights man, called nullification "incompatible with the existence of the Union" and obtained a **Force Bill**; Clay's **Compromise Tariff** of 1833 lowered rates over ten years. South Carolina repealed its ordinance, but the theory, and the fear behind it that federal power might one day touch slavery, survived.

**Indian removal.** Most white southerners and westerners wanted the lands of the Cherokee, Creek, Choctaw, Chickasaw and Seminole, and Georgia extended its laws over Cherokee territory after gold was found there in 1829. Jackson, an old Indian fighter, called removal "benevolent." The **Indian Removal Act** (1830) passed narrowly over strong opposition from northern reformers and many National Republicans. The Cherokee had adopted a written constitution (1827), a syllabary created by Sequoyah and a newspaper, and they went to court. In **Worcester v. Georgia** (1832) Marshall ruled that Georgia's laws had "no force" in their territory. Jackson did not enforce it. A minority faction signed the **Treaty of New Echota** (1835); Principal Chief John Ross and most Cherokees rejected it, but in 1838 the army forced about 16,000 people west on the **Trail of Tears**, and roughly 4,000 died. The Seminoles fought the Second Seminole War (1835–1842). Removal was federal power used to enlarge state power and slavery's domain.

## An American culture

Americans wanted a culture to match their political independence. **Romantic** writers celebrated emotion, nature and the national past: Washington Irving's Hudson Valley tales, James Fenimore Cooper's frontier novels, and later Hawthorne, Melville and Poe. Noah Webster's *American Dictionary* (1828) gave the nation its own spelling. **Thomas Cole** and the **Hudson River School** painted American wilderness as sublime, and sometimes as threatened by settlement.

**Transcendentalism** was the most radical expression. Ralph Waldo Emerson told Harvard's scholars in 1837 that "We have listened too long to the courtly muses of Europe" and urged self-reliance and truth found through nature and intuition. Henry David Thoreau lived at Walden Pond (1845–1847) and went to jail rather than pay a tax supporting slavery and the Mexican War; Margaret Fuller argued for women's equality. Transcendentalism's faith in the individual fed reform movements (week 13).

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Andrew Jackson, Bank Veto, July 10, 1832**

> "... when the laws undertake to add to these natural and just advantages artificial distinctions, to grant titles, gratuities, and exclusive privileges, to make the rich richer and the potent more powerful, the humble members of society—the farmers, mechanics, and laborers—who have neither the time nor the means of securing like favors to themselves, have a right to complain of the injustice of their Government."

*HIPP.* Situation: recharter forced four months before an election. Audience: voters more than Congress. Purpose: to frame the election as the people against privilege. Point of view: a westerner who distrusted banks since the Panic of 1819. **Why it matters:** it is the classic statement of Jacksonian political economy and of the president as tribune of the people.

**2. Alexis de Tocqueville, Democracy in America (1835)**

> "Amongst the novel objects that attracted my attention during my stay in the United States, nothing struck me more forcibly than the general equality of conditions."

*HIPP.* A French aristocrat writing for French readers about democracy's future in Europe. **Why it matters:** an outsider's comparison explains what was new about American society, but he wrote just as wealth inequality in northern cities was growing, and his volume also devotes long chapters to slavery and to the fate of Native Americans.

**3. Worcester v. Georgia (1832)**

> "The Cherokee nation, then, is a distinct community, occupying its own territory, with boundaries accurately described, in which the laws of Georgia can have no force ..."

*HIPP.* The Court's last great assertion of federal treaty power under Marshall, against a defiant state and an unwilling president. **Why it matters:** it shows the limits of judicial power without executive support.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: sourcing the Bank Veto.** An AP sourcing sentence explains how one HIPP element affects the document's argument, not just what it is:

> Because Jackson wrote the veto message in July 1832, after his opponents had forced the bank question into that year's presidential campaign, he aimed it at the newly enfranchised voters of the Democratic coalition, which explains why he framed a technical question of banking as a struggle of "farmers, mechanics, and laborers" against "the rich and powerful."

**Worked example 2: a comparison of the parties.**

| | Democrats | Whigs |
|---|---|---|
| Federal role in economy | Limited; no national bank; hard money | Active: bank, tariff, internal improvements |
| Presidency | Strong executive as the people's voice | Strong Congress; feared "King Andrew" |
| Moral reform | Government should not legislate morality | Supported temperance, public schools, Sabbath laws |
| Expansion | Favoured, especially Texas | Wary, especially in the North |
| Base | Small farmers, urban workers, immigrants, much of the South | Merchants, manufacturers, evangelicals, commercial farmers |

**Similarity:** both were national parties with northern and southern wings, which is why both avoided slavery as long as they could. That shared avoidance is a strong complexity point.

**Worked example 3: an SAQ on removal.** "(b) Briefly explain ONE reason the Worcester decision did not stop removal." *Answer:* The Supreme Court had no way to enforce its ruling, and President Jackson, who supported removal and had Georgia's backing, refused to act; the federal government instead obtained the Treaty of New Echota (1835) from a minority faction and used the army in 1838. One accurate claim, one specific supporting detail.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Jacksonian democracy gave everyone the vote."** It widened white men's suffrage while Black men lost ground and women and Native Americans were excluded.
- **"Jackson was a states'-rights president, full stop."** He crushed nullification and expanded presidential power, but backed Georgia against the Cherokee.
- **"Jackson said 'John Marshall has made his decision, now let him enforce it.'"** That line is attributed to him decades later and is not reliably documented; what is documented is that he did not enforce Worcester.
- **"The Trail of Tears was only the Cherokee."** Choctaw, Creek, Chickasaw and Seminole removals were also deadly.
- **"The Whigs were the successors of the Federalists."** They inherited Clay's nationalism but were a mass democratic party that campaigned like the Democrats.
