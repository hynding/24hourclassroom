## What you need to be able to do

- **7.7** Explain how new technologies of production and communication (the assembly line, electricity, radio, film) and consumer credit created a mass consumer economy and culture, and who was left out of the prosperity.
- **7.8** Explain the causes and effects of the decade's cultural and political conflicts: over immigration, religion and science, Prohibition, race and the roles of women, along with the conservative politics of "normalcy".

In plain language: the 1920s were the first decade that looks like modern America (cars, radio, movies, advertising, credit) and also one of the most anxious, as many Americans used law to defend an older nation against that modern one.

## The business of America: politics and economics (7.7)

Warren Harding won the presidency in 1920 with over 60 percent of the popular vote by promising "not heroics but healing; not nostrums but normalcy." The decade's three Republican presidents, Harding, Calvin Coolidge and Herbert Hoover, and Treasury Secretary Andrew Mellon (who served all three) believed that government should help business grow and otherwise step back. Mellon cut income taxes, above all on the highest incomes; the Fordney–McCumber Tariff (1922) raised import duties; regulators such as the Federal Trade Commission became partners of the industries they oversaw. Harding's administration was riddled with scandal (Teapot Dome sent Interior Secretary Albert Fall to prison), but Coolidge's personal honesty restored confidence. In 1925 he told newspaper editors that "the chief business of the American people is business."

The boom was real. After a sharp postwar recession, output and many workers' incomes rose. The engine was the **automobile**. Henry Ford's moving assembly line cut the time to build a Model T from about twelve hours to about ninety minutes, and its price fell below $300; by 1929 there were over 20 million cars on American roads. Cars consumed steel, glass, rubber and oil, created a road-building boom and gas stations, and let suburbs spread beyond streetcar lines.

**Electricity** reached most urban homes, creating a market for vacuum cleaners, washing machines and refrigerators. **Installment credit** let families buy them before saving the price, and **advertising** became a major industry, teaching consumers to want new products. The result was a mass consumer economy, though one with weak points: income was increasingly concentrated, much buying depended on debt, and the stock market became a speculative bubble.

The prosperity was uneven. **Farmers** suffered all decade: wartime demand ended, prices collapsed and debts mounted. Coolidge twice vetoed the McNary–Haugen bill to have the government buy surpluses. Coal miners and textile workers faced wage cuts, and union membership fell as employers promoted company unions and the open shop. Most farm families had no electricity.

## A national mass culture (7.7)

**Radio** began commercially when Pittsburgh's KDKA broadcast the 1920 election returns. By the late 1920s, networks carried the same programmes, sports and advertisements into millions of homes. **Movies** drew tens of millions of viewers a week; *The Jazz Singer* (1927) brought synchronised sound. Celebrities such as Babe Ruth and Charles Lindbergh, who flew alone across the Atlantic in 1927, became national heroes. For the first time Americans across regions shared the same entertainment, slang and consumer desires, and traditionalists worried that this national culture was eroding local, religious and family authority.

## A nation divided over its identity (7.8)

**Immigration.** The nativism of 1919 became law. The Emergency Quota Act (1921) and the **Immigration Act of 1924** set quotas by national origin, based on the census of 1890, before most Italians, Poles and Jews arrived. Asians "ineligible for citizenship" were barred completely, completing the exclusion that began with the Chinese in 1882. The Western Hemisphere was exempt, so Mexican immigration continued, while the new Border Patrol (1924) policed the line. The case of **Sacco and Vanzetti**, Italian anarchists executed in 1927 after a trial many considered unfair, became a symbol of the decade's nativism.

**The Klan.** Revived in 1915, the second **Ku Klux Klan** preached "100% Americanism" against African Americans, Catholics, Jews and immigrants. It claimed millions of members by the mid-1920s, was powerful in Indiana and Oregon as well as the South, and marched in Washington in 1925, then collapsed after scandals.

**Religion and science.** Fundamentalists, strongest in the rural South and Midwest, saw Darwinian evolution as a threat to faith and morality. Tennessee's **Butler Act** (1925) made it unlawful to teach "that man has descended from a lower order of animals." The ACLU recruited John Scopes to test it. At the **Scopes trial** Clarence Darrow questioned William Jennings Bryan about the Bible; Scopes was convicted and fined, but newspapers, especially H. L. Mencken's, presented fundamentalism as backward to urban readers.

**Prohibition.** The Eighteenth Amendment took effect in 1920. Alcohol consumption fell, but speakeasies, bootlegging and gangsters such as Al Capone flourished, and enforcement was weak in cities. "Wet" and "dry" divided the Democratic Party, and Al Smith, the wet, Catholic, urban Democrat of 1928, lost to Hoover amid anti-Catholic prejudice. Repeal came in 1933.

**Race and the Harlem Renaissance.** The Great Migration built large Black communities in northern cities. In Harlem, writers such as Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston, Claude McKay and Alain Locke (*The New Negro*, 1925) and musicians such as Duke Ellington and Louis Armstrong asserted a confident African American culture. Hughes declared in 1926: "We younger Negro artists who create now intend to express our individual dark-skinned selves without fear or shame." Marcus Garvey's **UNIA** drew mass support for Black pride, Black business and a return to Africa, while the NAACP fought lynching (the Dyer bill passed the House in 1922 but died in a Senate filibuster) and segregation in court.

**Women.** The Nineteenth Amendment did not produce a unified women's voting bloc, but fear that it might helped pass the Sheppard–Towner Act (1921) for maternal and infant health. Alice Paul's Equal Rights Amendment (1923) divided women reformers who wanted to keep protective labor laws. The flapper challenged older standards, and more women worked as clerks, typists and telephone operators, but most married women stayed out of paid work and pay remained unequal.

**American Indians.** The Indian Citizenship Act (1924) made all Native Americans born in the United States citizens, though several states continued to deny them the vote.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Calvin Coolidge, address to the American Society of Newspaper Editors, January 17, 1925**

> "After all, the chief business of the American people is business. They are profoundly concerned with producing, buying, selling, investing and prospering in the world."

*HIPP.* A president at the height of the boom, speaking to editors about the press's role. Purpose: to argue that newspapers, like Americans, were rightly devoted to commerce. Note what is usually left out: Coolidge added that wealth "can not be justified as the chief end of existence." *Why it matters:* the line is the decade's slogan, and the misquotation ("the business of America is business") shows how sources get flattened.

**2. Tennessee, Butler Act, 1925**

> "That it shall be unlawful for any teacher in any of the Universities, Normals and all other public schools of the State ... to teach any theory that denies the story of the Divine Creation of man as taught in the Bible, and to teach instead that man has descended from a lower order of animals."

*HIPP.* A state legislature, rural and Protestant in its base, responding to the spread of high schools and modern biology textbooks. *Why it matters:* it turned a cultural anxiety into criminal law and produced the decade's most famous trial.

**3. Langston Hughes, "The Negro Artist and the Racial Mountain," *The Nation*, 1926**

> "If white people are pleased we are glad. If they are not, it doesn't matter."

*HIPP.* A young poet writing in a liberal, mostly white magazine, answering a Black critic who had argued that Black art should not be different from white art. *Why it matters:* a manifesto of artistic independence and racial pride.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: an SAQ on the Scopes trial.** *(a) Briefly describe ONE cause of the Scopes trial.* "Fundamentalist Protestants, alarmed that public high schools were teaching Darwinian evolution, persuaded Tennessee's legislature to pass the Butler Act in 1925, and the ACLU recruited John Scopes to test the law." *(b) Briefly explain ONE way the trial reflected broader conflicts of the 1920s.* "It pitted rural, religious traditionalists, represented by William Jennings Bryan, against urban modernists, represented by Clarence Darrow and the national press, the same fault line visible in the debates over Prohibition and immigration."

**Worked example 2: comparing three Black responses.** Du Bois and the NAACP: integration and equal rights through courts, lobbying and *The Crisis*. Garvey and the UNIA: racial pride through separation, Black business and an African homeland. Hughes and the Harlem Renaissance: racial pride through art that did not seek white approval. All three drew on the confidence of the new urban communities; they differed on whether the goal was inclusion in American society or independence from it.

**Worked example 3: a continuity-and-change thesis.** Prompt: *Evaluate the extent to which immigration policy changed between 1882 and 1924.* Thesis: "Immigration policy changed significantly in scale, moving from the exclusion of one nationality in 1882 to a comprehensive system of quotas in 1924, but the underlying logic was continuous: in both, Congress used ideas of racial and national fitness to decide who could become American, while leaving employers' access to Mexican labor intact."

## Common misconceptions

- **"Everyone was rich in the 1920s."** Farmers, coal miners and textile workers struggled throughout, and income became more concentrated at the top.
- **"The 1920s were isolationist in every way."** The United States refused the League but hosted the Washington Naval Conference (1921–1922), helped reschedule German reparations (Dawes Plan, 1924) and signed the Kellogg–Briand Pact (1928).
- **"The Klan was only a southern organization."** The second Klan was national, with some of its greatest strength in the Midwest.
- **"Prohibition failed completely."** Drinking did decline; the law's costs (crime, disrespect for law, lost tax revenue) eventually outweighed its benefits in public opinion.
