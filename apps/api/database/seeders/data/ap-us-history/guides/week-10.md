## The "Revolution of 1800" and what it changed

The 1790s ended in fear. Federalists had passed the Alien and Sedition Acts and jailed Republican printers; Jefferson and Madison had answered with the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions; an undeclared naval war with France was winding down. When the electoral votes were counted in 1800, Jefferson and his running mate Aaron Burr had tied, and the Federalist House took 36 ballots to choose Jefferson. Many feared the losers would not give up power. They did, and that peaceful transfer is why Jefferson later described his victory as a revolution in the principles of government, and why historians still call it the Revolution of 1800. The Twelfth Amendment (1804) fixed the tie problem.

Jefferson's First Inaugural set the tone: "We are all Republicans, we are all Federalists." Both sides, he said, shared republican principles. In practice his administration did much of what it had promised:

- **Smaller, cheaper government.** Gallatin repealed internal taxes, including the whisky excise that had provoked rebellion in 1794, and paid down the national debt.
- **A smaller military.** The army and navy were cut; Jefferson preferred cheap coastal gunboats.
- **Undoing Federalist measures.** The Sedition Act expired and Jefferson pardoned those convicted under it; the Republican Congress repealed the Judiciary Act of 1801.

But he also kept a great deal. The Bank of the United States ran until its charter expired in 1811. Hamilton's funded debt was honoured. And the Barbary War (1801–1805) showed that even a frugal president would send the navy to protect commerce. The pattern is **continuity within change**.

## John Marshall and the federal courts

The Republicans won the presidency and Congress in 1800, but the courts stayed Federalist, and the most important Federalist of the next generation was Chief Justice John Marshall. **Marbury v. Madison (1803)** began with a minor appointment: William Marbury had been named a justice of the peace in Adams's last hours, but Secretary of State James Madison would not deliver the commission. Marshall held that Marbury was entitled to it, but that the section of the Judiciary Act of 1789 letting him sue directly in the Supreme Court was unconstitutional. "It is emphatically the province and duty of the Judicial Department to say what the law is." Jefferson won the case and had nothing to defy; the Court won judicial review.

Marshall's later decisions followed one line: the federal government over the states, contracts protected, a national market encouraged. *Fletcher v. Peck* (1810) and *Dartmouth College v. Woodward* (1819) protected contracts from state interference; *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819) upheld the Second Bank and barred states from taxing it; *Gibbons v. Ogden* (1824) gave Congress broad power over interstate commerce. The Federalist vision of a strong national government survived the party's collapse through the courts.

## Louisiana: opportunity versus principle

By 1802 Spain had secretly returned Louisiana to France and suspended Americans' right to deposit goods at New Orleans, which Pinckney's Treaty (1795) had guaranteed. Western farmers shipped their crops down the Mississippi, and Jefferson told his minister in Paris that whoever held New Orleans was "our natural & habitual enemy." He was prepared to "marry ourselves to the British fleet & nation", a striking line from the leader of the pro-French party.

Napoleon's plans collapsed in Saint-Domingue, where formerly enslaved revolutionaries and yellow fever destroyed his army; without the island, Louisiana was useless to him, and he needed money for war with Britain. In 1803 he sold the whole territory for $15 million. Jefferson believed the Constitution gave no power to acquire territory and drafted an amendment, then let it drop and used the treaty power for fear the deal would slip away. The purchase doubled the country and opened the way for Lewis and Clark, but it also raised the question that would dominate the century: would the new West be slave or free?

**Multiple perspectives.** New England Federalists feared new western states would drown their influence. The Native nations of the region, Osage, Lakota and others, were not consulted; to them the purchase was a transfer of claims between empires over lands they controlled.

## Regional interests: tariff, bank, roads and the Missouri crisis

After the War of 1812 (week 11), the Republicans adopted much of the Federalist program. Henry Clay of Kentucky called it the **American System**: a protective tariff so northern factories could grow, a national bank so credit and currency were stable, and federally funded internal improvements so western farm goods could go east and manufactures west. Madison signed the **Second Bank** and the **Tariff of 1816**, but vetoed the **Bonus Bill** (1817), saying roads and canals needed a constitutional amendment.

Each part served one region and burdened another. The Northeast wanted tariffs; the cotton-exporting South came to see the tariff as a tax on itself; the West wanted roads and cheap land. The **Panic of 1819**, when the Second Bank called in loans as cotton prices and land values fell, turned many westerners and southerners against the bank.

The deepest regional interest was slavery. When Missouri applied for statehood in 1819, New York's James Tallmadge proposed that no more enslaved people be brought in and that children born there be freed at 25. The House, where the more populous North had a majority, passed it; the Senate, balanced eleven to eleven, did not. Henry Clay helped broker the **Missouri Compromise (1820)**: Missouri entered as a slave state, Maine (separated from Massachusetts) as a free state, and slavery was "forever prohibited" in the rest of the purchase north of 36°30′. Jefferson, retired at Monticello, called the debate "a fire bell in the night."

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Jefferson, First Inaugural Address, March 4, 1801**

> "But every difference of opinion is not a difference of principle. We have called by different names brethren of the same principle. We are all Republicans, we are all Federalists."

*HIPP.* Historical situation: the first transfer of power between parties, after the Sedition Act prosecutions. Audience: Congress, the public and especially frightened Federalists. Purpose: reassurance and unity. Point of view: a victor who wants to govern a divided country. **Why it matters:** it shows the new party system learning to accept legitimate opposition, even as the Federalists faded.

**2. Marbury v. Madison, 1803**

> "It is emphatically the province and duty of the Judicial Department to say what the law is."

*HIPP.* Situation: a Federalist court facing a Republican president and Congress who had just abolished judgeships. Purpose: to assert the Court's authority in a way that could not be defied. **Why it matters:** judicial review underlies the Court's power in every later period.

**3. Jefferson to Robert R. Livingston, April 18, 1802**

> "There is on the globe one single spot, the possessor of which is our natural & habitual enemy. it is New Orleans, through which the produce of three eighths of our territory must pass to market ..."

*HIPP.* A private letter to a diplomat, meant to be shown to the French. Point of view: a pro-French president putting western trade first. **Why it matters:** it explains why Louisiana was bought and shows ideology yielding to interest.

**4. Jefferson to John Holmes, April 22, 1820**

> "... this momentous question, like a fire bell in the night, awakened and filled me with terror. I considered it at once as the knell of the Union."

*HIPP.* A private letter to a Massachusetts (soon Maine) congressman who had voted for the compromise. Point of view: an aging slaveholding founder who feared both slavery and emancipation. **Why it matters:** it predicts the sectional crisis of 1846–1861.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: a contextualization paragraph.** Prompt: "Evaluate the extent to which the election of 1800 changed American politics." A strong context paragraph is three or four sentences about the situation *before* 1800:

> In the 1790s Americans divided into two parties over Hamilton's financial program and over whether to favour Britain or revolutionary France. The Federalists' Alien and Sedition Acts (1798) jailed Republican editors, and Jefferson and Madison replied with the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions, claiming states could judge federal laws. Many on both sides doubted that a defeated party would peacefully surrender power. It was in this atmosphere that Jefferson's Republicans won the election of 1800.

It earns the point because it is broader than the prompt, specific, and leads into the argument.

**Worked example 2: an SAQ answered part by part.** "(a) Briefly describe ONE cause of the Missouri Compromise. (b) Briefly explain ONE effect. (c) Briefly explain ONE way the compromise reflected regional interests."

- (a) Missouri's application to enter as a slave state threatened to give slave states a majority in the Senate, and the Tallmadge Amendment's gradual emancipation plan passed the House on a sectional vote.
- (b) The 36°30′ line kept the peace on slavery's expansion for about a generation, until the Kansas-Nebraska Act repealed it in 1854.
- (c) Northern representatives, whose region had abolished slavery, used their House majority to try to limit it; southerners used Senate equality to protect their power, so the deal balanced states rather than settling the moral question.

Each part answers directly, then adds one specific detail.

**Worked example 3: from prompt to thesis.** Prompt: "Evaluate the extent to which Jefferson's presidency represented continuity with Federalist policies."

- Weak: "Jefferson's presidency had both continuity and change." (Restates the prompt.)
- Strong: "Although Jefferson reversed Federalist fiscal policy by repealing internal taxes and cutting the military, his presidency largely continued Federalist practice, because he kept the national bank, honoured Hamilton's debt and used broad federal power to buy Louisiana."

It answers "to what extent" and sets up the body paragraphs.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Jefferson dismantled Hamilton's system."** He cut taxes and debt but kept the bank and the funded debt; his successors chartered a second bank.
- **"Marbury v. Madison was a defeat for the Court."** Marbury lost his commission, but the Court gained judicial review.
- **"The Missouri Compromise banned slavery in the West."** Only north of 36°30′ in the Louisiana Purchase; land south of the line stayed open to slavery.
- **"The Era of Good Feelings was a time without conflict."** One party ruled, but the Panic of 1819 and the Missouri crisis split the country by class and section.
- **"The Federalists disappeared in 1800."** They held New England and the courts until the Hartford Convention finished them.
