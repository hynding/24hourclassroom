## The freedom movement at its height, 1960–1965

On February 1, 1960, four Black students sat down at a whites-only Woolworth's counter in Greensboro and refused to leave. Within weeks sit-ins spread across the South. Ella Baker helped the students found **SNCC**, which built local leadership through slow, dangerous voter registration work in Mississippi and Georgia. In 1961 CORE's **Freedom Riders** rode buses into the Deep South; after mobs burned a bus at Anniston and beat riders, the Kennedy administration sent marshals and the ICC banned segregated terminals.

The movement learned that televised violence could force federal action. In **Birmingham** (1963) police turned dogs and fire hoses on marching children; King, jailed there, wrote his famous letter defending direct action against those who counselled waiting. That June Kennedy told the nation civil rights was a moral issue and proposed a bill. In August about 250,000 people joined the **March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom**. After Kennedy's assassination Johnson pushed the **Civil Rights Act of 1964** through a record Senate filibuster: it banned discrimination in public accommodations and employment (by race, colour, religion, sex or national origin) and created the EEOC.

Voting remained the hardest fight. During **Freedom Summer** (1964) volunteers registered voters in Mississippi; three were murdered. The Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party, with Fannie Lou Hamer, was offered only two token seats at the Democratic convention, a lesson that bred distrust of liberal allies. In March 1965 Alabama troopers beat marchers at the Edmund Pettus Bridge in **Selma**. Eight days later Johnson addressed Congress, and in August he signed the **Voting Rights Act**, which suspended literacy tests and sent federal examiners south. Black registration in Mississippi rose from under 7% to about 60% within a few years.

## Divisions and the urban North, 1965–1968

Legal victories did not change Northern ghettos. Five days after the Voting Rights Act, Watts in Los Angeles erupted; Newark and Detroit followed in 1967. Johnson's **Kerner Commission** blamed white racism and segregation, not outside agitators. King took his campaign to Chicago (1966), opposed the Vietnam War (1967) and was planning a Poor People's Campaign when he was assassinated in Memphis in April 1968.

Many younger activists lost faith in nonviolence and integration. Malcolm X, assassinated in 1965, had argued for Black self-respect and self-defence. In 1966 Stokely Carmichael of SNCC called for **Black Power**: Black-controlled institutions and politics, pride in Black identity. The Black Panther Party combined armed patrols with breakfast and health programs. Older leaders and many whites feared the slogan; supporters saw it as realism. Later fights shifted to busing (Swann, 1971; Boston, 1974) and affirmative action (Bakke, 1978).

## The movement expands

The Black freedom struggle provided a model of tactics, legal arguments and moral language.

- **Women.** Betty Friedan's 1963 book described the frustration of educated housewives (describe it; do not quote it). The Equal Pay Act (1963) and Title VII gave legal tools; NOW (1966) pressed for enforcement. Radical feminists organised consciousness-raising groups. Gains included Title IX (1972) and Roe v. Wade (1973). The ERA passed Congress in 1972 but stalled three states short, opposed by Phyllis Schlafly and conservative women who argued it threatened protections for homemakers.
- **Latinos.** César Chávez, Dolores Huerta and Filipino American organiser Larry Itliong led the Delano grape strike (from 1965) and a national boycott that won contracts in 1970. Chicano students walked out of East Los Angeles high schools in 1968; La Raza Unida ran candidates; the Bilingual Education Act passed in 1968.
- **American Indians.** Reacting against termination and relocation, Red Power activists occupied Alcatraz (1969–1971); AIM occupied the BIA building (1972) and Wounded Knee (1973). Nixon endorsed self-determination in 1970, and Congress passed the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act in 1975.
- **Asian Americans** coined that shared name in 1968 and joined student strikes for ethnic studies.
- **Gay Americans.** The 1969 Stonewall uprising began gay liberation; by the late 1970s some cities passed anti-discrimination laws, and backlash campaigns repealed several.

## Youth culture

The baby boom produced the largest generation of young people in American history, and colleges swelled. Students for a Democratic Society's 1962 Port Huron Statement called for participatory democracy (describe, not quote). Berkeley's Free Speech Movement (1964) made campus protest a model. The draft turned the New Left into a mass antiwar movement. Alongside it the **counterculture** rejected mainstream values through rock music, drugs, communes and sexual freedom, its high point the Woodstock festival (1969). The Supreme Court protected student protest in **Tinker v. Des Moines** (1969), and the **Twenty-sixth Amendment** (1971) lowered the voting age to 18. Many Americans, including many young people, rejected the counterculture, and that reaction fed Nixon's appeal to the silent majority.

## The environment, 1968–1980

Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962) had alerted readers to pesticides. In 1969 a Santa Barbara oil spill and the burning of Cleveland's Cuyahoga River made pollution visible. About 20 million people joined the first **Earth Day** (April 1970). A bipartisan burst of law followed: the National Environmental Policy Act (1970), the **EPA** (1970), the Clean Air Act (1970), the Clean Water Act (1972) and the Endangered Species Act (1973). The 1973 Arab oil embargo then turned the issue toward energy: prices quadrupled, gasoline lines formed, and Congress imposed a 55 mph speed limit and fuel-economy standards. Carter created the Department of Energy (1977), but the Iranian Revolution brought a second oil shock in 1979, the year of the Three Mile Island accident and Love Canal's toxic-waste crisis.

## Society in transition: the 1970s

**Watergate.** In June 1972 burglars tied to Nixon's re-election committee were arrested at Democratic headquarters. Nixon joined the cover-up. Senate hearings revealed a taping system; in **United States v. Nixon** (1974) a unanimous Court ordered the tapes released, and Nixon resigned on August 9, 1974. Ford's pardon of Nixon and reforms such as campaign finance limits followed, but trust in government fell to new lows.

**Stagflation.** Vietnam spending without new taxes, the oil shocks and rising foreign competition produced inflation and unemployment together. Nixon imposed wage and price controls and ended the dollar's convertibility to gold (1971). Steel and auto towns of the Midwest became the Rust Belt as jobs moved to the Sun Belt and overseas.

**The New Right.** Opposition to busing, affirmative action and high taxes (California's Proposition 13, 1978), religious conservatives mobilised against Roe and the ERA (the Moral Majority, 1979) and critics of détente formed a coalition. Carter's July 1979 speech on a crisis of confidence captured the national mood; the Iran hostage crisis sealed his defeat by Ronald Reagan in 1980.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Lyndon B. Johnson, The American Promise, March 15, 1965**

> "There is no Negro problem. There is no Southern problem. There is no Northern problem. There is only an American problem."

*HIPP:* A Southern president, eight days after Selma, addressing Congress and a national television audience to win a voting rights law. *Why it matters:* the federal government fully embraced the movement's cause.

**2. Richard Nixon, message on Indian affairs, July 8, 1970**

> "The time has come to break decisively with the past and to create the conditions for a new era in which the Indian future is determined by Indian acts and Indian decisions."

*HIPP:* A Republican president during the Alcatraz occupation. *Why it matters:* it ended termination as policy.

**3. Richard Nixon, State of the Union, January 22, 1970**

> "The great question of the seventies is, shall we surrender to our surroundings, or shall we make our peace with nature and begin to make reparations for the damage we have done to our air, to our land, and to our water?"

*HIPP:* Three months before Earth Day, positioning the president ahead of a popular movement and rival Democrats. *Why it matters:* environmental protection began as a bipartisan cause.

**4. Jimmy Carter, address of July 15, 1979**

> "It is a crisis of confidence. It is a crisis that strikes at the very heart and soul and spirit of our national will."

*HIPP:* A president at low approval, after gas lines and inflation. *Why it matters:* it named the mood of the late 1970s, and its reception helped open the way for Reagan.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: sourcing Johnson's 1965 address.** Point of view: a Texan who had voted against civil rights bills in the 1940s now argued for them, which made his words carry special weight with Southern and moderate members. Purpose: to pass a bill quickly while national outrage over Selma was fresh. Audience: Congress and, through television, the nation.

**Worked example 2: a continuity-and-change thesis for Period 8.** "From 1945 to 1980 the goal of containing communism and the commitment to an active federal government persisted, but the period transformed American society by ending legal segregation and expanding rights for many groups, while the shocks of Vietnam, Watergate and stagflation replaced postwar confidence with distrust of government."

**Worked example 3: an SAQ part.** "Briefly explain ONE reason the civil rights movement divided after 1965." Answer: after the Voting Rights Act, many younger activists in SNCC concluded that nonviolence and alliance with white liberals had not changed economic conditions or stopped violence against them (the MFDP's treatment in 1964 and murders in Mississippi), so they embraced Black Power and community control, while King and older groups stayed committed to integration and nonviolence.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The movement ended in 1965."** It shifted to the North, to economics and to new groups.
- **"Black Power meant violence."** It meant self-determination; the Panthers' armed patrols were one expression among many.
- **"Environmentalism was a Democratic cause."** Nixon signed most of the founding laws.
- **"The ERA became law."** It fell three states short.
