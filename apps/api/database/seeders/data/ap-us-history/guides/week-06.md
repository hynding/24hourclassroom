## What you need to be able to do

- **3.1** Explain the context in which Britain's colonies moved from loyal membership of the empire to open resistance, 1754–1774.
- **3.2** Explain the causes and effects of the Seven Years' War, for Britain, France, Spain, the colonists and American Indians.
- **3.3** Explain how British attempts to tax and govern the colonies after 1763 produced colonial resistance, and how colonists justified that resistance.

In plain language: a war fought over the Ohio Valley left Britain with a huge debt and a huge territory. To pay the debt and police the territory, Britain tried to run its colonies more tightly. Colonists who had spent decades governing themselves read every new tax as an attack on their rights as Englishmen, and a quarrel over the constitution of the empire became, by 1774, a crisis on the edge of war.

## Context: an empire of loose ties (3.1)

By 1754 British North America held well over a million people, roughly a fifth of them enslaved. Colonists were bound to Britain by trade, Protestantism and pride, and thought of themselves as Britons overseas.

But the empire was loosely governed. The Navigation Acts (week 4) channelled trade through Britain on mercantilist principles, yet enforcement was lax and smuggling was common. Each colony had an elected assembly that, like the House of Commons, controlled taxes and used its power of the purse to bargain with the royal governor. Historians call this arrangement **salutary neglect**. Two cultural currents of the eighteenth century (week 5) reinforced colonial self-confidence: the **Great Awakening**, which taught ordinary believers to judge their ministers, and the **Enlightenment**, whose writers, John Locke above all, argued that legitimate government rests on consent and exists to protect natural rights.

Between the British coast and New France lay a broad interior where the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy, the Shawnee, the Delaware and others kept their independence by playing the two empires against each other.

## The Seven Years' War (3.2)

**Causes.** Britain and France had fought three wars between 1689 and 1748, and North America was always one front. The new flashpoint was the **Ohio River valley**. Virginia land speculators wanted it; the French built a chain of forts, including Fort Duquesne at the Forks of the Ohio, to connect Canada with Louisiana; and the Native nations who lived there wanted neither empire to take it. In 1754 Virginia sent a young militia officer, George Washington, to assert its claim. His men clashed with a French party, and he was forced to surrender his hastily built Fort Necessity.

That summer delegates at **Albany** approved Franklin's **Plan of Union**; every colonial assembly rejected it, unwilling to hand an intercolonial council the power to tax. The colonies would unite only against a threat to their own self-government.

**Course of the war.** Braddock's army was routed near Fort Duquesne in 1755, and the war spread to Europe in 1756 and then around the globe. From 1757 **William Pitt** poured money and regular troops into America and reimbursed colonial assemblies. Louisbourg fell in 1758, Quebec in 1759 and Montreal in 1760.

The war also bred friction: British officers scorned colonial militia and slow assemblies, and colonists resented British arrogance and quartering. Britain concluded the colonists had been protected at its expense; many colonists concluded they had won the war themselves.

**Effects.** The **Treaty of Paris (1763)** removed France from mainland North America. Britain took Canada and all French claims east of the Mississippi and took Florida from Spain; Spain received Louisiana west of the river. The consequences:

- **For Britain:** an empire doubled in size and a national debt that roughly doubled too, plus the cost of an army of about 10,000 men to garrison the new territory.
- **For American Indians:** the loss of the French counterweight. General Amherst cut off the diplomatic gifts, settlers moved west, and **Pontiac's War** (1763), inspired in part by the Delaware prophet **Neolin**, overran most British forts west of the mountains.
- **For colonists:** relief from the French threat, which made British protection seem less necessary, and the expectation that the West was now open.

## Taxation without representation (3.3)

**New policy.** The **Proclamation of 1763** reserved the land beyond the Appalachian crest for American Indians and forbade colonists to settle or buy there. London meant to avoid another costly frontier war; speculators and farmers saw their reward taken away, and many ignored the line. In Pennsylvania the **Paxton Boys** murdered peaceful Conestoga Indians and marched on Philadelphia.

George Grenville's ministry then set out to make the colonies pay part of the cost of their defence. The **Sugar Act (1764)** cut the molasses duty but meant to collect it, trying smugglers in vice-admiralty courts without juries; the **Currency Act** barred paper money as legal tender. The **Stamp Act (1765)**, a direct tax on newspapers, legal documents and licences, hit the most articulate colonists and was the first internal tax Parliament had ever laid on America.

**The constitutional argument.** Ministers claimed **virtual representation**: Parliament spoke for every subject, as it did for the many Englishmen who could not vote. Colonists answered that only representatives they chose, their own assemblies, could consent to their taxes. They accepted Parliament's regulation of trade but not taxation for revenue. The **Stamp Act Congress** said so in October 1765, with careful professions of loyalty.

**Resistance in practice.** The **Sons of Liberty** forced stamp distributors to resign; merchants signed **nonimportation agreements**; women spun homespun and gave up imported goods. Parliament repealed the Stamp Act in 1766, pressed by British merchants, but passed the **Declaratory Act** the same day, claiming authority over the colonies "in all cases whatsoever".

**Round two.** The **Townshend Acts (1767)** taxed imported glass, lead, paint, paper and tea, and used the revenue to pay royal governors and judges, escaping the assemblies' power of the purse. John Dickinson's *Letters from a Farmer in Pennsylvania* argued that duties laid for revenue were taxes all the same. Boycotts resumed, troops arrived in Boston in 1768, and in March 1770 soldiers killed five people in the **Boston Massacre**, among them Crispus Attucks; Paul Revere's engraving made it propaganda. Parliament repealed every Townshend duty except the one on tea.

**Round three.** **Committees of correspondence** (from 1772) knit resistance together. The **Tea Act (1773)** let the East India Company sell cheap, taxed tea directly; colonists saw a trick to make them accept taxation, and in December Bostonians dumped 342 chests into the harbour. The **Coercive Acts** (1774) closed Boston's port and curtailed Massachusetts self-government, and the **Quebec Act** extended Quebec into the Ohio country. Punishing one colony united the rest: the **First Continental Congress** adopted a continent-wide boycott and petitioned the king. Most delegates still wanted their rights within the empire, but militias were drilling.

**Multiple perspectives.** Royal officials, many Anglicans, merchants tied to British credit and many backcountry settlers were loyal or neutral; enslaved people drew their own conclusions from the language of liberty; and American Indians generally saw the crown as a lesser threat than the colonists.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. The Royal Proclamation of 1763** (Avalon Project)

> "And We do hereby strictly forbid, on Pain of our Displeasure, all our loving Subjects from making any Purchases or Settlements whatever, or taking Possession of any of the Lands above reserved . . . without our especial leave and Licence for that Purpose first obtained."

*HIPP.* Issued in October 1763 while Pontiac's War raged, addressed to governors, settlers and Native nations, to prevent frontier wars and put land purchases under crown control. Why it matters: the first post-war measure colonists read as a betrayal.

**2. Resolutions of the Stamp Act Congress, October 19, 1765** (Avalon Project)

> "That the people of these colonies are not, and from their local circumstances cannot be, represented in the House of Commons in Great-Britain.
>
> That the only representatives of the people of these colonies, are persons chosen therein by themselves, and that no taxes ever have been, or can be constitutionally imposed on them, but by their respective legislatures."

*HIPP.* Delegates from nine colonies, mostly lawyers and merchants, addressing king and Parliament. Purpose: to win repeal by a respectful constitutional argument, which is why the first resolution professes allegiance and "due subordination". Why it matters: the clearest statement of the colonial case against virtual representation.

**3. The Declaratory Act, 1766** (Avalon Project)

> "That the said colonies and plantations in America have been, are, and of right ought to be, subordinate unto, and dependent upon the imperial crown and parliament of Great Britain . . ."

*HIPP.* Parliament, speaking to the colonies and to its own members, at the moment of retreat. Purpose: to concede repeal without conceding principle, claiming power to bind the colonies "in all cases whatsoever". Why it matters: it left the constitutional question unresolved, so every later tax reopened it.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: a contextualization paragraph.** Prompt: *Evaluate the extent to which British policy caused colonial resistance, 1763–1774.* A strong context sentence moves from the broad situation to the topic in three steps:

> "For most of the eighteenth century Britain governed its colonies loosely; assemblies controlled taxes and salaries, and the Navigation Acts were weakly enforced. The Seven Years' War changed that balance. Victory left Britain with a doubled debt and a vast western territory to police, and ministers concluded that colonists should share the cost. The collision between new imperial demands and old habits of self-government set the stage for a decade of protest."

Why it works: it is specific, and its last sentence connects context to argument.

**Worked example 2: HIPP on the Stamp Act Congress.** Why does the document begin with loyalty? Crowds had just forced stamp distributors to resign, and moderates wanted to separate principled protest from riot; the audience was king and Parliament, who could repeal the act. So the professions of loyalty are both sincere and strategic. A sourcing point earns credit only when it explains how the circumstance shaped the content.

**Worked example 3: a causation chain.** Link each step with *because* or *so*:

1. The war doubled Britain's debt and left an army in America, **so** ministers sought colonial revenue.
2. The Stamp Act was a direct tax without consent, **so** colonists organised a congress, boycotts and crowd action.
3. Boycotts hurt British merchants, **so** Parliament repealed the act but passed the Declaratory Act, leaving the principle open.
4. The Townshend Acts and the Tea Act reopened the principle, **so** resistance resumed, now organised through committees of correspondence.
5. The Coercive Acts punished Massachusetts, **so** the colonies united in the First Continental Congress.

Each event is both effect and cause: name the mechanism, not just the sequence.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The colonists wanted independence from 1763."** Almost none did before 1775. They claimed the rights of Englishmen inside the empire.
- **"The taxes were crushing."** They were light; the stamp duties were lower than taxes paid in Britain. The dispute was about consent and control.
- **"The Tea Act raised the price of tea."** It lowered it. The objection was to the precedent and the monopoly.
- **"The Proclamation of 1763 was a tax."** It was a restriction on settlement, aimed at preventing war with Native nations.
