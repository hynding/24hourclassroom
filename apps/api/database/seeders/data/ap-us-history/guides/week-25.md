## Why the crash became a depression (7.9)

The stock market had risen spectacularly from 1927, fuelled by speculation and **buying on margin**. When prices broke in October 1929 (Black Thursday and Black Tuesday), margin calls forced more selling. But the crash alone did not make a decade-long depression. Underlying weaknesses did:

- **Unequal income.** Wages lagged behind productivity; the richest Americans held a growing share of income, so purchasing power could not keep up with production.
- **Debt.** Consumers had bought cars and appliances on installment credit; farmers were already in debt and depressed.
- **Banks.** Thousands of small, unregulated, uninsured banks failed (about 9,000 between 1930 and 1933), destroying savings and setting off runs. The Federal Reserve let the money supply shrink by about a third.
- **The world economy.** War debts, reparations and American loans to Germany formed a fragile circle; the **Smoot–Hawley Tariff** (1930) provoked retaliation and world trade collapsed.

Unemployment rose from 3.2 percent in 1929 to about 25 percent in 1933. Families lost homes and farms; shantytowns called **Hoovervilles** spread; hunger and malnutrition were common. African Americans, "last hired, first fired", suffered unemployment far above the average. Mexican and Mexican American workers faced **repatriation** campaigns that drove hundreds of thousands, many of them U.S. citizens, to Mexico. On the southern plains, drought and the ploughing of grassland during the wheat boom produced the **Dust Bowl**, and hundreds of thousands of migrants went to California.

**Hoover's response.** Hoover was no do-nothing. He asked business leaders to hold wages, supported public works such as the Hoover Dam, and created the **Reconstruction Finance Corporation** (1932) to lend to banks and corporations. But he believed, as he had said in 1928, that "rugged individualism" was the American system, and that federal relief payments to individuals would create dependence and centralise power. Relief was left to charities and localities that ran out of money. When the **Bonus Army** of unemployed veterans was driven from Washington with tear gas in 1932, Hoover's defeat was assured.

## The First New Deal, 1933–1934 (7.10)

Franklin D. Roosevelt took office in March 1933 with banks closing across the country. "The only thing we have to fear is fear itself," he declared, and asked for "broad Executive power to wage a war against the emergency." In the **Hundred Days** Congress passed a flood of laws, organised around the "three Rs":

- **Relief.** The Federal Emergency Relief Administration sent money to the states; the **Civilian Conservation Corps** put young men to work in forests and parks.
- **Recovery.** The **Agricultural Adjustment Act** paid farmers to cut production; the **National Recovery Administration** let industries write codes setting prices, wages and hours.
- **Reform.** A bank holiday and the Emergency Banking Act reopened sound banks; the Glass–Steagall Act separated commercial and investment banking and created the **FDIC**; the Securities and Exchange Commission (1934) policed the stock market; the **Tennessee Valley Authority** brought public power, flood control and development to a poor region.

Roosevelt explained it all in radio **fireside chats**, the first presidential use of mass media to speak directly to citizens.

## Critics, the Court and the Second New Deal, 1935–1938 (7.10)

The New Deal was attacked from both sides. On the right, the **American Liberty League** (1934) said it destroyed property rights and the Constitution. On the left, Senator **Huey Long**'s "Share Our Wealth" plan, **Father Charles Coughlin**'s radio sermons and **Dr Francis Townsend**'s pension plan won millions of followers by saying Roosevelt did too little. The **Supreme Court** struck down the NRA in *Schechter* (1935) ("Extraordinary conditions do not create or enlarge constitutional power") and the AAA in 1936.

Roosevelt responded in 1935 with a **Second New Deal** aimed at lasting reform: the **Works Progress Administration** (millions of jobs building roads, schools and airports, and supporting artists and writers); the **Social Security Act** (old-age insurance, unemployment insurance, aid to dependent children); the **Wagner Act**, guaranteeing workers the right to organise and bargain collectively; and higher taxes on the rich. Unions surged: the **CIO** organised mass-production industries, and the sit-down strike at General Motors in Flint (1936–1937) won recognition for the United Auto Workers.

After a landslide re-election in 1936, Roosevelt overreached. His **Court-packing** plan (1937) was rejected by Congress, though the Court began upholding New Deal laws anyway. Then the **"Roosevelt recession"** of 1937–1938, caused by spending cuts and tighter credit, sent unemployment back up. The **Fair Labor Standards Act** (1938), with a minimum wage and maximum hours, was the last major reform before a conservative coalition of Republicans and southern Democrats stalled the New Deal.

## Who gained, who was left out (7.10)

**Workers** gained collective bargaining rights and a minimum wage. **The elderly and unemployed** gained a permanent if modest safety net. **Women** gained a cabinet member (Frances Perkins) and Eleanor Roosevelt's advocacy, but programmes often paid women less. **African Americans** found some help (Mary McLeod Bethune's "Black Cabinet", WPA jobs), and northern Black voters shifted to the Democrats, but Social Security excluded agricultural and domestic workers, AAA payments led to evictions of sharecroppers, programmes were segregated, and Roosevelt would not back an anti-lynching bill. **American Indians** saw allotment ended by the **Indian Reorganization Act** (1934), passed "to grant certain rights of home rule to Indians", though its model constitutions did not always fit tribal traditions. **Mexican Americans** faced repatriation and exclusion from Social Security.

The New Deal did not end the Depression; unemployment was still about 15 percent in 1940. War spending did that. But it changed what Americans expected of Washington, created institutions (FDIC, SEC, Social Security, the NLRB) that still exist, and built the **New Deal coalition** that kept Democrats dominant for a generation.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Herbert Hoover, campaign address, New York City, October 22, 1928**

> "We were challenged with a peacetime choice between the American system of rugged individualism and a European philosophy of diametrically opposed doctrines—doctrines of paternalism and state socialism."

*HIPP.* A candidate at the height of the boom, speaking to voters about why the wartime controls of 1917–1918 had been dismantled. *Why it matters:* it explains why, three years later, Hoover refused direct federal relief.

**2. Franklin D. Roosevelt, first inaugural address, March 4, 1933**

> "So, first of all, let me assert my firm belief that the only thing we have to fear is fear itself—nameless, unreasoning, unjustified terror which paralyzes needed efforts to convert retreat into advance."

*HIPP.* Spoken on the day banks in most states were closed; audience a frightened nation listening by radio. Purpose: to restore confidence and prepare Americans for emergency executive action. *Why it matters:* it launched the Hundred Days.

**3. Franklin D. Roosevelt, second inaugural address, January 20, 1937**

> "The test of our progress is not whether we add more to the abundance of those who have much; it is whether we provide enough for those who have too little."

*HIPP.* After a landslide, as Roosevelt prepared to confront the Court. *Why it matters:* the clearest statement of the New Deal's idea of positive federal responsibility, and a direct contrast to Hoover in 1928.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: reading the unemployment series.** Unemployment (percent of the labor force): 1929, 3.2; 1933, 24.9; 1937, 14.3; 1938, 19.0; 1941, 9.9 (Historical Statistics of the United States). *What it shows:* a collapse to 1933, a real but incomplete recovery to 1937, a sharp setback in 1938, and a return toward normal only with defense spending in 1940–1941. *Source note:* this series counts people on emergency work relief as unemployed; series that count them as employed show lower New Deal unemployment. Always ask how a statistic was defined.

**Worked example 2: an SAQ comparison.** *Briefly explain ONE difference between Hoover's and Roosevelt's responses to the Depression.* "Hoover provided federal loans to banks and businesses through the Reconstruction Finance Corporation but refused direct relief to individuals, believing it would undermine self-reliance; Roosevelt, through FERA and later the WPA, gave federal money and jobs directly to the unemployed, accepting federal responsibility for their welfare."

**Worked example 3: a DBQ sourcing sentence.** Document: Chief Justice Hughes in *Schechter* (1935): "Extraordinary conditions do not create or enlarge constitutional power." Sourcing: "Because a unanimous Court that included liberal justices such as Brandeis wrote this, it shows that objections to the NRA were not only conservative; the decision reflected a widely shared belief that even an emergency could not erase constitutional limits on federal power." Point of view, explained in relation to the argument, earns the sourcing point; merely naming the author does not.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The crash caused the Depression."** It triggered it; bank failures, inequality, debt, farm distress and the collapse of world trade made it deep and long.
- **"Hoover did nothing."** He did more than any earlier president in a downturn, but within limits his philosophy set.
- **"The New Deal ended the Depression."** It reduced suffering and reformed institutions; full employment came with the Second World War.
- **"The New Deal was socialism."** Banks and industries stayed private; most programmes aimed to stabilise capitalism.
- **"New Deal programmes helped everyone equally."** Exclusions, segregation and local control left many African Americans, Mexican Americans and women with less.
