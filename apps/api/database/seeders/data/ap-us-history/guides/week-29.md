## A global Cold War, 1953–1979

**Eisenhower: deterrence and covert action.** Eisenhower ended the Korean War and sought containment at lower cost. Nuclear deterrence was one tool; the CIA was another. In 1953 it helped overthrow Iran's prime minister Mohammad Mossadegh, who had nationalised oil, and in 1954 Guatemala's elected president Jacobo Árbenz, whose land reform threatened the United Fruit Company. These quick successes created lasting resentment in both countries. In the 1956 Suez Crisis, Eisenhower forced Britain, France and Israel to withdraw from Egypt, and the 1957 Eisenhower Doctrine made the United States the main Western power in the Middle East. As dozens of former colonies in Asia and Africa became independent, the superpowers competed for them with aid and propaganda, and many chose non-alignment.

Eisenhower left office warning, in his farewell address, against the "unwarranted influence" of a new military-industrial complex.

**Kennedy: crisis and flexible response.** Kennedy promised to be more active. He expanded conventional and special forces, launched the Peace Corps and the Alliance for Progress for Latin America, and approved the CIA's Bay of Pigs invasion of Cuba (April 1961), which failed in days. That August East Germany built the Berlin Wall. In October 1962 U.S. spy planes found Soviet nuclear missiles in Cuba. Kennedy rejected an air strike, imposed a naval quarantine and negotiated: Khrushchev removed the missiles in return for a no-invasion pledge and a secret promise to remove American missiles from Turkey. Both sides drew back: a hotline and the Limited Test Ban Treaty followed in 1963.

**Nixon and Kissinger: détente.** By 1969 the Soviet Union had reached rough nuclear parity, Vietnam had drained American power, and China and the Soviet Union were openly hostile to each other. Nixon and his adviser Henry Kissinger used that split. Nixon visited China in February 1972 and signed the SALT I and Anti-Ballistic Missile treaties in Moscow that May. Détente was realpolitik: managing rivalry rather than ending it.

**Carter: human rights and new crises.** Carter emphasised human rights, returned the Panama Canal by treaty (1977) and brokered the Camp David Accords (1978) between Egypt and Israel. But the Iranian Revolution brought the seizure of the U.S. embassy in Tehran in November 1979, and the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December ended détente. Week 31 continues from here.

## The Vietnam War

**Roots (1945–1963).** Ho Chi Minh's Viet Minh fought French colonial rule after 1945. The United States, seeing Ho first as a Communist, paid most of France's war costs by 1954. After the French defeat at Dien Bien Phu, the Geneva Accords divided Vietnam at the 17th parallel, with elections set for 1956. Eisenhower, citing the domino theory, backed Ngo Dinh Diem's government in the South, and the elections were never held. Diem's regime was narrow and repressive; Buddhist protests in 1963 led to a U.S.-approved coup in which Diem was killed, three weeks before Kennedy's own assassination. About 16,000 American advisers were in Vietnam by then.

**Escalation (1964–1968).** After reported attacks on U.S. destroyers in August 1964, Congress passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution almost unanimously. Johnson used it to launch Operation Rolling Thunder and send combat troops in 1965. American strategy was attrition: kill enemy soldiers faster than they could be replaced. By 1968 more than half a million U.S. troops were in Vietnam. Officials claimed progress; reporters described a stalemate. That credibility gap widened when the Tet Offensive (January 1968) struck cities across the South. Tet failed militarily, but it convinced many Americans the war could not be won soon. In March Johnson announced he would not run again.

**At home.** Opposition grew from teach-ins (1965) to mass marches. The draft fell heavily on working-class and minority young men; Martin Luther King Jr. publicly opposed the war in 1967; televised combat brought the war home. 1968 brought the assassinations of King and Robert Kennedy and violence at the Democratic convention in Chicago. Yet many Americans supported the war, or wanted it fought harder, and resented the protesters.

**Nixon's war (1969–1975).** Nixon pursued Vietnamization: withdrawing U.S. troops while bombing heavily and building up South Vietnam's army. In November 1969 he appealed to the "great silent majority" against the protesters. The 1970 invasion of Cambodia set off campus protests in which National Guardsmen killed four students at Kent State and police killed two at Jackson State. The revelation of the My Lai massacre and the 1971 Pentagon Papers further eroded trust. The Paris Peace Accords (January 1973) ended U.S. combat; North Vietnam conquered the South in April 1975. About 58,000 Americans and millions of Southeast Asians died.

**Consequences.** Congress passed the War Powers Resolution (1973) over Nixon's veto. The draft ended in 1973, and the Twenty-sixth Amendment (1971) lowered the voting age to 18. Distrust of government, which Watergate deepened, became a lasting feature of American politics.

## The Great Society and the rights revolution

Kennedy's domestic program, the New Frontier, stalled in Congress. After his assassination, Lyndon Johnson used his legislative skill, sympathy for the slain president and, after his 1964 landslide over Barry Goldwater, huge Democratic majorities to pass:

- **War on Poverty:** the Economic Opportunity Act (1964): Job Corps, VISTA, Community Action, and Head Start.
- **Health:** Medicare and Medicaid (1965).
- **Education:** the Elementary and Secondary Education Act and the Higher Education Act (1965).
- **Civil rights:** the Civil Rights Act (1964) and Voting Rights Act (1965) (week 30).
- **Immigration:** the Hart-Celler Act (1965), ending the national-origins quotas of 1924.
- **Cities and culture:** the Department of Housing and Urban Development, the National Endowments for the Arts and Humanities, and early environmental and consumer laws.

The official poverty rate fell from about 19% in 1964 to about 11% in 1973, most sharply among the elderly. The Warren Court expanded rights: Gideon v. Wainwright (1963), Miranda v. Arizona (1966), Engel v. Vitale (1962), and Reynolds v. Sims (1964).

**Opposition and limits.** Conservatives, energised by Goldwater's campaign, attacked big government, high taxes and the Court's rulings on crime and religion. Many white working-class voters saw the programs as benefiting others at their expense, especially after urban riots in 1965–1968. On the left, critics said the War on Poverty was underfunded, and Vietnam drained the money. Yet Nixon, a Republican, kept and even expanded parts of it (food stamps, Social Security increases, the Environmental Protection Agency), showing how far the federal role had changed.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Eisenhower, news conference, April 7, 1954**

> "You have a row of dominoes set up, you knock over the first one, and what will happen to the last one is the certainty that it will go over very quickly."

*HIPP:* Spoken as the French faced defeat at Dien Bien Phu, to reporters and through them the public. *Why it matters:* the image justified twenty years of commitment.

**2. Tonkin Gulf Resolution, August 7, 1964**

> "That the Congress approves and supports the determination of the President, as Commander in Chief, to take all necessary measures to repel any armed attack against the forces of the United States and to prevent further aggression."

*HIPP:* Passed within days of a disputed incident, three months before an election. *Why it matters:* a blank cheque for war without a declaration; Congress repealed it in 1971.

**3. Johnson, State of the Union, January 8, 1964**

> "This administration today, here and now, declares unconditional war on poverty in America."

*HIPP:* Johnson's first annual message, seven weeks after Kennedy's death, to a Congress he hoped to move quickly. *Why it matters:* the federal government took responsibility not just for relieving poverty but for preventing it.

**4. Nixon, address on Vietnam, November 3, 1969**

> "And so tonight—to you, the great silent majority of my fellow Americans—I ask for your support."

*HIPP:* Delivered weeks after massive Moratorium protests. *Why it matters:* it named a political constituency that conservatives would build on.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: sourcing the Tonkin Resolution.** "Explain how the historical situation of the resolution affects its reliability as evidence of what happened in the Gulf of Tonkin." Answer: the resolution was drafted before the incident and rushed through after a second attack that probably did not occur; it reflects the administration's account and its political need to look firm before the 1964 election, so it is strong evidence of policy and weak evidence of events at sea.

**Worked example 2: a causation chain.** Tet (January 1968) → widened credibility gap and Cronkite-style doubts on television → Johnson's March 31 withdrawal from the race and bombing halt → Nixon elected promising peace with honour → Vietnamization and gradual withdrawal → Paris Accords (1973). Each arrow needs a "because."

**Worked example 3: comparing the Great Society with the New Deal.** Similarity: both expanded federal responsibility for economic security (Medicare was added to the Social Security system). Difference: the New Deal answered a depression and focused on recovery and relief for the working class; the Great Society assumed abundance and targeted those left out, adding civil rights, health and education. Thesis: "The Great Society extended the New Deal's logic from security for workers to opportunity for the excluded, but its dependence on continued growth made it vulnerable when the boom ended."

## Common misconceptions

- **"Tet was a Communist military victory."** It was a military defeat and a political victory.
- **"Congress declared war on North Vietnam."** It never did; the Tonkin Resolution was the authority.
- **"The Great Society was only about poverty."** It also covered health, education, immigration, culture and civil rights.
- **"Détente ended the Cold War."** It eased tension for a few years; the arms race and proxy conflicts continued.
