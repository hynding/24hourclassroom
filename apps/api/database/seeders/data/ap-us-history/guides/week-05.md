## American Indians: resistance, accommodation, diplomacy (2.5)

Native nations met colonization with every tool they had, and their choices shaped the colonies.

**Wars of resistance against English expansion.** In the Chesapeake, the Powhatan under Opechancanough struck the tobacco settlements in **1622**, killing about a quarter of the colonists, and again in **1644**; English reprisals broke Powhatan power. In New England, the **Pequot War** (1636–1637) ended with the burning of the Pequot fort at Mystic and the opening of southern New England to settlement. Forty years later the Wampanoag leader **Metacom** (King Philip) led a coalition against English land-taking, colonial courts and missionary pressure. **Metacom's War** (1675–1676) was among the deadliest wars in American history relative to population. The English won; many Native survivors were sold into slavery in the West Indies.

**Bacon's Rebellion (1676)** is a Native-relations story and a class story at once. Frontier settlers and former servants in Virginia wanted Governor William Berkeley to let them attack American Indians and take their land; Berkeley, who profited from Native trade, refused. Nathaniel Bacon's followers attacked Native peoples, friendly and hostile alike, and burned Jamestown. Bacon died of disease and the rebellion collapsed, but it frightened planters (see 2.6).

**The Pueblo Revolt (1680).** In New Mexico, decades of forced labor, tribute and suppression of Pueblo religion, worsened by drought and Apache raids, ended in a coordinated uprising led by **Popé**. About four hundred Spaniards, including twenty-one friars, were killed, and the survivors fled south. When Diego de Vargas returned in 1692, Spanish rule resumed with more **accommodation**: lighter labor demands, more tolerance of Pueblo ceremony, and Pueblo–Spanish alliance against Apache and Comanche raiders.

**Trade, war and diplomacy in the north.** The fur trade reshaped Native politics. Armed by the Dutch, the **Haudenosaunee** fought the **Beaver Wars** against the Huron and others to control the trade and to replace people lost to disease. In 1701 they made peace with the French at Montreal while keeping their alliance with the English, and for half a century they practised **play-off diplomacy**, staying neutral and making both empires court them.

**Comparison (2.8).** The pattern from week 3 holds: Spain, with few settlers and a need for Native labor, was forced into accommodation; France, needing trading partners, built alliances; England, with many settlers wanting land, fought repeated wars of displacement. The 1715 **Yamasee War** in South Carolina, fought partly over the Carolina trade in enslaved American Indians, shows the British pattern in the South.

## Slavery in the British colonies (2.6)

**Scale.** Roughly twelve and a half million Africans were forced aboard slave ships between the 1500s and the 1860s; about ten and a half million survived the crossing. Most went to Brazil and the Caribbean. Fewer than 400,000 landed in mainland North America, where, unlike the Caribbean, the enslaved population later grew mainly through births.

**From servants to slaves in the Chesapeake.** Africans arrived in Virginia in 1619, but for decades most bound laborers were English servants, and the status of Africans was not yet fixed: some gained freedom and even land. From about 1680 the balance shifted, for several reasons working together:

1. **Supply of servants fell:** wages in England rose and emigration slowed.
2. **Political fear:** Bacon's Rebellion showed the danger of a class of armed, landless former servants.
3. **Supply of enslaved Africans rose:** the Royal African Company's monopoly ended in 1698, and private traders brought more captives.
4. **Longer lives:** falling death rates made a lifetime of labor a better investment than a few years of a servant's.

**Law made slavery racial and hereditary.** Virginia's law of **1662** made a child's status follow the mother's; a 1667 law said baptism did not free a person; the **slave code of 1705** gathered such laws into one, defining enslaved people as property and sharpening legal lines between white servants and Black people. In the Lower South, Carolina copied **Barbados**'s slave code.

**Regions.** Slavery was legal in every colony. In the Lower South enslaved Africans were a majority; in the Chesapeake a large and rising minority; in the North a smaller share, many working in port cities (New York had a revolt in 1712).

**Resistance.** Enslaved people resisted daily: working slowly, breaking tools, feigning illness, running away, and keeping African languages, music, beliefs and family ties alive (**Gullah** culture in the Lowcountry). Open revolt was rare and deadly. In the **Stono Rebellion** (1739), enslaved men near Charleston, many probably from Kongo, seized weapons and marched toward Spanish Florida, where Spain offered freedom at **Fort Mose**. The militia crushed them, and the **Negro Act of 1740** imposed harsher controls.

## Colonial society and culture (2.7)

**The Great Awakening (1730s–1740s).** Revival preachers called on listeners to recognise their sinfulness and seek a personal experience of God's grace. **Jonathan Edwards** led a revival in Northampton, Massachusetts, in the mid-1730s. **George Whitefield**, an English Anglican, toured the colonies from 1739, preaching outdoors to crowds of thousands. Benjamin Franklin, no revivalist himself, wrote in his *Autobiography* (paraphrased) that after Whitefield's visits the people of Philadelphia seemed to have grown religious all at once, so that hymns could be heard from house after house in the evening. The revivals split congregations into **New Lights** and **Old Lights**, created new churches and colleges (Princeton, 1746), drew in women and enslaved Africans, and taught colonists that ordinary believers could judge established authorities.

**The Enlightenment.** Educated colonists absorbed European ideas that reason and science could explain the world and improve society. **John Locke** argued that government rests on consent and exists to protect natural rights. **Franklin** embodied the movement: printer, founder of a subscription library, author of *Poor Richard's Almanack*, scientist.

*Tell them apart:* the Great Awakening appealed to emotion and faith and reached all classes; the Enlightenment appealed to reason and reached mainly the educated. Both encouraged people to question authority.

**Immigration and diversity.** In the 1700s, Germans fleeing war and poverty and **Scots-Irish** Presbyterians fleeing rents and hard times poured through Philadelphia into the Pennsylvania backcountry and down the Appalachian valleys. Penn's **Charter of Privileges** (1701), protecting anyone who acknowledged one God, drew them.

**Anglicization and politics.** Colonists bought British goods, read newspapers full of London news, built Anglican churches and modelled their assemblies on the House of Commons. They thought of themselves as Britons with English rights. The **Zenger trial** (1735) advanced freedom of the press when a New York jury acquitted a printer who had criticised the governor.

**Patriarchy and family.** Colonial law and custom subordinated women: married women generally could not own property or sign contracts in their own names. The **Salem witch trials** (1692), in which nineteen people were hanged, reflected Puritan fears, local tensions and frontier war.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Olaudah Equiano, *The Interesting Narrative* (1789)**

> "The closeness of the place, and the heat of the climate, added to the number in the ship, which was so crowded that each had scarcely room to turn himself, almost suffocated us. ... The shrieks of the women, and the groans of the dying, rendered the whole a scene of horror almost inconceivable."

*HIPP.* **Historical situation:** published in London as the British campaign against the slave trade grew. **Audience:** British readers and members of Parliament. **Purpose:** to expose the trade's cruelty and support abolition. **Point of view:** a formerly enslaved man who had bought his freedom. Historians debate whether Equiano was born in Africa, as he wrote, or in South Carolina, as some records suggest; his description matches other evidence of the Middle Passage. *Why it matters:* a rare first-person account by a survivor, written to change policy.

**2. William Penn, Charter of Privileges (1701)**

> "BECAUSE no People can be truly happy, though under the greatest Enjoyment of Civil Liberties, if abridged of the Freedom of their Consciences, as to their Religious Profession and Worship ... That no Person or Persons, inhabiting in this Province or Territories, who shall confess and acknowledge One almighty God ... shall be in any Case molested or prejudiced ... because of his or their conscientious Persuasion or Practice ..."

*HIPP.* **Point of view:** a Quaker proprietor whose sect had been persecuted. **Purpose:** to protect liberty of conscience and attract settlers. *Why it matters:* broader than Maryland's 1649 act (all monotheists, not only Christians), and part of why Pennsylvania became so diverse.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: sourcing with HIPP.** Don't just label; explain how the label affects the source's use. "Because Equiano wrote in 1789 for a British audience during the campaign against the slave trade, he had reason to stress the trade's horrors; but his details agree with ship records and other survivors' accounts, so the source is strong evidence of Middle Passage conditions."

**Worked example 2: a causation chain (Chesapeake labor).** Tobacco boom → demand for labor → indentured servants (1610s–1670s) → freed servants want land → Bacon's Rebellion (1676) → planters fear poor whites; English emigration falls; more enslaved Africans available after 1698 → shift to slavery → laws of 1662, 1667, 1705 make slavery racial and hereditary → a society divided by race in which poor whites gain status above all Black people.

**Worked example 3: a comparison thesis (2.8).** Prompt: *Evaluate the extent of similarity in how Spanish, French and British colonists interacted with American Indians, 1607–1754.* Thesis: "All three empires depended on Native trade and allies and justified colonization with religion, but they differed more than they resembled each other: Spain's need for labor led to coercion and then accommodation after the Pueblo Revolt, France's need for furs led to alliance and intermarriage, and Britain's hunger for land led to repeated wars of displacement."

## Common misconceptions

- **"Slavery in Virginia was fully formed in 1619."** Laws making slavery racial, lifelong and hereditary came over the following eighty years.
- **"Enslaved people did not resist."** Resistance was constant, mostly in everyday forms.
- **"The Great Awakening and the Enlightenment were the same."** One stressed emotion and faith, the other reason.
- **"Native peoples always lost."** The Pueblos expelled Spain for twelve years and won concessions; the Haudenosaunee held the balance of power for decades.
