- **7.1** Explain the context of 1890–1945: how an industrial, increasingly urban nation became a world power and how Americans argued over the proper role of government at home and abroad.

- **7.3** Explain the causes and effects of the Spanish–American War, including the war in the Philippines and the new protectorate over Cuba.
- **7.4** Explain the causes and effects of the Progressive movement, its goals (democracy, efficiency, social justice, regulation) and its limits.

In plain language: know why the United States reached for overseas territory after 1890, who said no and why, what the war of 1898 won and cost, and how middle-class reformers tried to tame big business, clean up politics and protect workers, consumers and the land, while often leaving African Americans and immigrants outside the circle of reform.

## Period 7 in one paragraph (7.1)

Between 1890 and 1945 the United States changed three ways at once. Economically, it became an urban, mass-consumer society; the 1920 census was the first to count more Americans in towns and cities than on farms. Politically, Americans argued over how much government should do: Progressives built regulatory agencies, wartime mobilization showed what a powerful state could do, the 1920s pulled back, and the New Deal made Washington responsible for the economy and a basic floor of security. Internationally, a regional power with a small army became the strongest country on earth. Every shift was contested. Frame any Period 7 essay around one of them and the arguments it generated.

## Why the United States looked outward (7.2)

**Economic motives.** Industrial output outran domestic demand, and the depression that began in 1893 made the surplus feel like a crisis. Manufacturers and farmers wanted overseas markets, especially the imagined market of China. Hawaii's American sugar planters wanted to sell sugar duty-free in the United States; in 1893 they overthrew Queen Liliuokalani with help from U.S. Marines, and in 1898 Congress annexed the islands by joint resolution.

**Strategic motives.** Alfred Thayer Mahan argued that great powers rest on sea power: a steel navy, Pacific coaling stations and a Central American canal. By 1898 the new navy could fight a European power.

**Ideological motives.** Turner's frontier thesis suggested the nation needed new outlets. Social Darwinism applied to nations, Anglo-Saxon racial pride and Protestant missionary zeal all suggested that "advanced" peoples had a duty to rule "backward" ones. Senator Albert Beveridge put it bluntly in 1898: "the rule of liberty that all just government derives its authority from the consent of the governed, applies only to those who are capable of self-government."

**The critics.** The Anti-Imperialist League (1898) gathered an unlikely coalition: Mark Twain, the steel magnate Andrew Carnegie, Jane Addams, labor leader Samuel Gompers and many former abolitionists. Their strongest argument was constitutional and moral: a republic founded on the consent of the governed could not rule colonies. Some also feared militarism, and some (an argument you should note honestly) feared that annexing nonwhite peoples or importing their labor threatened white workers and racial order. Anti-imperialism was not the same thing as racial egalitarianism.

## The war of 1898 and its aftermath (7.3)

Cubans had rebelled against Spain in 1895, and Spain's "reconcentration" of civilians into camps killed tens of thousands. The yellow press magnified every atrocity. In February 1898 the battleship *Maine* exploded in Havana harbor; Americans blamed Spain (the cause was never proven), and in April Congress declared war, adding the **Teller Amendment** promising not to annex Cuba.

The fighting was short: Dewey destroyed the Spanish fleet at Manila Bay in May, and the army, including the Rough Riders and veteran Black regiments, took Santiago, Cuba, in July. Disease killed far more U.S. soldiers than combat. In the **Treaty of Paris** Spain gave up Cuba and ceded Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines.

The aftermath was longer and uglier. Filipino nationalists led by Emilio Aguinaldo, who had declared independence from Spain, fought U.S. annexation. The **Philippine–American War** (1899–1902) became a guerrilla war; U.S. forces concentrated civilians, burned villages and used torture, and at least 200,000 Filipinos died, many from disease and famine. In Cuba, the **Platt Amendment** made independence conditional: the United States could intervene and kept Guantánamo Bay. The Supreme Court's **Insular Cases** let Congress govern Puerto Rico and the Philippines without granting full constitutional rights.

Roosevelt then backed Panama's revolt against Colombia (1903) to get a canal zone; the canal opened in 1914. His **Roosevelt Corollary** (1904) claimed a police power over the hemisphere, Taft's **dollar diplomacy** used loans to the same end, and Marines occupied Nicaragua, Haiti and the Dominican Republic for years. In East Asia, Hay's **Open Door notes** sought trade without colonies.

## The Progressives (7.4)

**Who they were.** Progressives were mostly urban, middle-class and educated: journalists, lawyers, Social Gospel ministers, social workers, clubwomen, and some business leaders who wanted stable markets. They trusted experts and active government, and took up Populist demands (direct election of senators, an income tax) for cities rather than farms.

**Exposure.** Muckrakers made abuses visible: Riis's tenement photographs, Ida Tarbell on Standard Oil, Lincoln Steffens on city machines, and Upton Sinclair's *The Jungle* (1906), which produced the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act within months.

**Democracy and efficiency.** In the states, Robert La Follette's Wisconsin and others adopted the direct primary, the initiative, the referendum and the recall; cities tried commission and city-manager government. The **Seventeenth Amendment** (1913) made senators directly elected. Women's suffrage grew state by state in the West and became national in 1920.

**Social justice.** Settlement-house women such as Jane Addams and Florence Kelley pushed for child labor laws and workplace safety; the Triangle Shirtwaist fire (1911) killed 146 garment workers and produced a wave of New York factory laws. *Muller v. Oregon* (1908) upheld hours limits for women, by reasoning that defined women by motherhood.

**Regulation at the federal level.** Roosevelt's **Square Deal** revived the Sherman Act against the Northern Securities trust, strengthened railroad regulation (Hepburn Act, 1906), protected consumers and conserved public lands. In 1912 Roosevelt's **New Nationalism** (regulate big business with a strong state) faced Wilson's **New Freedom** (restore competition), Taft and the socialist Eugene Debs. Wilson won and delivered an income tax, the **Federal Reserve Act** (1913), the **Clayton Antitrust Act** and the **Federal Trade Commission** (1914).

**Limits.** Wilson's administration segregated federal offices; southern Progressives called disfranchisement "clean elections"; many reformers backed literacy tests, immigration restriction and eugenic sterilisation. African Americans organised on their own: Ida B. Wells's anti-lynching campaign, Du Bois's Niagara Movement (1905) and the interracial **NAACP** (1909).

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Theodore Roosevelt, Annual Message to Congress, December 6, 1904**

> "Chronic wrongdoing, or an impotence which results in a general loosening of the ties of civilized society, may in America, as elsewhere, ultimately require intervention by some civilized nation, and in the Western Hemisphere the adherence of the United States to the Monroe Doctrine may force the United States, however reluctantly, in flagrant cases of such wrongdoing or impotence, to the exercise of an international police power."

*HIPP.* Historical situation: European creditors had blockaded Venezuela (1902–1903) and the Dominican Republic was defaulting on its debts. Audience: Congress and, beyond it, European governments. Purpose: to pre-empt European intervention by claiming the job for the United States. Point of view: a president who believed in a hierarchy of "civilized" nations. *Why it matters:* it turns the Monroe Doctrine from a shield into a licence for intervention.

**2. Platform of the American Anti-Imperialist League, October 1899**

> "We hold that the policy known as imperialism is hostile to liberty and tends toward militarism, an evil from which it has been our glory to be free."

*HIPP.* Written during the Philippine–American War for voters before the 1900 election. *Why it matters:* expansion was contested from the start, on founding principles.

**3. W. E. B. Du Bois, *The Souls of Black Folk*, 1903**

> "Mr. Washington represents in Negro thought the old attitude of adjustment and submission ..."

*HIPP.* A Harvard-trained sociologist writing for a national, largely white, reading public at the low point of Jim Crow. Purpose: to break Booker T. Washington's hold on Black leadership and argue for civil and political rights. *Why it matters:* it marks the turn toward the Niagara Movement and the NAACP.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: a contextualization paragraph.** Prompt: *Evaluate the extent to which U.S. foreign policy changed between 1890 and 1914.*

> For most of the nineteenth century American expansion meant moving west across the continent, and foreign policy rested on Washington's warning against entangling alliances and Monroe's warning to Europe. By the 1890s that world was ending. The frontier was declared closed, the depression of 1893 convinced many business leaders that the nation produced more than it could sell at home, and Mahan argued that great nations needed navies and bases while Europe divided Africa and Asia.

Why it works: it starts before the period, names specific developments, and ends where the prompt begins.

**Worked example 2: an SAQ answered part by part.** *(a) Briefly describe ONE difference between the strategies of Booker T. Washington and W. E. B. Du Bois.* "Washington urged African Americans to accept segregation for the time being and build economic strength through vocational training, while Du Bois demanded immediate civil and political rights and higher education for leaders." *(b) Briefly explain ONE reason Washington's strategy appealed to many Americans in the 1890s.* "Where lynching made open protest dangerous, it promised Black progress without provoking white violence, and it drew funding from white philanthropists such as Andrew Carnegie." One claim, one specific, each time.

**Worked example 3: turning a prompt into a defensible thesis.** Prompt: *Evaluate the extent to which the Progressive movement changed the role of the federal government, 1890–1920.* Weak: "The Progressives changed the government a lot." Strong: "Progressives significantly expanded the federal government's role as a regulator of business, food and money, through laws such as the Meat Inspection Act and the Federal Reserve Act, but the change was limited because courts still struck down labor laws, most social reform stayed at the state level, and reform excluded African Americans." The strong version answers "to what extent", names categories of evidence, and builds in a limit.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The Spanish–American War was mainly fought in Cuba, so its main result was Cuban independence."** The longest and deadliest fighting came afterwards, in the Philippines, and Cuba's independence was limited by the Platt Amendment.
- **"Anti-imperialists were all racial egalitarians."** Many were; some opposed empire because they did not want nonwhite peoples inside the nation.
- **"Progressives wanted to destroy big business."** Most wanted to regulate it; even Wilson's antitrust laws targeted unfair practices more than size.
- **"The Roosevelt Corollary was part of the original Monroe Doctrine."** It was added in 1904, eighty-one years later.
