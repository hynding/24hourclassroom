- **6.1** Explain the historical context for the rapid industrialization, urbanization and western expansion of the years 1865–1898.

## Context: a nation remade, 1865–1898

Period 6 begins as the Civil War ends and closes with the war against Spain. In those three decades the United States became the world's largest industrial economy. Railroads stitched together a national market; steel, oil and electricity created new industries; corporations grew larger than any private organization the country had known. Cities swelled with migrants from the countryside and from Europe, Asia and Latin America. And the trans-Mississippi West, still largely controlled by Native nations in 1865, was conquered, surveyed, fenced and connected to world markets.

These changes raised the questions that run through this unit: who should benefit from growth, and what did government owe workers, farmers, freedpeople and American Indians? The West is the first place to see them.

## Building the western economy

**Federal power came first.** The settlement of the West is often told as a story of rugged individuals, but the federal government shaped it at every step. In 1862, with Southern Democrats gone from Congress, Republicans passed the Homestead Act, the Pacific Railway Act and the Morrill Land-Grant College Act, a program for a West of free farmers linked to eastern markets. Congress and the states eventually granted railroads well over 100 million acres. The army fought the wars that confined Native nations to reservations.

**Railroads made the market.** The first transcontinental line was finished in 1869, when the Central Pacific, built east from California largely by Chinese workers, met the Union Pacific, built west largely by Irish and other European immigrants and Civil War veterans. Track mileage roughly tripled between 1870 and 1890. Railroads sold their land grants, advertised in Europe for settlers, set freight rates that could make or break a town and in 1883 even created the standard time zones.

**Mining, ranching and farming** followed the rails. Mining booms in Nevada, Colorado, the Dakotas and Montana began with individual prospectors and ended with corporations, deep shafts, wage labor and eastern or British capital. The cattle kingdom depended on railroads too: from 1867 cowboys, many of them Mexican American and African American, drove Texas longhorns to Kansas railheads for the Chicago packinghouses. Overgrazing, falling prices, the terrible winter of 1886–87 and barbed wire ended the open range.

Farmers came last and stayed. Steel plows, windmills, barbed wire and mechanized reapers made Plains farming possible on a large scale. But commercial farming tied families to forces they could not control: world crop prices that fell for most of the period, mortgage interest, and railroad and grain-elevator rates where one company held a monopoly. The **Grange** (1867) turned that frustration into cooperatives and state laws regulating rates, upheld by the Supreme Court in *Munn v. Illinois* (1877). Week 21 follows this grievance to the Populists.

## Conquest and its consequences for American Indians

For the Lakota, Cheyenne, Comanche, Apache, Nez Perce and dozens of other nations, these decades were a struggle for survival. The **destruction of the bison**, from tens of millions to a few hundred by the late 1880s, by hide hunters with army encouragement, destroyed the economic base of independent life on the Plains.

**Wars and treaties** alternated. Colorado militia massacred Cheyenne and Arapaho people at Sand Creek in 1864. The Lakota under Red Cloud forced the United States to abandon forts on the Bozeman Trail, and the **Treaty of Fort Laramie** (1868) set apart the Great Sioux Reservation, including the Black Hills. After an army expedition found gold there in 1874, miners poured in; the Lakota and Cheyenne destroyed Custer's command at the **Little Bighorn** in 1876, and the government seized the Black Hills the next year. The Nez Perce under Chief Joseph fled more than a thousand miles before surrendering in 1877; the Apache leader Geronimo surrendered in 1886. In 1871 Congress stopped making treaties altogether, treating Native nations as subjects of domestic law rather than as foreign powers.

**From reservation to assimilation.** By the 1880s white reformers who called themselves the Friends of the Indian, moved by exposures such as Helen Hunt Jackson's *A Century of Dishonor* (1881), argued that Native people would survive only by becoming individual Christian farmers. Their program had two parts. Boarding schools, starting with Richard Henry Pratt's **Carlisle Indian Industrial School** (1879), took children from their families, cut their hair, changed their names and forbade their languages. The **Dawes Act** (1887) broke reservations into individual allotments and sold the 'surplus' to white settlers. The results were catastrophic: Native-held land fell from about 138 million acres in 1887 to about 48 million by 1934.

**Native responses** were varied. Some nations fought; some fled; some negotiated. The **Ghost Dance**, spread by the Paiute prophet Wovoka, promised renewal of the world and the return of the dead. Federal agents feared it; the attempt to arrest Sitting Bull ended in his death, and on December 29, 1890, the cavalry killed roughly 150 to 300 Lakota at **Wounded Knee**. Yet Native peoples persisted, keeping languages and ceremonies alive and defending what remained in courts and in Congress.

## Many Wests: other peoples on the move

The West was also home to Mexican Americans whose families had lived there for generations. The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848) promised to protect their property, but many **Californios** and **Tejanos** lost land to costly title proceedings, squatters and taxes. In New Mexico, Las Gorras Blancas cut the fences that enclosed common grazing land in 1889–90.

Chinese immigrants built railroads and worked mines, and faced violence and, in 1882, federal exclusion (week 20). African Americans left the post-Reconstruction South: in 1879 tens of thousands of **Exodusters** moved to Kansas. Wyoming Territory granted women the vote in 1869. And a conservation impulse appeared: Congress created Yellowstone, the first national park, in 1872, and John Muir founded the Sierra Club in 1892.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Treaty of Fort Laramie (1868), Articles I and II.**

> "From this day forward all war between the parties to this agreement shall forever cease." ... [the reservation] "shall be and the same is set apart for the absolute and undisturbed use and occupation of the Indians herein named ..."

*Sourcing (HIPP):* a treaty negotiated after Red Cloud's War, when the Lakota had forced the army to withdraw. Its audience is both governments; its purpose, peace on terms the Lakota won. *Why it matters:* the word "absolute" lasted six years, until gold was found in the Black Hills. The treaty shows that the United States once dealt with the Lakota as a sovereign power, and its breach shows how quickly economic pressure overrode that recognition.

**2. Dawes Act (1887), Sections 1 and 6.**

> "... to allot the lands in said reservation in severalty to any Indian located thereon in quantities as follows: To each head of a family, one-quarter of a section ..." Section 6 declares a citizen every Indian "who has voluntarily taken up, within said limits, his residence separate and apart from any tribe of Indians therein, and has adopted the habits of civilized life ..."

*Sourcing:* Congress, urged on by reformers; see worked example 2. *Why it matters:* assimilation was a policy of land transfer as much as of culture.

**3. Frederick Jackson Turner (1893).**

> "Up to our own day American history has been in a large degree the history of the colonization of the Great West. The existence of an area of free land, its continuous recession, and the advance of American settlement westward, explain American development."

*Sourcing:* a young historian addressing other historians at the 1893 world's fair in Chicago, three years after the census said the frontier line had disappeared. Purpose: to make the West, not Europe or the slavery conflict, the key to the American past. *Why it matters:* it became the most influential interpretation of American history for half a century, and its blind spot ('free land') is the place to start any critique.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: a contextualization paragraph for any Period 6 essay.**
Prompt: "Evaluate the extent to which federal policy shaped the development of the West, 1865–1898."
*Context:* "Before the Civil War, Southern opposition in Congress had blocked a free-homestead law and a northern route for a Pacific railroad, and the trans-Mississippi West remained largely under the control of Native nations. Secession changed that: in 1862 a Republican Congress passed the Homestead and Pacific Railway Acts, laying the legal foundation for settlement. After 1865, as the industrializing North demanded food, metals and new markets, the federal government turned its army and its land office toward the West."
It sets the scene before the prompt's period and leads to the thesis.

**Worked example 2: sourcing the Dawes Act.**
Point of view: Congress and reformers who believed communal life kept Native people poor. Purpose: to make American Indians individual farmers and citizens, and to open 'surplus' land to settlers. Historical situation: the Plains wars were ending and reformers wanted an alternative to extermination. Using it: "The act's promise of citizenship only to those who adopted 'the habits of civilized life' shows that reformers' generosity was conditional on cultural surrender."

**Worked example 3: a causation chain.**
Transcontinental railroad (1869) → hide hunters and settlers reach the Plains → bison nearly destroyed by the late 1880s → Plains nations lose their economic base → confinement on shrinking reservations → reformers push allotment and boarding schools → spiritual revival in the Ghost Dance → federal suppression → Wounded Knee (1890). Each arrow is a claim you could support with one piece of evidence.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The West was settled by individuals without government help."** The federal government granted the land, subsidized the railroads, fought the wars and ran the reservations.
- **"The frontier was empty land."** It was home to Native nations and Mexican American communities; 'free land' was taken land.
- **"Reformers who supported the Dawes Act wanted to harm Native people."** Most believed they were helping. The lesson is that good intentions plus a belief in cultural superiority produced dispossession.
- **"Indian resistance ended in 1890."** Armed resistance on the Plains largely did; cultural, legal and political resistance continued and continues.
