## Whose Reconstruction? President against Congress

**Lincoln's plan.** In December 1863 Lincoln offered pardons to most Confederates and readmission once 10% of a state's 1860 voters swore loyalty and accepted emancipation. Congress's **Wade-Davis Bill** (1864) demanded a loyal majority; Lincoln pocket-vetoed it. Before his death he had begun to speak publicly of the vote for some Black men, especially veterans.

**Johnson's plan (1865).** Andrew Johnson, a Tennessee Democrat who hated the planter elite but shared white Southern views on race, pardoned most Confederates (thousands of wealthy planters among them), required the states to abolish slavery and repudiate secession, and let white Southerners rebuild their governments. They elected former Confederate leaders to Congress and passed **Black Codes**: annual labor contracts, vagrancy laws that let sheriffs arrest unemployed Black men and hire them out, limits on the jobs Black people could hold. Johnson also ordered land that freedpeople had been settled on under Sherman's **Special Field Order No. 15** returned to its former owners. To Northerners who had just won a war, the South seemed to be restoring slavery under another name.

**Congress takes over (1866–1867).** When Congress met in December 1865 it refused to seat the Southern delegations. It extended the **Freedmen's Bureau** and passed the **Civil Rights Act of 1866**, which made all persons born in the United States citizens with equal civil rights. Johnson vetoed both, arguing that the bill made "the distinction of race and color ... operate in favor of the colored and against the white race." Congress overrode the civil rights veto, then wrote its principles into the **Fourteenth Amendment** so no future Congress or court could undo them. After Johnson campaigned against the amendment and Republicans won the 1866 elections decisively, Congress passed the **Reconstruction Acts** (1867): ten states were placed in five military districts and had to write new constitutions, elected by Black and white men, and ratify the Fourteenth Amendment. When Johnson obstructed enforcement, the House **impeached** him in 1868; the Senate acquitted him by a single vote. The **Fifteenth Amendment** (1870) barred denial of the vote by race, though it left room for literacy tests and poll taxes. It also split the women's movement: Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Susan B. Anthony opposed an amendment that enfranchised Black men but not women, while Lucy Stone supported it.

## What freedom meant

Freedpeople did not wait for Washington. They searched for spouses, parents and children sold away, legalised marriages, and moved, sometimes just off the old plantation, to show that they could. They founded independent churches, which became the centre of community and political life. They built schools with the Freedmen's Bureau and Northern missionary societies; colleges such as Fisk (1866) and Howard (1867) date from these years. Many families withdrew women and children from field work.

**Politics.** Under the Reconstruction Acts, Black men voted in large numbers and held office. Sixteen served in Congress, including Senators Hiram Revels and Blanche K. Bruce of Mississippi; many hundreds served in state legislatures and local posts. Biracial Republican governments, with Northern newcomers (whom opponents called carpetbaggers) and white Southern Republicans (scalawags), wrote new constitutions, created the South's first public school systems, and expanded hospitals and railroads. Higher taxes and some corruption gave opponents a weapon, though corruption was common in Northern politics of the same years.

**Land and labor.** Freedpeople wanted land, the basis of independence; Congress never redistributed it. Planters wanted gang labor; freedpeople refused to work under overseers. **Sharecropping** was the compromise: a family worked a plot and paid a share of the crop, often half, to the owner. It gave families autonomy over their daily work, but combined with the **crop-lien** system, in which merchants advanced supplies on credit secured by the next crop, it trapped many Black and white farmers in debt and in cotton.

## Why Reconstruction failed

**Terror.** The **Ku Klux Klan**, founded in Tennessee in 1866, whipped and murdered Black voters, officeholders and teachers and their white allies. Grant asked Congress for power to stop it, and the **Enforcement Acts** and **Ku Klux Klan Act** (1870–71) made conspiracies against civil rights federal crimes. Grant suspended habeas corpus in nine South Carolina counties; federal prosecutions broke the first Klan. But paramilitary violence returned in new forms: the **Colfax Massacre** (1873), the White League in Louisiana, the rifle clubs of the **Mississippi Plan** (1875), the Red Shirts in South Carolina.

**The courts.** In the **Slaughterhouse Cases** (1873) the Supreme Court read the Fourteenth Amendment's privileges or immunities clause narrowly. In **United States v. Cruikshank** (1876), arising from Colfax, it held that the amendment restrained states, not private individuals, which left protection from private violence to Southern authorities who would not provide it.

**Northern retreat.** The **Panic of 1873** began a long depression; Democrats won the House in 1874. Scandals in the Grant administration and Liberal Republican calls for amnesty and local self-government sapped Northern will. When Mississippi's governor asked for troops in 1875, Grant declined. The **Civil Rights Act of 1875**, banning discrimination in public accommodations, was barely enforced.

**The end.** In 1876 Democrat Samuel Tilden won the popular vote, but returns from South Carolina, Florida and Louisiana were disputed. An electoral commission gave all twenty disputed electoral votes to Republican Rutherford B. Hayes, 185 to 184. In the **Compromise of 1877**, Democrats accepted Hayes, and federal troops stopped protecting the last Republican governments. Redeemer Democrats now ruled every Southern state.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Thirteenth Amendment, Section 1 (1865).**

> "Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude, except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted, shall exist within the United States, or any place subject to their jurisdiction."

*HIPP.* Proposed by Congress in January 1865 to make emancipation permanent. Why it matters: the exception clause was used by Southern states, through vagrancy laws and convict leasing, to compel Black labor after Reconstruction.

**2. Andrew Johnson, veto of the Civil Rights Bill (March 27, 1866).**

> "They establish for the security of the colored race safeguards which go infinitely beyond any that the General Government has ever provided for the white race."

*HIPP.* A states' rights Democrat addressing Congress and the Northern public, arguing that the bill was unconstitutional and unfair to white Americans. Why it matters: his vetoes drove moderates into the Radical camp.

**3. Ulysses S. Grant, special message to Congress (March 23, 1871).**

> "A condition of affairs now exists in some of the States of the Union rendering life and property insecure and the carrying of the mails and the collection of the revenue dangerous."

*HIPP.* The president, a former Union commander, asking Congress for power to act against Klan violence. Why it matters: it led to the Ku Klux Klan Act, the high point of federal enforcement.

**4. United States v. Cruikshank (1876).**

> "The Fourteenth Amendment prohibits a State from depriving any person of life, liberty, or property without due process of law, but this adds nothing to the rights of one citizen as against another."

*HIPP.* The Supreme Court, ruling on convictions from the Colfax Massacre. Why it matters: the state-action doctrine left Black Southerners without federal protection against private violence.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: sourcing Johnson's veto.** Point of view: a Southern Unionist Democrat who believed in states' rights and white supremacy. Purpose: to defeat a bill and rally Northern opinion against the Republican Congress. Audience: Congress and the voters of the 1866 elections. Historical situation: the Black Codes had just been passed, and Northerners were debating whether the war's results were secure. Effect: the argument backfired; Congress overrode the veto and voters gave Republicans veto-proof majorities.

**Worked example 2: an SAQ answered part by part (Cruikshank).**

- (a) *Describe the reasoning:* "The Court held that the Fourteenth Amendment limits what state governments may do, but does not give the federal government power to punish private individuals who violate other citizens' rights."
- (b) *Explain one effect:* "Because Louisiana's courts would not punish white attackers, the Colfax killers went free, and paramilitary groups such as the White League could intimidate Black voters without fear of federal prosecution."
- (c) *Explain one other cause of Reconstruction's end:* "The Panic of 1873 shifted Northern attention to the depression and helped Democrats win the House in 1874, weakening support for intervention."

Each part does exactly what its verb asks, with one specific piece of evidence.

**Worked example 3: a comparison thesis for 5.12.** Prompt: *Compare the Compromise of 1850 and the Compromise of 1877.*

> Both compromises traded concessions between sections to preserve the Union and the authority of the national government, but they differed in who paid the price: in 1850 white Northerners accepted a harsher Fugitive Slave Act to keep slavery's expansion from causing disunion, while in 1877 Republicans abandoned Black Southerners' rights to end a constitutional crisis. In both, the interests of African Americans were bargained away for sectional peace.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Reconstruction failed because Black officeholders were incompetent."** That claim belongs to a discredited school of history. Black officials served ably; Reconstruction was overthrown by violence and abandoned by the North.
- **"Freedpeople received forty acres."** A few did briefly under Field Order No. 15; Johnson took most of that land back, and redistribution never became policy.
- **"The Fifteenth Amendment guaranteed the right to vote."** It barred racial discrimination in voting but not race-neutral devices like literacy tests and poll taxes.
- **"Reconstruction achieved nothing lasting."** The amendments stayed in the Constitution and were the legal foundation of twentieth-century civil rights victories.
- **"Johnson and the Radicals disagreed only about punishment."** The deepest disagreement was over Black citizenship and suffrage.
