## Four regions (and an island fifth)

### New England: covenant communities

Puritan families came to New England to build godly communities. Their environment (a short growing season, thin rocky soil, plenty of timber, good harbours and rich fishing grounds) did not suit a staple crop, so New Englanders farmed small plots for their own use, fished, cut timber, built ships and traded. They lived in compact **towns** around the meetinghouse, where the same men who worshipped together also governed through the **town meeting**. A cold climate and clean water meant New Englanders lived far longer than other colonists, and a balanced sex ratio meant large families: after the Great Migration of the 1630s, the region grew mainly by births.

Puritan unity was always contested. **Roger Williams** argued that magistrates should not punish religious offences and that colonists must buy Native land fairly; banished, he founded Providence in 1636, and Rhode Island became a haven for dissenters. **Anne Hutchinson** taught that grace came through inner experience and that most ministers had it wrong; she was banished in 1638, partly because a woman teaching men alarmed the colony's leaders. As the second generation reported fewer conversion experiences, churches adopted the **Halfway Covenant** (1662). The Salem witch trials of 1692 (week 5) revealed the region's anxieties.

### The Middle Colonies: diversity and grain

New York (formerly New Netherland), New Jersey, Pennsylvania and Delaware had fertile soil and a longer growing season. Family farms grew wheat and other grains for export, much of it as flour to the West Indies, earning the region the nickname "breadbasket". Philadelphia and New York became major ports.

The defining feature was **diversity**. New York kept its Dutch population and its tradition of commercial tolerance. William Penn, a Quaker, received Pennsylvania in 1681 and offered religious freedom, an elected assembly, cheap land and, during his lifetime, comparatively fair dealing with the Lenape. Quakers, Germans, Welsh, Scots-Irish and Dutch made it the most varied region in British America.

### The Chesapeake: tobacco and bound labor

Virginia and Maryland were built on **tobacco**. The crop exhausted soil quickly and rewarded whoever held the most land and laborers, so planters spread out along the tidewater rivers, shipping from their own docks; there were few towns. The **headright system** gave land to whoever paid laborers' passage, concentrating land and labor among the wealthy.

For most of the 1600s the labor came from **indentured servants**: young, poor, mostly male English migrants who sold four to seven years of work for passage. Malaria and other diseases killed many before their terms ended; men far outnumbered women; families were fragile. From the 1670s planters shifted to **enslaved Africans** (week 5). Politically, Virginia had the first elected assembly, the **House of Burgesses** (1619), and the Church of England was established there. Maryland, granted to the Catholic Lord Baltimore in 1632, needed Protestant settlers, which is why it passed the **Toleration Act** of 1649.

### The Lower South: rice, indigo and an African majority

Carolina (chartered 1663; Charles Town founded 1670) was settled in large part by planters from **Barbados**, who brought the Caribbean's plantation model and its harsh slave code. Enslaved West Africans, some from rice-growing regions, contributed knowledge that made **rice** a hugely profitable export; **indigo** followed in the 1740s. By the early 1700s enslaved Africans made up a majority of South Carolina's population. **Georgia** (1732) began differently: James Oglethorpe and the trustees intended a buffer against Spanish Florida and a fresh start for poor English families, and banned slavery in 1735. Settlers pressed for slavery and it was legalised in 1751, bringing Georgia into the Lower South's pattern.

### The British West Indies

**Sugar** made Barbados and Jamaica the most valuable British colonies, worked by enslaved majorities under some of the cruellest conditions in the Americas. The islands depended on the mainland for food, timber and horses, which tied New England and the Middle Colonies to plantation slavery through trade.

## Self-government

Distance from London and English habits produced strong local government: the House of Burgesses (1619), the Mayflower Compact (1620), the Fundamental Orders of Connecticut (1639), New England town meetings and elected assemblies in nearly every colony. Voting was limited to property-holding white men, but land was easier to own than in England, so more men could vote. Assemblies copied the House of Commons and used control of taxes and governors' salaries as leverage.

## The Atlantic economy (2.4)

**Mercantilism** held that a nation grew powerful by exporting more than it imported and accumulating wealth. Colonies existed to supply raw materials and to buy the mother country's manufactures. Parliament put the theory into law with the **Navigation Acts** (1651, 1660, 1663, 1696):

1. Colonial trade had to be carried in English or colonial ships with mostly English crews (aimed at the Dutch).
2. **Enumerated goods** (sugar, tobacco, cotton, indigo and others) could be shipped only to England or other English colonies.
3. Most European goods bound for the colonies had to pass through England first (1663).

The acts burdened tobacco planters, who lost Dutch buyers, but colonial-built ships counted as English, so New England shipbuilding thrived.

When James II tried to tighten control, colonists resisted. The **Dominion of New England** (1686–1689) abolished assemblies across New England, later adding New York and New Jersey, and Governor Sir Edmund Andros enforced the trade laws. When news arrived that the **Glorious Revolution** had removed James, Bostonians overthrew Andros in April 1689, and the colonies restored their assemblies. Colonists drew a lesson: Parliament's victory over a king was a victory for English liberties they shared.

In the decades that followed, especially under Robert Walpole (1720s to early 1740s), Britain practised **salutary neglect**: enforcement was lax so long as the colonies stayed loyal and profitable. The **Molasses Act** (1733), which taxed molasses from French islands, was widely evaded by smugglers. Meanwhile a **consumer revolution** filled colonial homes with British cloth, ceramics and tea, tying colonists to Britain by taste and by credit.

The so-called **triangular trade** (rum to Africa, enslaved people to the West Indies, molasses to New England) is a useful simplification of a web of routes. Every region was linked to the slave trade, directly or through the provisions and shipping that kept plantations running.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Maryland Toleration Act (1649)**

> "And whereas the inforceing of the conscience in matters of Religion hath frequently fallen out to be of dangerous Consequence in those commonwealthes where it hath been practised ... noe person or persons whatsoever within this Province ... professing to beleive in Jesus Christ, shall from henceforth bee any waies troubled, Molested or discountenanced for or in respect of his or her religion nor in the free exercise thereof ..."

*HIPP.* **Historical situation:** a Catholic proprietor governing a Protestant majority while England fought a civil war over religion and power. **Audience:** Maryland's colonists. **Purpose:** to keep peace and protect the Catholic minority by protecting all Christians. **Point of view:** a practical, not a philosophical, case for tolerance. *Why it matters:* an early step toward religious liberty, with a clear limit: non-Christians were not protected.

**2. Navigation Act (1660)**

> "... noe Goods or Commodities whatsoever shall be Imported into or Exported out of any Lands Islelands Plantations or Territories to his Majesty belonging ... in Asia Africa or America in any other Ship or Ships ... but in such Ships or Vessells as doe truely and without fraude belong onely to the people of England or Ireland ... or are of the built of, and belonging to any of the said Lands Islands Plantations or Territories ..."

*HIPP.* **Historical situation:** commercial rivalry with the Dutch. **Audience:** merchants, customs officers, colonial governors. **Purpose:** to keep the profits of colonial trade inside the empire. *Why it matters:* the legal backbone of mercantilism; note the clause that let colonial ships count as English.

**3. Fundamental Orders of Connecticut (1639)** (modernised spelling, as printed by the Avalon Project)

> "... we the Inhabitants and Residents of Windsor, Hartford and Wethersfield ... well knowing where a people are gathered together the word of God requires that to maintain the peace and union of such a people there should be an orderly and decent Government established according to God ... do therefore associate and conjoin ourselves to be as one Public State or Commonwealth ..."

*Why it matters:* Puritan covenant thinking applied to civil government; an example of self-government by agreement.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: from comparison table to thesis.** Prompt: *Evaluate the extent of difference between New England and the Chesapeake, 1607–1754.* Weak thesis: "New England and the Chesapeake were different in many ways." Strong thesis: "New England and the Chesapeake differed sharply in economy and society because Puritan families settled a cold, rocky region while young servants settled a warm tobacco country; yet both developed strong representative assemblies, so the difference was great but not total." The strong version gives a reason (*because*) and a qualification (*yet*).

**Worked example 2: an SAQ answered part by part** (on the Fundamental Orders excerpt).

- (a) *Describe one purpose.* "To create an orderly government for the three river towns by their own agreement, to keep peace and union."
- (b) *Explain how religion shaped it.* "The Puritan authors believed God's word required ordered government; the document calls the settlement providential and promises government 'according to God', a covenant idea."
- (c) *Explain a broader pattern.* "Like the House of Burgesses and the Mayflower Compact, it shows colonists building representative institutions, which grew stronger under salutary neglect."

Each part answers its verb in two or three sentences with a specific example.

**Worked example 3: a causation chain.** Navigation Acts (1660) → Dutch excluded, enumerated goods → colonists evade and resent enforcement → James II creates the Dominion of New England (1686) → Glorious Revolution (1688) → Bostonians overthrow Andros (1689) → assemblies restored; Britain wary of strict enforcement → salutary neglect → smuggling and strong assemblies → the colonists' shock when Britain enforces the rules after 1763 (Unit 3).

## Common misconceptions

- **"All southern colonies were the same."** The Chesapeake used servants for decades before slavery dominated; the Lower South relied on enslaved Africans from the start and grew rice, not tobacco.
- **"Slavery was only southern."** It was legal in every colony, and northern merchants profited from the slave trade and West Indian trade.
- **"Puritans came for religious freedom for everyone."** They came for the freedom to practise *their* faith and banished dissenters.
- **"The Navigation Acts only hurt colonists."** New England shipbuilders and merchants benefited.
