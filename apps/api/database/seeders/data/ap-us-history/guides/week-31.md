## The 1970s: the context for Period 9

Period 9 begins with a sense of national decline. Between 1973 and 1980 Americans lived through two oil shocks, inflation that reached double digits, unemployment that did not fall as the textbooks said it should, and the first sustained decline of manufacturing jobs in the industrial North. Faith in Washington had already been damaged by the credibility gap over Vietnam and by Watergate and Nixon's resignation in 1974. In 1979 Islamic revolutionaries in Iran took 52 Americans hostage, and the Soviet Union invaded Afghanistan.

Jimmy Carter tried to name the problem directly. In a televised address in July 1979 he described "a crisis of confidence" and asked Americans to accept limits, especially on energy. Many heard pessimism. Ronald Reagan's 1980 campaign answered with optimism and a different diagnosis: the trouble lay not in the American people but in a government that taxed, spent and regulated too much.

A conservative movement built since the 1950s (free-market intellectuals, anticommunists, the activists who nominated Barry Goldwater in 1964) gained a mass base in the 1970s. White Southern voters had been leaving the Democratic Party since the civil rights laws of the mid-1960s. Suburban homeowners joined tax revolts such as California's Proposition 13 (1978). Evangelical Christians, many of whom had stayed out of politics, organized against the legalization of abortion in Roe v. Wade (1973), against the Equal Rights Amendment, and for school prayer.

## Reagan and the conservative coalition

Reagan won in 1980 with 489 electoral votes to Carter's 49; in 1984 he carried 49 states. His coalition joined four groups that did not always agree: economic conservatives who wanted lower taxes and less regulation; religious conservatives who wanted government to defend traditional morality; anticommunists who wanted a stronger military; and Reagan Democrats, white working-class voters who had voted for New Deal Democrats but were drawn by Reagan's patriotism, social conservatism and economic message.

**What changed.** The Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 cut income tax rates by about a quarter and lowered the top rate from 70 to 50 percent; the bipartisan Tax Reform Act of 1986 lowered it to 28 percent while closing loopholes. Deregulation, which had begun under Carter with airlines and trucking, widened to banking, broadcasting and some environmental enforcement. Reagan's firing of striking air traffic controllers in 1981 signaled a harder line toward unions. His appointments of Sandra Day O'Connor, Antonin Scalia and Anthony Kennedy, and the elevation of William Rehnquist to Chief Justice, moved the Supreme Court rightward; the Senate's rejection of Robert Bork in 1987 began the era of fiercely contested confirmations.

**What did not.** The federal government did not shrink. Defense spending rose sharply, and Social Security and Medicare, popular with the middle class, survived. The Social Security Amendments of 1983, worked out by a bipartisan commission, preserved the system by raising payroll taxes and gradually raising the retirement age. Democrats controlled the House throughout the 1980s and bargained over every budget. With tax cuts and higher defense spending, deficits reached the largest peacetime levels to that point and the gross federal debt nearly tripled.

**The arguments, in each side's own terms.** Supporters argued that lower taxes and lighter regulation unleashed the long expansion that followed the 1981–1982 recession, that inflation fell from over 13 percent in 1980 to about 4 percent by 1984, and that national pride and military strength were restored. Critics argued that the benefits went disproportionately to the wealthy, that cuts to programs for the poor and to housing hurt vulnerable Americans, that deregulation helped cause the savings and loan crisis, and that deficits burdened future generations. An AP essay should weigh both rather than choose a side by assertion.

## The end of the Cold War

Reagan's first term was confrontational. He called the Soviet Union "an evil empire," increased defense spending, deployed new missiles in Western Europe, proposed the Strategic Defense Initiative, and, under the Reagan Doctrine, aided anticommunist fighters in Afghanistan, Nicaragua and Angola. Congress, wary after Vietnam, barred aid to Nicaragua's Contras through the Boland Amendment; the Iran-Contra affair (revealed 1986) showed National Security Council staff circumventing it, raising questions about executive power.

Then the Soviet side changed. Mikhail Gorbachev, who took power in 1985, faced a stagnant economy, a costly war in Afghanistan and a population that knew it was falling behind the West. He introduced glasnost and perestroika, cut military spending and sought agreements with the West. Reagan negotiated. After the near-agreement at Reykjavik (1986), the two leaders signed the INF Treaty in 1987, eliminating a whole class of nuclear missiles. In Berlin that year Reagan challenged Gorbachev to open the wall as proof that reform was real.

In 1989 the Soviet bloc in Eastern Europe came apart. Poland's Solidarity movement won partly free elections; Hungary opened its border with Austria; crowds in East Germany forced the opening of the Berlin Wall on November 9. Gorbachev did not send tanks, as Soviet leaders had in 1956 and 1968. Germany reunified in 1990. After a failed coup by hard-liners in August 1991, the Soviet republics broke away, and on December 25 Gorbachev resigned. The Soviet Union was gone.

**Why did it end?** Historians give weight to different causes. Some stress American pressure: the buildup, SDI and the Reagan Doctrine raised costs for an economy that could not compete. Others stress Soviet internal weakness and Gorbachev's choices, especially his refusal to use force in 1989, and the courage of Eastern Europeans themselves. Many combine them, noting that Reagan's willingness to negotiate in his second term mattered as much as the pressure of his first.

## Primary sources to read closely

**1. Reagan, Inaugural Address, January 20, 1981.**

> "In this present crisis, government is not the solution to our problem; government is the problem. ... It is my intention to curb the size and influence of the Federal establishment and to demand recognition of the distinction between the powers granted to the Federal Government and those reserved to the States or to the people."

*Historical situation:* stagflation and the hostage crisis, which ended the day of the speech. *Audience:* the nation, and a Congress with a Democratic House. *Purpose:* to define a mandate for tax cuts and a smaller federal role. *Point of view:* a movement conservative who had spent twenty years arguing against the New Deal order. Why it matters: it is the clearest statement of the period's political turn, and the qualifier "in this present crisis" is worth noticing.

**2. Reagan, remarks to the National Association of Evangelicals, March 8, 1983.**

> "I believe that communism is another sad, bizarre chapter in human history whose last pages even now are being written."

*Audience and purpose:* evangelical leaders, many of whose churches were debating a nuclear freeze; Reagan wanted them to see the Cold War as a moral contest. Why it matters: it shows the religious right and anticommunism reinforcing each other.

**3. Reagan, address at the Brandenburg Gate, West Berlin, June 12, 1987.**

> "General Secretary Gorbachev, if you seek peace, if you seek prosperity for the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe, if you seek liberalization: Come here to this gate! Mr. Gorbachev, open this gate! Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall!"

*Audience:* Berliners, West European allies worried about the arms race, and Moscow. *Purpose:* to test whether glasnost was real. Use with care: the wall fell in 1989 because of events in the East, so the speech shows U.S. rhetoric, not what caused the collapse.

## Historical thinking: worked examples

**Worked example 1: a contextualization paragraph for 1980.** Prompt: explain the context for Reagan's election.

> By 1980 the postwar liberal order was under strain. Two oil shocks and stagflation had undermined the Keynesian economics that had guided policy since the New Deal; Vietnam and Watergate had eroded trust in Washington; and the Iran hostage crisis made the nation look weak abroad. At the same time a conservative movement that had lost badly with Goldwater in 1964 had gained a mass base: white Southerners leaving the Democratic Party after the civil rights laws, suburban tax rebels, and evangelical Christians mobilized against Roe v. Wade and the ERA.

Why it works: it moves from broad conditions to the specific coalition and sets up an argument.

**Worked example 2: an SAQ on the end of the Cold War, part by part.** Given two interpretations, one crediting U.S. pressure and one crediting Soviet and Eastern European choices:

- (a) *Describe a difference.* "A treats the end of the Cold War as caused from outside, by American pressure; B treats it as caused from inside the Soviet bloc."
- (b) *Evidence for A not in the excerpt.* "Pershing II missiles deployed in West Germany in 1983 showed NATO would match Soviet missiles, raising the costs of the arms race for Moscow."
- (c) *Evidence for B.* "In 1989 Gorbachev let Hungary open its border and did not intervene when East Germany collapsed, unlike in 1956 and 1968."

**Worked example 3: from prompt to thesis.** Prompt: evaluate the extent to which the conservative movement changed the role of the federal government from 1980 to 2000.

- Weak: "Conservatism changed the government a lot." (No line of reasoning.)
- Defensible: "Conservatives cut tax rates, loosened regulation and reshaped the courts, but they did not shrink the federal government, because Social Security and Medicare were too popular to dismantle and defense spending rose; their deepest change was in the terms of debate, which Democrats such as Clinton came to accept."

The second answers "to what extent" and sets up continuity and change.

## Common misconceptions

- *"Reagan shrank the federal government."* Federal spending and debt grew; some domestic programs were cut, but defense and entitlements expanded.
- *"Reagan alone ended the Cold War."* Most historians assign major roles to Gorbachev, Soviet economic decline and Eastern European movements, as well as to U.S. policy.
- *"The religious right was always in politics."* Large-scale evangelical political organizing was new in the late 1970s.
