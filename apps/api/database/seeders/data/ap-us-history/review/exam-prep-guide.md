# AP U.S. History · Exam Prep Guide

**Exam:** Friday 7 May 2027, morning session · **Format:** fully digital in the Bluebook app, two sections, 3 hours 15 minutes · **Built from:** the College Board AP U.S. History Course and Exam Description and the AP Central pages on the exam and on the 2027 history exam updates (accessed 2026-10-13).

## The format

| Section | Items | Time | Weight | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I, Part A · Multiple choice | 55 questions, four options each | 55 minutes | 40% | Sets of 3 or 4 questions on one source: a primary text, a historian's interpretation, an image, a map, a chart or a table. No penalty for guessing. |
| I, Part B · Short answer (SAQ) | 3 questions, all required | 40 minutes | 20% | Each has three parts, (a), (b) and (c), worth 1 point each. SAQ 1 uses a secondary text, SAQ 2 a primary text, SAQ 3 a non-text source (map, image, chart). No two from the same period; together they span 1491 to 2001. |
| II · Document-based question (DBQ) | 1 question, 7 documents | 60 minutes, including a 15-minute reading period | 25% | Scored out of 7. |
| II · Long essay question (LEQ) | 1 question, required | 40 minutes | 15% | Scored out of 6. |

Because the exam is digital, you type every essay. You can highlight and annotate sources in Bluebook and move between questions within a section, and your answers are submitted automatically when time runs out. Practise typing timed essays this year, not handwriting them.

### What changed for 2027

According to AP Central (the AP U.S. History exam page and the AP History Exam Updates page, accessed 2026-10-13), the May 2027 exam changes the free-response questions but not the course:

- **Every SAQ now has a source.** Earlier exams required two SAQs with sources and let you choose between two more that had none. From 2027 you answer three required questions, each built on a source, in a fixed pattern: secondary text, primary text, non-text.
- **One LEQ prompt, no choice.** You used to choose one of three prompts from different eras. Now there is a single broad prompt with a short introductory statement that suggests lines of analysis; the choice now lies inside the question, in which developments you write about.
- **The DBQ can range more widely.** The DBQ covers a wider chronological range, so evidence (in the documents and your own) may come from across the course rather than only 1754 to 1980.
- **Unchanged:** the course content, the historical thinking skills, the multiple-choice section and the scoring rubrics. The LEQ still needs at least two pieces of specific evidence, and the DBQ still asks for the same kinds of evidence.

## The skills, the reasoning processes and the themes

The College Board writes every question to test one of six **historical thinking skills**:

| Skill | What it asks you to do |
|---|---|
| 1 · Developments and processes | Identify and explain historical concepts, developments and processes. |
| 2 · Sourcing and situation | Identify and explain a source's point of view, purpose, historical situation and audience (HIPP), and why they matter. |
| 3 · Claims and evidence in sources | Identify a source's argument and the evidence for it; decide what supports or challenges a claim. |
| 4 · Contextualization | Place a development in the broader historical setting that explains it. |
| 5 · Making connections | Link developments across time, place and theme using a reasoning process. |
| 6 · Argumentation | Build a defensible claim, support it with evidence, and qualify or modify it. |

and three **reasoning processes**, which you will use to organise essays:

- **Comparison:** describe and explain similarities and differences, and why they existed.
- **Causation:** explain causes and effects, distinguish primary from secondary causes and short-term from long-term effects.
- **Continuity and change over time:** describe patterns of continuity and change and explain why they happened.

The course is organised around eight **themes**. A good habit when you review a period is to write one sentence per theme.

| Code | Theme | Ask yourself |
|---|---|---|
| NAT | American and National Identity | Who counted as American, and how did ideas of citizenship and national purpose change? |
| WXT | Work, Exchange, and Technology | How did people make a living, and how did technology and markets change work? |
| GEO | Geography and the Environment | How did the land shape settlement and the economy, and how did people reshape the land? |
| MIG | Migration and Settlement | Who moved, why, and how were they received? |
| PCE | Politics and Power | Who held power, how was it contested, and how did the role of government change? |
| WOR | America in the World | How did the United States interact with other nations and peoples? |
| ARC | American and Regional Culture | How did religion, art, ideas and regional cultures develop? |
| SOC | Social Structures | How did class, race, gender and family structure society? |

## The task verbs

| Verb | What a scoring answer contains |
|---|---|
| **Identify** | Name it: a person, law, event or development. One phrase, but specific. |
| **Describe** | Give the relevant characteristics, in more than a phrase: what it was, when, who, and what it did. |
| **Explain** | Give the how or why: connect a cause to an effect, or evidence to a claim, with a because. |
| **Compare** | Give a similarity or difference and explain the reason for it. |
| **Support, modify or refute** | Agree, adjust or disagree with an interpretation, using specific evidence. |
| **Evaluate the extent** | Make a judgment about how much (a great deal, somewhat, very little), then defend it with evidence on both sides. |

The three most common ways to lose points: answering describe with a single word, answering explain with a description and no because, and writing about the wrong period.

## Short-answer technique: ACE

Each SAQ part is worth one point, and graders look for one clear, accurate, specific answer per part. Use **ACE**:

- **A, Answer:** answer the question in your first sentence, using the verb's language.
- **C, Cite:** give one specific piece of evidence: a named law, person, event, court case or number, with a date.
- **E, Explain:** connect the evidence to the question in one or two sentences with because or which shows that.

Label every part (a), (b), (c). Do not hedge with two answers in one part; if one is wrong, the grader may not award the point. Watch the date range in each part: an answer from the wrong decade earns zero even if it is true. In SAQ 1 (a secondary source), quote or paraphrase the author's claim before you agree or disagree; in SAQ 3 (a non-text source), describe what the map, image or chart actually shows before you explain it.

**Worked example (SAQ 2 on a primary text).** Source: Abigail Adams's 1776 letter asking her husband to remember the ladies in the new code of laws. Part (b): Briefly explain one way the letter reflects the ideas of the American Revolution.

> **Answer:** Adams applies the patriots' own argument about representation to women. **Cite:** She warns that women will not be bound by laws in which they have no voice or representation, echoing the colonial slogan of no taxation without representation used against the Stamp Act. **Explain:** By turning the principle of consent against men, she shows that the Revolution's ideals could be extended to groups the revolutionaries did not have in mind.

## Multiple-choice technique for source sets

- **Read the source line first.** The author, date, audience and type (speech, court opinion, cartoon, interpretation) are often the key to the first question in the set.
- **Ask what the source is responding to.** Most sets include a question on historical situation or cause: the stem most directly a response to points to something just before the source's date.
- **For a historian's interpretation, find the claim in one sentence** before you look at the options. Then ask which option supports, challenges or extends that exact claim.
- **For a map, chart or table, read titles, labels, units and dates** and describe the pattern to yourself before reading options.
- **Eliminate on chronology.** Many wrong options are true statements from the wrong period.
- **Prefer the option that explains, over one that is merely true.** Absolutes (always, never, ended, completely) are usually wrong.
- **Pace:** one minute per question. Mark hard items and return; never leave one blank.

## The DBQ rubric, point by point (7 points)

| Point | How to earn it |
|---|---|
| **Thesis** (1) | A historically defensible claim that answers the prompt and sets out a line of reasoning, in the introduction or conclusion. |
| **Contextualization** (1) | Describe a broader development before, during or after the period that bears on the prompt, in several sentences, not a phrase. |
| **Evidence from the documents** (up to 2) | 1 for accurately describing the content of at least three documents; 2 for using at least four documents to support your argument. |
| **Evidence beyond the documents** (1) | One specific, relevant piece of evidence not in the documents, used in your argument (and different from your contextualization). |
| **Sourcing** (1) | For at least two documents, explain how or why the point of view, purpose, historical situation or audience is relevant to your argument. |
| **Complex understanding** (1) | Show sophistication across the essay: explain both sides, multiple causes, both change and continuity, or connections to another period; or use all seven documents with corroboration and qualification. |

**Plan the reading period.** Spend the 15 minutes reading, grouping documents into two or three categories that answer the prompt, marking HIPP notes beside at least three, and listing two pieces of outside evidence. Then write for 45 minutes.

## The LEQ rubric, point by point (6 points)

| Point | How to earn it |
|---|---|
| **Thesis** (1) | A defensible claim with a line of reasoning that responds to the prompt. |
| **Contextualization** (1) | Describe a broader historical context relevant to the prompt. |
| **Evidence** (up to 2) | 1 for identifying at least two specific, relevant examples; 2 for using specific evidence to support your argument. |
| **Analysis and reasoning** (up to 2) | 1 for using comparison, causation or continuity and change to frame or structure the argument; 1 for demonstrating a complex understanding. |

From 2027 the single LEQ prompt is broad. Read the introductory statement carefully: it suggests lines of analysis. You are expected to choose developments within the range, not to cover every decade.

## Worked example: thesis and contextualization

Prompt: *Evaluate the extent to which the Progressive movement changed the role of the federal government in the American economy, 1890 to 1920.*

**Weak thesis (0):** The Progressive movement changed the role of the federal government in many ways. It restates the prompt and gives no line of reasoning.

**Defensible thesis (1):** Between 1890 and 1920 Progressives turned the federal government into an active regulator of big business and finance, through antitrust suits, food and drug laws, the income tax and the Federal Reserve, but federal protection for workers remained limited because the courts defended liberty of contract and left labor laws mainly to the states. It takes a position on extent (great change in one area, limited in another) and previews the categories the body will follow.

**Contextualization (1):** After the Civil War, industrialization created national corporations such as Standard Oil and Carnegie Steel, along with dangerous workplaces and child labor. Gilded Age governments largely followed laissez-faire, and the Supreme Court used the Fourteenth Amendment to protect corporations from regulation. In the 1890s the Populists demanded railroad regulation, an income tax and direct election of senators, and their defeat in 1896 left those demands to a new generation of middle-class reformers. That is a full paragraph about the broader setting that leads into the argument, not a single sentence.

## Two-week review plan (weeks 33 and 34)

About 75 minutes a day. Each day pairs one or two unit summaries (the night-before sheets at the end of each unit) with practice, and ends with writing, because the free-response section is 60% of the score.

| Day | Content | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Unit 1 and Unit 2 summaries (Periods 1–2) | Re-take the Unit 2 exam's multiple-choice items; write one ACE SAQ on colonial regions |
| 2 | Unit 3 summary (Period 3) | Draw a causation chain from 1754 to 1776; write a contextualization paragraph for the Constitution |
| 3 | Unit 4 summary (Period 4) | One timed SAQ on the market revolution; twenty flashcards from weeks 10–13 |
| 4 | Unit 5 summary (Period 5) | Comparison chart: Presidential versus Congressional Reconstruction; write a thesis on the causes of the Civil War |
| 5 | Unit 6 summary (Period 6) | Sourcing drill: HIPP notes for three Gilded Age documents from the Unit 6 exam |
| 6 | Unit 7 summary (Period 7), part 1: empire, Progressives, WWI, 1920s | One timed LEQ (40 minutes) using the eight-theme check |
| 7 | Rest, or re-take any week quiz you scored under 70% on | — |
| 8 | Unit 7 summary, part 2: Depression, New Deal, WWII | One timed DBQ from the Unit 7 exam (60 minutes) |
| 9 | Unit 8 summary (Period 8) | Continuity and change paragraph on civil rights, 1945–1980; Unit 8 exam stimulus sets |
| 10 | Unit 9 summary (Period 9) | One SAQ on a non-text source; review the period-weight table and target your weakest period |
| 11 | **Practice exam, Section I** (items 1–58: 55 minutes, then 40) | Mark multiple choice; list every miss by topic code and period |
| 12 | **Practice exam, Section II** (items 59–60: 100 minutes) | Score the DBQ and LEQ against the scoring notes, point by point |
| 13 | Re-study the five topic codes you missed most | Re-take those weeks' quizzes; rewrite the thesis you scored lowest |
| 14 | This guide's rubric tables and task verbs; each unit summary's thesis-ready arguments | Stop early. Charge your device, check Bluebook is updated, sleep. |

## The night before and on the day

Re-read the rubric tables and the period-weight table, not new content. On the day: pace Part A at one question a minute; in Part B, write ACE answers and label every part; in Section II, use the full reading period, write the DBQ first and leave a full 40 minutes for the LEQ. Every point on the DBQ and LEQ rubrics is independent, so a thesis and contextualization written under time pressure still count.
