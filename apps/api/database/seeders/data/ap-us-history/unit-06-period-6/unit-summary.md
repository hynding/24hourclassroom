# Unit 6 · Period 6: 1865–1898 · The night-before sheet

**Exam weight:** 10–17% of the AP exam · **CED topics:** 6.1 to 6.14 · **Themes:** WXT, MIG, PCE, SOC, GEO, NAT
**Built from:** weeks 18–21: the Treaty of Fort Laramie, the Dawes Act, Turner's frontier thesis; Plessy v. Ferguson, the Sherman Act and E. C. Knight; the Chinese Exclusion Act, Lazarus and Riis; Munn, the Interstate Commerce Act, Cleveland's 1887 veto, the Omaha Platform and Bryan's 1896 address

## The ideas that generate every question

1. **Industrial capitalism remade the country.** Railroads, steel, oil and electricity, organized by corporations, trusts and investment banks, created a national market and the world's largest industrial economy. Its defenders (Social Darwinism, the Gospel of Wealth) and critics (George, Bellamy, the Populists) frame every argument in the unit.
2. **The West was conquered, not settled empty.** Federal land grants, homesteads and the army opened the West to railroads, miners, ranchers and farmers; Native nations lost the bison, their land and much of their autonomy, moving from war to reservations to allotment and boarding schools.
3. **The New South changed less than it claimed.** Some industry arrived, but cotton, sharecropping and the crop-lien persisted, and a new racial order (disfranchisement, Jim Crow, lynching) replaced slavery, approved by the Supreme Court.
4. **Workers and immigrants built the economy and fought over its terms.** Wage labor, mass immigration from southern and eastern Europe and Asia, and explosive urban growth produced unions, great strikes, machines, settlement houses and nativism.
5. **What does government owe its citizens?** Government subsidized business but rarely regulated it or relieved hardship. Farmers, workers and reformers challenged that, producing the first federal regulation (ICA 1887, Sherman 1890) and the Populist revolt, while courts and presidents held the line.
6. **Politics was close, loud and finally realigned.** Balanced parties, high turnout, patronage, tariffs and the money question ended in 1896 with McKinley's victory over Bryan and the Populists.

## Chains to know

- **Causation (West):** Pacific Railway Act → transcontinental (1869) → hide hunters and settlers → bison destroyed → reservations → Dawes Act and boarding schools → Ghost Dance → Wounded Knee.
- **Causation (farm protest):** commercial farming → debt, falling prices, railroad monopoly → Grange (Munn) → Wabash → ICA → Alliances → Populists (1892) → Bryan and fusion (1896) → Progressive adoption of the income tax and direct election of senators.
- **Causation (Jim Crow):** Compromise of 1877 → Redeemers → Civil Rights Cases (1883) → Mississippi Plan (1890) → Plessy (1896) → Williams (1898).
- **Comparison:** Knights vs AFL; Carnegie vs Rockefeller; Washington vs Wells; Sumner vs George; Indian removal (1830s) vs allotment (1887); old vs new immigration.
- **Continuity and change:** racial hierarchy from slavery to Jim Crow; federal promotion of business from 1862 to 1898; nativism from the Know-Nothings to the Immigration Restriction League; anti-monopoly politics from Jackson's Bank War to Bryan.

## Thesis-ready arguments

- "Although the federal government claimed to follow laissez-faire, it actively promoted business through land grants, tariffs and the use of troops and injunctions against strikers, while refusing relief to farmers and workers until protest forced the first, limited regulation."
- "The settlement of the trans-Mississippi West was driven more by federal policy than by individual pioneers, since land grants, homestead law and military conquest made private enterprise possible there."
- "The New South changed far less than its boosters claimed, because cotton tenancy, outside ownership and a racial order enforced by disfranchisement and segregation preserved the hierarchy of the old South."
- "The Populists failed as a party but succeeded as a movement, since much of the Omaha Platform became law within two decades."
- "Immigration transformed American cities and labor between 1865 and 1898, but the nativist response, from Chinese exclusion to the literacy-test campaign, shows the limits of the welcome."

## The unit exam

Forty-five questions in about 75 minutes: five stimulus sets (Fort Laramie and the Dawes Act; an urban-population table; the Fourteenth Amendment; Bryan's 'Cross of Gold'; Lazarus and the Chinese Exclusion Act) and stand-alone items across all fourteen topics, with about a fifth reviewing earlier periods (colonial land conflict, the 1785 land survey, the commerce clause, the market revolution, Jackson, Manifest Destiny, the Civil War and Reconstruction). Two SAQs (robber barons versus industrial statesmen; Nast's Tammany tiger) and a seven-document DBQ on how ideas about government's economic role changed. For the DBQ, sort the documents into 'expanding government' and 'limiting government', source at least two (Cleveland and the Omaha Platform are the easiest), and bring one piece of outside evidence such as the Sherman Act or *In re Debs*.
