# Unit 3 · Period 3: 1754–1800 · The night-before sheet

**Exam weight:** 10–17% of the AP exam · **CED topics:** 3.1 to 3.13 · **Themes:** PCE, WOR, NAT, MIG, SOC, ARC
**Built from:** weeks 6–9 of this course; the Proclamation of 1763, the Stamp Act Congress resolutions, the Declaratory Act, the Declaration of Independence, Common Sense, the Articles of Confederation, the Northwest Ordinance, the Constitution, The Federalist, Washington's Farewell Address and the Sedition Act

## The ideas that generate every question

1. **Victory created the crisis.** The Seven Years' War removed France, doubled Britain's debt and left an army in America. Britain's decision to make the colonists pay and obey ended salutary neglect, and the collision with colonial habits of self-government drove everything from 1763 to 1776.
2. **The argument was about consent before it was about independence.** Colonists claimed the rights of Englishmen (no taxation without representation) until 1774–1775; only war, the king's rejection of compromise and Paine's Common Sense turned the claim into natural rights and independence.
3. **The Americans won with help.** Washington's survival strategy, militia control of the countryside and British overstretch mattered, but French money, troops and sea power after Saratoga were decisive.
4. **Revolutionary ideals cut further than their authors meant.** "All men are created equal" was turned against slavery, coverture and established churches. Results were real (northern emancipation, disestablishment) but limited (southern slavery, women's legal status, Native dispossession).
5. **Americans swung from too little central power to a fight over how much.** The Articles were weak by design; Shays' Rebellion and fiscal paralysis produced the Constitution; the 1790s were spent arguing over what the Constitution allowed (bank, Sedition Act, Kentucky Resolutions).
6. **Land and Native sovereignty run through the whole period.** From the Ohio Valley in 1754 to the Treaty of Greenville in 1795, settler demand for land and Native resistance shaped war and policy; what changed was who claimed sovereignty over the West.

## Chains to know

- **Causation:** war debt → new taxes → resistance (boycotts, congresses, crowds) → repeal plus Declaratory Act → Townshend Acts → Tea Act → Coercive Acts → First Continental Congress → Lexington → independence.
- **Causation:** Saratoga → French alliance → global war → Yorktown → Treaty of Paris.
- **Causation:** no taxing power → unpaid debts → heavy state taxes → Shays' Rebellion → Philadelphia Convention.
- **Comparison:** Articles vs Constitution (taxes, commerce, executive, courts, representation, amendment). Federalists vs Anti-Federalists. Hamilton vs Jefferson. Shays' vs Whiskey Rebellion.
- **Continuity and change:** subjects → citizens; colonial assemblies → written constitutions; factions → parties. Continuities: slavery in the South, coverture, settler pressure on Native land, debate over central vs local power.

## Thesis-ready arguments

- *The Seven Years' War was the turning point in Anglo-American relations*, because its debt and territory led Britain to abandon salutary neglect; but colonial resistance drew on older traditions of self-government.
- *French intervention was the most important cause of American victory*, but it depended on American survival and Saratoga.
- *The Revolution was more radical in principle than in practice*: it created a republic, disestablished churches and began northern emancipation, but left southern slavery, women's legal status and Native dispossession largely intact.
- *The Constitution resolved the Articles' weakness of power but created a lasting conflict over the limits of power*, from the bank to the Kentucky Resolutions.
- *Native relations changed in framework but not in pattern*: the end of the French–British rivalry and the arrival of an expansionist republic transformed Native diplomacy, while war, boundary lines and cessions continued.

## The unit exam

Forty-five questions: five source-based multiple-choice sets (the Declaratory Act, Common Sense, the Treaty of Paris, Jefferson on rebellion, Federalist No. 51), single items across all thirteen topics, about nine review items from Periods 1 and 2, two short-answer questions (a historian's economic interpretation of the Constitution and Revere's engraving of the Boston Massacre), and a document-based question: *evaluate the extent to which the American Revolution changed American society, 1775–1800.* For the DBQ, plan a thesis that weighs change against continuity, group the seven documents by category (religion and identity; slavery; women; American Indians), source at least two of them, and bring in at least one piece of outside evidence such as Pennsylvania's Gradual Abolition Act or the cotton gin.
