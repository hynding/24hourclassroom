# Unit 5 · Period 5: 1844–1877 · The night-before sheet

**Exam weight:** 10–17% of the AP exam · **CED topics:** 5.1 to 5.12 · **Themes:** PCE, NAT, MIG, GEO, WXT, SOC, WOR
**Built from:** the week 14–17 guides; Polk's war message (1846); the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848); the Fugitive Slave Act (1850); the Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854); Dred Scott (1857); Lincoln's speeches (1858–1865); the Emancipation Proclamation (1863); the Reconstruction amendments; Johnson's 1866 veto; Grant's 1871 message; Cruikshank (1876)

## The six ideas that generate every question

1. **Land reopened the slavery question.** Texas, Oregon and the Mexican Cession (1845–1848) added land outside the Missouri Compromise. Every acre forced Congress to decide whether slavery could expand, and every answer (Wilmot Proviso, popular sovereignty, Calhoun's doctrine, Dred Scott) split the sections further.
2. **Section replaced party.** The second party system survived by keeping slavery off the agenda. After 1846 votes split North against South; the Whigs died in 1854, the Democrats split in 1860, and a purely Northern Republican Party won the presidency.
3. **Two societies, two ideologies.** Free labor (mobility through work, a West of small farms) against the positive good (slavery as a benevolent order, cotton as king). Each saw the other as a threat to its future.
4. **The war made emancipation a war aim.** Enslaved people's flight, congressional confiscation, Lincoln's proclamation and 180,000 Black soldiers turned a war for Union into a war against slavery, finished by the Thirteenth Amendment.
5. **War built a stronger national government.** Drafts, income taxes, greenbacks, national banks, land grants and a railroad charter in the Union; drafts, impressment and centralisation even in the Confederacy.
6. **Reconstruction changed the law more than the South.** The amendments redefined citizenship and voting, and Black Southerners built churches, schools and a decade of political power, but violence, the courts, depression and Northern retreat ended federal protection in 1877.

## Chains to know

- **Causation (the road to war):** Mexican Cession → Wilmot Proviso → Gold Rush and California → Compromise of 1850 → Fugitive Slave Act resistance → Kansas-Nebraska → party realignment → Dred Scott → Democratic split → Lincoln's sectional victory → secession → Fort Sumter.
- **Causation (emancipation):** flight to Union lines → Confiscation Acts → Antietam → Emancipation Proclamation → Black enlistment → Thirteenth Amendment.
- **Causation (end of Reconstruction):** violence + Supreme Court narrowing + Panic of 1873 + Northern weariness and scandal → Redemption → Compromise of 1877.
- **Continuity and change (citizenship):** Dred Scott (1857) denies it → Fourteenth Amendment (1868) defines it → Cruikshank (1876) narrows its protection.
- **Comparison:** Compromises of 1850 and 1877 (sectional peace at Black Americans' expense); Union and Confederate centralisation; Johnson's and Congress's plans; the South before and after (freedom and citizenship changed; land, cotton and white political control persisted).

## Thesis-ready arguments

- Territorial expansion, not abolitionism, put slavery at the centre of national politics, because new land forced Congress to choose whether slavery would spread.
- The Kansas-Nebraska Act was the decisive failure of compromise because it destroyed the last cross-sectional party structure.
- Secession was a defence of slavery: the seceding states said so, and the order of secession followed slavery's concentration.
- The Union won because it turned material advantages into a total war and an antislavery war that added soldiers and kept Europe out.
- Reconstruction was a revolution in law and a failure in enforcement: its amendments outlasted its collapse and became the foundation of twentieth-century civil rights.

## The unit exam

Forty-six items: forty-four multiple choice, select-all, true/false and fill-in-the-blank items, many in sets on Lincoln's First Inaugural, the Kansas-Nebraska Act, the Homestead Act, the Thirteenth Amendment and the 1876 returns; about a fifth review earlier periods (the Land Ordinance, the 1787 Constitution, nullification, the market revolution, reform). Then one SAQ on a map of the enslaved population in 1860, and a seven-point DBQ on citizenship from Dred Scott to Cruikshank. For the DBQ, plan before writing: a thesis that weighs legal change against practical change, a context paragraph on the sectional crisis, four or more documents used as evidence, one outside example (the Black Codes, the Fifteenth Amendment, Revels and Bruce, the Compromise of 1877), and sourcing for at least two documents.
