# Unit 9 · Period 9: 1980–Present · The night-before sheet

**Exam weight:** 4–6% of the AP exam · **CED topics:** 9.1 to 9.7 · **Themes:** PCE, WOR, WXT, MIG, SOC, NAT
**Reasoning process:** Causation (the CED's focus for Period 9)
**Built from:** Reagan's inaugural (1981), evangelicals (1983) and Brandenburg Gate (1987) addresses; G. H. W. Bush's new world order address (1990); Clinton's State of the Union (1996) and welfare-law signing remarks (1996); G. W. Bush's address of September 20, 2001; the 9/11 Commission Report (2004); the Immigration Reform and Control Act (1986); Census Bureau and Bureau of Labor Statistics data

**Scope note.** The exam's coverage of Period 9 effectively ends in the early 2000s. Know later events (the Great Recession, the 2008 election, the Affordable Care Act) only as brief context; essays are judged on the period from 1980 to roughly 2008.

## The ideas that generate every question

1. **A conservative turn, with limits.** Stagflation and lost trust in the 1970s helped a coalition of economic, religious and anticommunist conservatives elect Reagan. They cut tax rates, deregulated and reshaped the courts, but popular programs (Social Security, Medicare) survived, defense spending rose, and the debt grew. By 1996 a Democratic president accepted much of their language.
2. **The Cold War ended, and historians weigh the causes.** U.S. pressure (buildup, SDI, the Reagan Doctrine), Soviet decline, Gorbachev's reforms and refusal to use force, and Eastern European movements all mattered. Reagan moved from confrontation (1983) to negotiation (1987).
3. **Technology and globalization remade work.** Computers, the Internet, automation and freer trade moved jobs from factories to services, lowered prices, created new fortunes and hurt older industrial regions and unions.
4. **New immigration changed the nation's face.** After the 1965 law, immigration grew and came mainly from Latin America and Asia; policy swung between legalization and enforcement.
5. **A new kind of threat.** With the Soviet Union gone, U.S. policy searched for a purpose (Gulf War, humanitarian interventions) until September 11, 2001, made terrorism the focus, bringing wars in Afghanistan and Iraq and a debate over security and liberty.
6. **Old arguments, new forms.** Period 9 debates about the size of government, immigration, trade and civil liberties echo the Gilded Age, the 1920s, the New Deal and earlier wartime crises. That is how Period 9 shows up in essays on other periods.

## Causation, comparison and continuity chains

- **Why Reagan won:** stagflation + Vietnam/Watergate distrust + hostage crisis → Southern realignment + religious right + tax revolt + Reagan Democrats → 1980 landslide.
- **Why government did not shrink:** popular entitlements + Democratic House + defense buildup → spending and debt rise despite tax cuts.
- **Why the Cold War ended:** Soviet stagnation + Afghanistan + U.S. pressure → Gorbachev's reforms → refusal to use force in 1989 → revolutions → dissolution (1991).
- **Why the economy changed:** technology + globalization + policy (tax, deregulation, trade) → services rise, manufacturing and unions fall, inequality widens.
- **Why immigration changed:** 1965 law + economic pull + crises in Central America and Asia → new sources → IRCA, Proposition 187, 1996 law.
- **Why foreign policy changed:** USSR gone → search for purpose (Gulf War, Balkans) → 9/11 → war on terror and homeland security.
- **Comparisons:** Reagan and Gilded Age laissez-faire (both trust markets; Reagan keeps the safety net); immigration 1880–1920 and 1970–2000 (both nativist debates; different sources; lower share now); civil liberties in 1798, 1917, 1942 and 2001.

## Thesis-ready arguments

- Conservatism changed the terms of political debate more than it changed the size of government: tax rates and welfare changed, but Social Security, Medicare and the debt grew.
- The Cold War ended because American pressure met a Soviet system in decline whose leader chose reform over force; neither cause alone is enough.
- Technology, more than trade policy, drove the shift to a service economy, but policy choices determined who gained and who lost.
- Period 9 immigration resembled the Gilded Age wave in the debates it provoked but differed in its origins and in a share of the population that never reached the 1910 peak.
- September 11 gave post-Cold War foreign policy a new organizing purpose and reopened the recurring American argument over security and liberty.

## The unit exam

Forty-six questions in about 75 minutes: five source sets (Reagan in Berlin, Bush's new world order, the 1996 welfare law, an interpretation comparing the period with the Gilded Age, and Bush's address after September 11), standalone multiple choice, select-all, true/false and fill-in items, one SAQ on an interpretation of conservatism's success, and an LEQ asking you to weigh the causes of economic change from 1980 to 2008. About a fifth of the items review earlier periods through comparison: be ready to connect Period 9 to the Gilded Age, the 1920s, the New Deal, World War II and the Cold War.
